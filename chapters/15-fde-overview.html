20  The Forward Deployed Engineer Role

Note

Who this chapter is for: FDE (all levels)

What you’ll be able to answer after reading this:

  • What distinguishes an FDE from a traditional software or ML engineer
  • How to scope a GenAI solution engagement end-to-end
  • How to navigate the stakeholder landscape at technical, product, and executive layers
  • What FDE interviews test that pure engineering interviews don’t
  • How to build, present, and hand off a working AI prototype in a customer engagement

20.1 What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?

The Forward Deployed Engineer role originated at Palantir, where the premise was radical: rather than selling software and letting customers figure it out, you embed a highly technical engineer at the customer site to build working software against real operational data within days. That premise has spread to Anthropic, OpenAI, Scale AI, Cohere, and a growing number of enterprise companies that are weaving AI into their platforms and need people who can make it real for individual customers. The FDE title signals a specific kind of person — one who can fly somewhere Monday morning, understand a novel domain problem by Tuesday afternoon, have a working prototype by Thursday, and present it credibly to a CFO on Friday.

What makes the FDE distinct is precisely what it is not. A sales engineer demonstrates existing products to prospective buyers; they are constrained by the product catalog. A management consultant writes analysis and recommendations but rarely builds anything; their deliverable is a deck, not a Python script. A traditional software engineer ships features on internal roadmaps on multi-month timelines; their accountability is to a product manager, not directly to a paying customer. An ML engineer optimizes models, writes research prototypes, and tunes pipelines — but is rarely in the room with the person who signed the contract.

An FDE does all of the above in compressed form: they build real software (not demos), they deliver it into a customer’s hands (not a roadmap backlog), and they are directly accountable to an external stakeholder who paid money and expects results. On any given week you might debug a customer’s Kafka pipeline in the morning, explain embedding distances to a VP of Operations after lunch, and review a security questionnaire with a CISO before end of day.

Common Misconception: FDE = Technical Sales

FDE is not the same as solutions engineering or sales engineering. A solutions engineer’s primary job is to help sell a product. An FDE’s primary job is to build something new that did not exist before they arrived. FDEs write production code, architect real systems, and are held accountable for outcomes — not demos. If you are preparing for an FDE interview and you frame yourself as someone who “supports the sales process,” you will not get the role.

The technical depth requirement is real and often underestimated by candidates approaching the role from a consulting background. Customers can tell within twenty minutes whether you actually understand the system you are proposing to build. Saying “we’ll use RAG” is not enough — you need to know what chunk size you would use, why, what embedding model, how you would handle freshness, and what the latency and cost implications are. At the same time, technical depth alone is not sufficient. An engineer who can design a perfect system but cannot explain it to the person who controls the budget, or who over-engineers the solution because the elegant version is more interesting than the simple version, is not functioning as an FDE. The role requires holding technical rigor and customer pragmatism in tension simultaneously, at all times.

20.2 FDE vs. SWE vs. ML Engineer: Role Comparison

Understanding where FDE sits relative to adjacent roles is one of the first things interviewers test. Each role has different primary accountability, different time horizons, and different success metrics.

Dimension SWE ML Engineer FDE
Primary accountability Internal product / roadmap Model performance / research Customer outcome
Time horizon Quarters to years Weeks to months Days to weeks
Builds Features in a production codebase Models, training pipelines, evals Customer-specific prototypes → production
Communication audience Engineering team, PM Research and data science team CTO, VP Ops, technical champions, executives

Failure mode Missing requirements, technical debt Overfitting, distributional shift Over-engineering, poor scoping, bad communication

When Asked to Compare These Roles in an Interview

The best answers demonstrate nuance: “I’ve done work that overlaps with all three, and the FDE version of each is different from the pure version. When I’m building, I’m making pragmatic trade-offs a SWE wouldn’t make because I’m on a four-week timeline. When I’m thinking about models, I’m asking ‘what will work well enough for this customer’ rather than ‘what is the best approach in the literature.’ The through-line is always the customer outcome.”

20.3 The FDE Skill Stack

Effective FDEs develop a layered skill stack. No one enters the role with all of it — you build it through engagements. But in an interview, you should be able to articulate where you are on each axis and give concrete evidence for each claim.

20.3.1 Layer 1: Technical Depth

You need enough depth to build real systems fast and to identify when a proposed approach will not work. For AI/GenAI FDE roles, this means:

  • LLM fundamentals: How attention works at a conceptual level. Difference between pre-training, fine-tuning, and prompting. Tokens, context windows, temperature.
  • RAG architecture: Chunking strategies (fixed-size, semantic, recursive), embedding models, vector databases, retrieval mechanisms (dense, sparse, hybrid), re-ranking. You should be able to build a working RAG pipeline from scratch in under two hours.
  • Agentic systems: Tool use, planning loops, failure modes (infinite loops, hallucinated tool calls), when to use agents vs. simpler pipelines.
  • Systems integration: REST APIs, webhooks, authentication patterns (OAuth2, API keys), database connectors, cloud services (S3, Azure Blob, GCS).
  • Evaluation: How to measure accuracy, faithfulness, groundedness, and latency. How to build a lightweight eval set in a customer engagement.

20.3.2 Layer 2: Customer Communication

This is the hardest skill to train and the one most candidates underestimate. It includes:

  • Translation: Converting “the model has low confidence on out-of-distribution queries” into “the AI is most likely to be wrong about topics not well-covered in the documents you gave it.”
  • Discovery: Asking questions that surface the real problem rather than the stated one. See Chapter 16 for the full discovery framework.
  • Expectation management: Being honest about what AI can and cannot do, and doing it in a way that maintains confidence rather than causing panic.
  • Executive communication: Translating everything into business impact in minutes, not after slides are built.

20.3.3 Layer 3: Fast Prototyping

The rhythm of FDE work is: understand the problem → build the smallest thing that proves or disproves the hypothesis → show it → adjust → repeat. This requires:

  • A personal toolkit of battle-tested patterns you can assemble quickly (RAG scaffold, agent scaffold, structured output extractor, eval harness).
  • Comfort with “good enough for now” — knowing which shortcuts compound into debt and which are genuinely throwaway.
  • The ability to present rough work in a way that communicates progress and controls narrative, rather than triggering loss of confidence.

20.3.4 Layer 4: Domain Learning on Demand

FDEs work in legal, healthcare, finance, manufacturing, retail, and every other enterprise sector. You will not be an expert in any of them before arriving. The skill is rapid domain acquisition: reading the right three documents, asking the right questions, and absorbing enough context to sound credible and ask good follow-ups within a few hours. The failure mode is pretending you know a domain you don’t — customers detect this immediately and lose trust.

20.4 A Typical FDE Day

Understanding what the job actually looks like day-to-day matters both for deciding if you want it and for answering “why FDE” questions in interviews convincingly.

Morning — Customer Discovery Call (9–10:30am)

You are on site at a mid-size financial services firm. This is day two of a five-day discovery sprint. This morning’s call is with the head of the compliance operations team and two of her analysts. You have reviewed their current process documentation and identified three candidate use cases. Your goal for today is to narrow from three to one by understanding data availability, success criteria, and stakeholder buy-in for each. You ask structured questions (see Chapter 16), listen carefully, and take detailed notes. The conversation reveals that one of the three candidates — AI-assisted regulatory filing review — has a blocker: the documents are in a system that IT has not approved for third-party API access. You mentally remove it from the list.

Mid-morning — Architecture Sketch and Data Access (10:30am–12pm)

After the call you sketch the architecture for the two remaining candidates. For the more promising one (contract clause extraction), you ask the technical champion to pull a sample of 50 contracts in whatever format they are currently stored. They come back as scanned PDFs. You run a quick OCR quality test: 80% of pages parse cleanly, 20% have formatting artifacts. You note this as a risk item and estimate it will add two days of data cleaning to the scope. You update your preliminary scope doc accordingly.

Lunch — Stakeholder Alignment (12–1pm)

Lunch with the product owner. You share what you learned this morning and your preliminary recommendation: focus on contract clause extraction because the data is accessible, the success metric is clear (extraction accuracy on a defined field set), and there is a willing technical champion who will own it after you leave. You also surface the OCR risk. The product owner is relieved to have a clear recommendation. You agree to share a one-page scope doc before end of day.

Afternoon — Prototype Build (1–4pm)

Back at your laptop. You stand up a minimal extraction pipeline: PDF → OCR cleanup → Claude with a JSON schema prompt → Pydantic validation → simple review UI in Streamlit. By 4pm you have something that correctly extracts party names, effective dates, and payment terms from 40 of the 50 sample contracts. The 10 failures are in the documents with OCR artifacts. You document which field types fail and why.

Late Afternoon — Demo and Scope Doc (4–5pm)

Thirty-minute informal demo for the technical champion. You show the working prototype, walk through the 10 failure cases, and explain what it would take to fix them. She suggests two additional fields the business would value. You take notes. After she leaves, you write the scope doc: one-page, with the problem statement, success criteria (>90% extraction accuracy on 5 specified fields across a defined test set), in-scope and out-of-scope explicitly listed, dependencies (OCR cleanup pipeline, data access approval), risks, and a rough four-week timeline. You send it before 5:30pm.

Why Same-Day Documentation Matters

Sending a scope document or meeting summary the same day it was created does several things: it signals professionalism and follow-through, it gives the customer a chance to correct misunderstandings while the conversation is still fresh, and it creates a written record that protects both parties if expectations drift later. This discipline, more than any single technical skill, separates FDEs who customers trust from ones they merely like.

20.5 Enterprise AI Challenges Every FDE Encounters

Beyond the technical architecture, every enterprise AI deployment runs into the same class of cross-cutting concerns. FDEs who understand these before they arrive are far more credible than those who encounter them for the first time on-site.

20.5.1 Security and Compliance

Enterprise customers have legal and regulatory obligations that constrain what you can build. The most common:

  • Data residency: Customer data may not leave a specific geography (EU data, HIPAA-covered records, government data). This constrains which APIs you can call and where models must run.
  • HIPAA: Healthcare data requires Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) with all processors, audit logging, and access controls. OpenAI and Anthropic offer BAA-compliant enterprise tiers; verify this before designing the architecture.
  • SOC 2: Customers will ask if your solution is SOC 2 compliant. This is about the infrastructure you deploy, not just the AI provider. Know what that means.
  • Model output auditing: Some regulated industries require that every AI-generated output be loggable and reviewable. Design logging from day one.
  • Prompt injection: In agentic systems that take user input and pass it to tools, adversarial inputs can manipulate the agent’s behavior. This is a real attack surface in production systems.

20.5.2 Latency and Scale

A prototype on your laptop handles one request at a time. Production handles hundreds of concurrent users. The gap:

  • Vector search at scale: Local Chroma works for a POC. Pinecone, Weaviate, or pgvector with connection pooling for production.
  • LLM latency: A GPT-4o call takes 2–8 seconds. For interactive use cases, this requires streaming (token-by-token output to the UI), caching (same question should not re-query the LLM), and clear UI affordances (loading states, progress indicators).
  • Throughput limits: LLM API rate limits are real and will affect batch processing jobs. Model this in your architecture estimate.

20.5.3 Integration with Existing Systems

Customers almost never want a standalone AI application — they want AI embedded in tools their teams already use. Common integration patterns:

  • Slack/Teams bots: webhook integration, conversational context management, permission scoping.
  • CRM integration (Salesforce, HubSpot): Salesforce’s API is well-documented but has rate limits and a complex data model. Budget time for integration.
  • Document management (SharePoint, Confluence, Google Drive): Each has its own API, permissions model, and file format. SharePoint’s Graph API, in particular, has enough quirks to surprise you the first time.
  • Authentication: Enterprise customers use SSO (SAML, OAuth2 with their identity provider). Your system must integrate with their IdP, not create standalone credentials.

20.5.4 Change Management

Technology is easy. Getting people to change how they work is hard. The most technically excellent AI systems fail because:

  • The people who use it every day were never involved in its design and do not trust it.
  • No one trained the team on what the system can and cannot do, so early failures destroyed adoption.
  • The workflow change required by the AI system added steps rather than removing them.

FDEs who think about adoption early — involving end users in design, building simple interfaces, running training sessions, identifying internal champions — have dramatically better engagement outcomes than those who focus solely on the technical build.

20.7 Core Responsibilities and the Engagement Arc

A typical FDE engagement moves through four distinct phases: Discovery, Scoping, Build, and Handoff. Understanding this arc is essential for doing the job and for answering interview questions about it. The failure modes at each phase are distinct.

20.7.1 Phase 1: Discovery

Discovery is structured listening and observation. You are not there to propose solutions yet — you are there to understand the problem deeply enough that you can propose the right one. This means conducting interviews at multiple levels, watching how the team actually does their work (not how they describe it), reviewing data artifacts, and building a mental model of what is painful and what it costs.

Good discovery surfaces the real problem, which is frequently not what was described in the initial brief. Customers often describe a desired solution (“we want a chatbot”) rather than the underlying need (“our support queue SLA is being missed and leadership is unhappy”). Your job in discovery is to find the underlying need.

Key discovery outputs: - A written problem statement (one paragraph, confirmed by the customer) - A list of candidate use cases with rough feasibility assessment - A data availability map: what data exists, where it lives, who owns it - Preliminary success criteria - A stakeholder map: who cares about this, who will block it, who will champion it

20.7.2 Phase 2: Scoping

Scoping is the translation from problem to feasible plan. A good scope document answers four questions: What specifically will be built? What does success look like, measured how? What are the dependencies and risks? What is explicitly not in scope?

The last item is as important as the first. Scope creep is the primary execution risk on FDE engagements, and it almost always originates in a vague or absent scope document.

Scope Conversations Are Uncomfortable — Do Them Anyway

Customers want to hear “yes, we can do everything.” Telling them a smaller, focused solution delivered well is worth more than an ambitious solution delivered partially feels like you are diminishing your own value. It is the opposite. The FDE who sets accurate expectations and delivers against them builds more trust than one who promises everything and delivers 70%.

20.7.3 Phase 3: Build

The rhythm is: build something small that demonstrates the core value → show it to the customer → get feedback → adjust → repeat. The first working demo should exist within the first week, even if rough.

This is critical because customers’ understanding of what they want evolves when they see a working system. Requirements that seemed clear in a document become ambiguous or change entirely when there is something real to react to. Daily stand-ups with the customer’s technical champion are standard.

The anti-pattern is going dark for two weeks and revealing a polished solution — by then, course corrections are expensive.

20.7.4 Phase 4: Handoff

A solution that works when you are on-site but stops working six months later has not delivered value. Good handoff includes:

  • Documentation sufficient for the customer’s team to understand, maintain, and extend the system
  • A training session walking the team through how it works and what to do when it breaks
  • Runbooks for common failure scenarios
  • A clear escalation path back to your company for issues
  • An agreed monitoring and alerting setup

The FDE’s goal is to make themselves unnecessary as quickly as possible while ensuring the customer continues extracting value. Customers who feel abandoned after an engagement end do not renew contracts.

20.8 Stakeholder Navigation

Every FDE engagement involves at least three types of stakeholders, and the failure mode of managing them as a homogeneous group is one of the most common reasons technically successful engagements feel like failures. The three archetypes are the technical champion, the product owner, and the economic buyer.

20.8.1 The Technical Champion

This is typically the engineer, data scientist, or IT lead who will live with the system after you leave. They care about how it works, whether it is maintainable, and whether you understand what you are building.

How to earn their trust: Be technically rigorous in their presence. Ask about their existing stack in detail. Acknowledge trade-offs honestly. Never oversell what the model can do.

What happens if you don’t: Technical champions who don’t trust the FDE will quietly undermine the engagement — pointing out every failure in demos, raising concerns to management, and declining to maintain the system after handoff. Build this relationship first.

20.8.2 The Product Owner

They are accountable for the project outcome and interface between the technical team and leadership. They care about timelines, feature scope, user experience, and whether the engagement is on track.

What they need from you: Clarity and predictability. Update them proactively. Frame technical decisions in terms of user experience and timeline impact. When you hit obstacles, come with a proposed solution, not just a problem.

Critical rule: A one-week delay that the product owner knew about for three days is manageable. A one-week delay revealed on the delivery date is a trust violation.

20.8.3 The Economic Buyer

The executive who approved the budget. They have typically not read the technical spec, do not know what RAG means, and are measuring the engagement against the business case that justified the investment.

What they need: Confirmation that the investment was sound and the risk is managed. In executive-facing conversations: eliminate jargon entirely, anchor every statement to business impact, and be direct about risks and mitigation.

The formula for executive communication: State the business metric → state what you observed in the pilot → translate to dollars or hours → propose a clear next step with a go/no-go criterion.

Managing Conflicting Stakeholder Requirements

The most common tension: the technical champion wants the “right” solution (latest model, custom retrieval pipeline), but the product owner needs a demo in two weeks. The solution is almost always decoupling — build a simple version first that the product owner can demo, with an architecture that can be upgraded. Make both stakeholders feel heard. Make both feel the outcome serves their interests.

20.9 The FDE Interview Format and What Interviewers Test

FDE interviews are distinctive from standard engineering interviews in their emphasis on applied judgment, communication, and customer scenario reasoning. Technical questions exist, but they are almost always embedded in a customer context.

“Design a distributed caching system” becomes “A customer’s RAG retrieval is taking 8 seconds per query and they have 200 concurrent users. What do you do?”

The four main components of an FDE interview loop:

1. Customer Scenario Round You are given a brief — two or three sentences about a company, industry, and vague AI objective — and asked to drive a discovery conversation, scope a solution, or design an architecture. Interviewers look for: Do you ask clarifying questions before jumping to solutions? Do you identify the right constraints? Do you scope to something deliverable? Can you communicate trade-offs clearly?

Common failure modes: rushing to a solution before understanding the problem (shows you listen poorly), proposing a trivial solution without acknowledging complexity (shows shallow depth), proposing a six-month project for a four-week engagement (shows you cannot scope).

2. Communication Round You explain a technical concept to an interviewer playing a non-technical executive, or roleplay a difficult stakeholder conversation. Assessed on: clarity, composure, and whether you maintain technical integrity while being accessible. Jargon is penalized.

3. Technical Round Covers LLM fundamentals, RAG architecture, agentic systems, and applied systems design. The depth expected is applied depth — “how do you build this and why do the design decisions matter” — not theoretical mastery. Know how to build things, not just how they work in academic settings.

4. Live Coding / Take-Home POC Build a working POC: a RAG system over a document set, a tool-using agent, a streaming API handler. These test execution speed, code quality, and pragmatic trade-offs. Bring your own scaffolding — a battle-tested project template. The best candidates arrive with a toolkit, not a blank editor.

20.10 FDE Cheat Sheet

  • FDE = build real solutions for real customers, not demos for sales.
  • The four engagement phases are Discovery → Scoping → Build → Handoff. Interview questions map to one of these phases.
  • Discovery is listening first, proposing second. Never arrive with a predetermined solution.
  • The real problem is rarely the stated problem. Use “five whys” to trace back from a desired solution to the underlying need.
  • A scope document has four components: what is built, how success is measured, what is not in scope, and what the risks and dependencies are.
  • The first working demo should exist within one week, even if rough. Daily feedback loops with the technical champion.
  • The three stakeholder types (technical champion, product owner, economic buyer) each require a completely different communication approach.
  • Technical champions need to see technical rigor. Product owners need clarity and predictability. Economic buyers need business-metric framing.
  • Executive communication rule: eliminate jargon, anchor to business impact, be direct about risk.
  • POC to production gap: reliability, latency/scale, security/access control, monitoring, data freshness. Never promise they are the same thing.
  • Scope creep almost always originates in a vague scope document. Write one, get it confirmed, reference it.
  • FDEs who over-engineer fail. The “right” technical solution and the “right” customer solution are frequently different.
  • Security and compliance constraints (data residency, HIPAA, SOC 2) shape architecture. Identify them in discovery, not after you’ve designed the system.
  • Change management determines adoption. Involve end users in design. Build simple interfaces. Run training sessions.
  • Handoff is half the job. A system that works when you’re on-site but fails three months later is a failed engagement.
  • Same-day documentation of meetings signals professionalism and prevents expectation drift.
  • Interview tip: when asked about your experience, narrate through the engagement arc (Discovery / Scoping / Build / Handoff).
  • Your personal toolkit (RAG scaffold, agent scaffold, eval harness) is a legitimate competitive advantage. Build and refine it continuously.
  • The FDE career path leads to: solution architecture, product management, founding roles, customer success leadership.

20.11 Interview Questions

Forward Deployed Engineer

20.11.1 Entry-Level Questions

Q1. What is the difference between a Forward Deployed Engineer and a Sales Engineer?

Model Answer

The core difference is accountability for outcomes. A sales engineer’s primary job is to support the sales process — demonstrating existing product capabilities to prospective customers to help close a deal. Their success metric is deal closure. They work with what the product already does.

An FDE’s primary job is to build new things that did not previously exist. They are deployed into a customer engagement after a deal is in motion or closed, with the mandate to produce working software specific to that customer’s needs. Their success metric is customer outcomes — does the system they built actually solve the business problem?

In practice: a sales engineer will show you how their product handles document search. An FDE will build you a custom document search system integrated into your SharePoint instance, adapted to your access control model, with an evaluation suite tuned to your actual query distribution.

The other difference is technical depth. Sales engineers need enough depth to answer product questions convincingly. FDEs need enough depth to build production systems and debug customer-specific failure modes — the kind of depth that comes from actually building things, not from studying what a product can do.

Q2. You have your first discovery call with a new enterprise customer tomorrow. How do you prepare?

Model Answer

Preparation for a discovery call has three stages: pre-read, question planning, and logistics.

Pre-read: I review whatever briefing exists — sales call notes, the customer’s website, their recent press releases, and any technical documentation they’ve shared. I want to arrive understanding their industry, their stated pain, and who will be on the call. I shouldn’t spend the first 20 minutes of a discovery call asking things I could have learned in 10 minutes of research.

Question planning: I prepare a structured question set with five to seven core questions and follow-up branches for each likely answer. My core questions always cover: the specific workflow they want to improve, the current cost of the problem (time/money/error rate), what data they have and where it lives, what success looks like measured numerically, and what they’ve tried before. I don’t plan to ask these in rigid order — I want a conversation, not an interview — but having them prepared means I don’t lose track of what I need to learn.

Logistics: I confirm who is attending and their roles. A call with a VP and two analysts is structured differently from a call with a CTO. I prepare to take detailed notes — either I’m typing live or I’m recording and transcribing afterward. I plan to send a written summary with a preliminary problem statement and next steps within two hours of the call ending.

The mindset I bring: I am here to understand, not to impress. The worst first discovery calls I’ve seen are from engineers who arrived having already decided what they’d build.

Q3. What makes a GenAI solution “production-ready” as opposed to a POC?

Model Answer

A POC proves the approach works. A production system proves the approach works reliably, at scale, for real users, under conditions you didn’t anticipate in the demo. The gap between those is real and should not be minimized.

Five categories of work that separate POC from production:

Reliability and error handling: A POC fails gracefully in demos because I control the inputs. Production users find every edge case. The LLM will return malformed JSON. The vector DB will time out under load. The OCR pipeline will encounter a document format you’ve never seen. Production code handles these gracefully and logs them for investigation.

Latency and scale: A POC that works for one user on my laptop needs load testing and potentially caching, connection pooling, and infrastructure changes for 100 concurrent users.

Security and access control: Production needs proper authentication (SSO integration), authorization (document-level ACLs in the retrieval layer), and audit logging. A POC typically has none of these.

Monitoring and observability: If something breaks at 2am, someone needs to be paged, and there needs to be enough logging to diagnose it. Structured logs, health checks, alerting.

Data freshness: The POC used a static dataset. Production needs a pipeline that keeps the index current as documents are created, updated, and deleted.

I frame this to customers as: “The POC validated the approach. What we now have is a clear map of what production engineering requires.” Then I scope the production phase with a concrete work estimate.

20.11.2 Mid-Level Questions

Q4. How do you balance building the “ideal” solution versus what can ship in four weeks?

Model Answer

My mental model: what is the minimum viable version that proves the value hypothesis? If I can prove value in four weeks with a simpler architecture, that is better than proving nothing with a more sophisticated one. The risk I am managing is not “did I build the perfect system” — it is “does the customer believe AI will help them.” A working RAG system with basic hybrid retrieval that answers 70% of questions correctly in four weeks is more valuable than a perfectly fine-tuned system that is 40% done.

I separate what I call “load-bearing” design decisions — ones that would require a full rewrite to change — from “iterative” decisions that can be improved. I will not compromise on load-bearing choices: data model, API contract, security model, authentication integration. These are expensive to change. But I will use a simpler model, a smaller test set, and manual evaluation in week one rather than spending the first two weeks building an automated eval pipeline.

I am explicit with customers about this distinction: “What we’ll ship in four weeks is a strong foundation. What we’ll improve in the next iteration is X and Y, and here’s why I’m deferring them.”

The failure mode on the other side: under-building to the point where the solution requires a full rewrite to scale. The judgment call is which shortcuts compound and which are genuinely reversible. Hardcoded prompts are reversible. A wrong choice of data model for the knowledge base often is not.

Q5. Walk me through how you’d run a discovery session with a new enterprise customer. What are the first five questions you ask?

Model Answer

Before the session: review briefing materials, understand their industry, and plan my question set. I want to arrive already understanding their context so I can listen, not explain basic concepts.

My first five questions, in order:

One: “Can you walk me through a specific workflow that you believe AI could improve — what does a team member actually do today, step by step?” This anchors us in concrete operational reality rather than abstract aspirations. I need to understand the exact steps, not a summary.

Two: “What is the current cost of this problem — in time, money, or error rate?” This establishes business stakes and is the foundation for ROI framing later. Customers who cannot answer this do not have a well-defined problem yet.

Three: “What data do you have that relates to this workflow, and who owns it?” Data availability is usually the binding constraint. This question often reveals that what a customer says they have and what they actually have are quite different.

Four: “What does a successful outcome look like six months from now — specifically, what would be different?” This surfaces success criteria in business terms. Push for something measurable: a reduction in a named metric, a time savings, an error rate threshold.

Five: “What have you already tried, and what happened?” This reveals constraints, political history around previous tech projects that failed, and failure modes I need to avoid.

After these five, I usually know whether there is a viable project and roughly what shape it takes. I write up a brief summary with a preliminary problem statement and send it the same day for confirmation.

Q6. You’re on-site and discover the customer’s data quality is much worse than they described. What do you do?

Model Answer

First, I need to understand the scope of the problem. Is this a data quality issue (inconsistent formatting, missing fields, duplicates) or a data existence issue (the data I need simply does not exist in the form I assumed)? Those are different problems. Data quality issues can often be fixed with cleaning pipelines — they are a cost and timeline impact, not necessarily a blocker. Data existence issues may require reconsidering the approach entirely.

I bring this finding to the technical champion first — not to alarm them, but to verify my understanding and explore options together. They often know about quality issues and have workarounds or undocumented data sources that can help.

Then I go to the product owner with a clear options analysis: here is what I found, here are three ways to respond — descope the solution to what the current data supports, invest two weeks in data cleaning before building, or pivot to a different data source. Here is my recommendation, and here is the revised timeline.

I never hide bad news, and I always come with options, not just problems. The customer will remember how you handled the crisis more than the crisis itself. An FDE who says “I found a data problem and here are three ways to handle it, my recommendation is X” builds more trust than an FDE who either hides the problem or delivers it without a path forward.

20.11.3 Senior-Level Questions

Q7. A customer wants to “add AI to everything.” How do you guide this conversation productively?

Model Answer

“Add AI to everything” is a goals statement without a problem statement. My job is to help the customer translate aspiration into a concrete, prioritized plan — and to do that without dismissing the aspiration, because it often represents a genuine strategic commitment from leadership that has momentum behind it.

I start by getting specific: “That’s a strong direction — can you walk me through which workflows today feel most manual, error-prone, or slow? I want to make sure we focus the initial investment where it creates the clearest business impact.” This moves us from “AI everywhere” to a concrete list of candidate problems.

I then apply a quick prioritization framework against each candidate: (1) How measurable is the outcome — is there a clear metric that would move? (2) Is the data available and accessible? (3) Is there a willing technical champion who will own it after we’re done? Problems that score well on all three become phase one. Problems that score poorly on one become phase two or get deprioritized.

I’m also honest that AI is not the right answer for every problem on the list. If a customer has a workflow that is slow because of an organizational approval bottleneck, AI won’t fix that. Better to say “for this one, the root cause is the approval process, and the solution is probably workflow automation or process change, not AI specifically” — and focus our effort on the problems where AI has a genuine leverage advantage.

The goal by the end of this conversation is a short-list of two to three concrete, scoped candidates, with a shared understanding of why these are the right ones to start with.

What great looks like: The candidate doesn’t just narrow the list — they also reframe the customer’s mental model from “AI project” to “business problem with AI as the enabler.” The best FDEs leave discovery calls with the customer feeling like they’ve been genuinely helped to think more clearly, not just been handed a scope document.

Common Mistake: Agreeing to “add AI to everything” without pushing back, then trying to scope everything in parallel. This leads to shallow work on multiple fronts, diluted impact, and a customer who eventually feels like they got consulting outputs rather than working software.

Q8. The technical champion loves your solution but the executive sponsor is skeptical about ROI. How do you handle that meeting?

Model Answer

I prepare for that meeting by translating everything into the executive’s language before I walk in. That means identifying the specific business metric the engagement was supposed to move, quantifying what the solution delivers against that metric, and anchoring it to money or time that has real meaning at their level.

Not: “The system achieves 84% retrieval accuracy.” Instead: “Based on your team’s current rate of two hours per analyst per day spent searching documents, and our observed reduction to 25 minutes in the pilot, this translates to 1.75 hours recovered per analyst per day across your 40-person team. At fully-loaded cost, that is approximately $X per year.”

In the meeting itself, I acknowledge the skepticism directly rather than avoiding it: “I understand ROI on AI projects can be hard to evaluate — let me show you specifically how we measured impact in the pilot.” I present the data, acknowledge what is and is not proven yet, and propose a clear next step with a defined go/no-go criterion.

I do not get defensive about the technology. Executives have seen many technology investments that delivered technically while failing to move the business metric they were supposed to move. If I can explain clearly why our solution moves the specific metric they care about, I differentiate myself from the pattern they have learned to be skeptical of.

What great looks like: The candidate walks in having already calculated the dollar or hour impact, with the methodology visible and auditable. They acknowledge the limits of the pilot data honestly (“this is from 30 days and 40 users — here’s our confidence interval”) and frame uncertainty as “here’s the next measurement we’d take” rather than “the results speak for themselves.”

Common Mistake: Leading with technical accomplishments in an executive meeting. The executive does not care that the retrieval recall improved from 71% to 88%. They care whether the project will generate more value than it cost. These are different conversations.

Q9. You’ve shipped a POC that works. The customer now wants to “scale it to production.” What do you tell them about what that actually requires?

Model Answer

I tell them that a POC proves the approach works, and a production system proves the approach works reliably, at scale, for real users, under conditions we didn’t anticipate in the demo. The gap between those two things is real and should not be minimized — but it is also mappable and estimable.

I walk through five categories of work the POC does not address:

Reliability and error handling: The POC fails gracefully in my demos because I control the inputs. Production users will find every edge case. Every external call needs retry logic, timeouts, and graceful degradation.

Latency and scale: A POC that works for one user needs load testing and potentially caching, connection pooling, and infrastructure changes for hundreds of concurrent users. I’d want to run a load test before committing to a production timeline.

Security and access control: Production needs proper authentication (SSO integration with their IdP), authorization (document-level ACLs in the retrieval layer), and audit logging. None of these exist in the POC.

Monitoring and observability: If something breaks at 2am, there needs to be alerting, structured logging, and enough instrumentation to diagnose the failure. This is a non-trivial build.

Data freshness: The POC used a static dataset. Production needs a pipeline that keeps the index current as documents are created, updated, and deleted — with failure handling and reprocessing logic.

I frame all of this not as “the POC is not good enough” but as “the POC validated the approach, and now we have a clear map of what production engineering requires.” Then I produce a concrete production-phase scope with an honest timeline estimate.

What great looks like: The candidate enumerates specific gaps (not vague “there’s more work”), sizes each one roughly in person-weeks, and offers a clear handoff plan that includes what the customer’s own engineering team needs to own versus what remains an FDE engagement.

Common Mistake: Underestimating the production gap to avoid an awkward conversation. Customers who hear “we’re close to production” and then discover a three-month production engineering effort lose trust in ways that are very hard to recover from.

## What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?

The Forward Deployed Engineer role originated at Palantir, where the premise was radical: rather than selling software and letting customers figure it out, you embed a highly technical engineer at the customer site to build working software against real operational data within days. That premise has spread to Anthropic, OpenAI, Scale AI, Cohere, and a growing number of enterprise companies that are weaving AI into their platforms and need people who can make it real for individual customers. The FDE title signals a specific kind of person — one who can fly somewhere Monday morning, understand a novel domain problem by Tuesday afternoon, have a working prototype by Thursday, and present it credibly to a CFO on Friday.

What makes the FDE distinct is precisely what it is *not*. A sales engineer demonstrates existing products to prospective buyers; they are constrained by the product catalog. A management consultant writes analysis and recommendations but rarely builds anything; their deliverable is a deck, not a Python script. A traditional software engineer ships features on internal roadmaps on multi-month timelines; their accountability is to a product manager, not directly to a paying customer. An ML engineer optimizes models, writes research prototypes, and tunes pipelines — but is rarely in the room with the person who signed the contract.

An FDE does all of the above in compressed form: they build real software (not demos), they deliver it into a customer's hands (not a roadmap backlog), and they are directly accountable to an external stakeholder who paid money and expects results. On any given week you might debug a customer's Kafka pipeline in the morning, explain embedding distances to a VP of Operations after lunch, and review a security questionnaire with a CISO before end of day.

::: {.callout-warning title="Common Misconception: FDE = Technical Sales"}
FDE is not the same as solutions engineering or sales engineering. A solutions engineer's primary job is to help sell a product. An FDE's primary job is to build something new that did not exist before they arrived. FDEs write production code, architect real systems, and are held accountable for outcomes — not demos. If you are preparing for an FDE interview and you frame yourself as someone who "supports the sales process," you will not get the role.
:::

The technical depth requirement is real and often underestimated by candidates approaching the role from a consulting background. Customers can tell within twenty minutes whether you actually understand the system you are proposing to build. Saying "we'll use RAG" is not enough — you need to know what chunk size you would use, why, what embedding model, how you would handle freshness, and what the latency and cost implications are. At the same time, technical depth alone is not sufficient. An engineer who can design a perfect system but cannot explain it to the person who controls the budget, or who over-engineers the solution because the elegant version is more interesting than the simple version, is not functioning as an FDE. The role requires holding technical rigor and customer pragmatism in tension simultaneously, at all times.

## The FDE Skill Stack

Effective FDEs develop a layered skill stack. No one enters the role with all of it — you build it through engagements. But in an interview, you should be able to articulate where you are on each axis and give concrete evidence for each claim.

### Layer 1: Technical Depth

You need enough depth to build real systems fast and to identify when a proposed approach will not work. For AI/GenAI FDE roles, this means:

- **LLM fundamentals:** How attention works at a conceptual level. Difference between pre-training, fine-tuning, and prompting. Tokens, context windows, temperature.
- **RAG architecture:** Chunking strategies (fixed-size, semantic, recursive), embedding models, vector databases, retrieval mechanisms (dense, sparse, hybrid), re-ranking. You should be able to build a working RAG pipeline from scratch in under two hours.
- **Agentic systems:** Tool use, planning loops, failure modes (infinite loops, hallucinated tool calls), when to use agents vs. simpler pipelines.
- **Systems integration:** REST APIs, webhooks, authentication patterns (OAuth2, API keys), database connectors, cloud services (S3, Azure Blob, GCS).
- **Evaluation:** How to measure accuracy, faithfulness, groundedness, and latency. How to build a lightweight eval set in a customer engagement.

### Layer 2: Customer Communication

This is the hardest skill to train and the one most candidates underestimate. It includes:

- **Translation:** Converting "the model has low confidence on out-of-distribution queries" into "the AI is most likely to be wrong about topics not well-covered in the documents you gave it."
- **Discovery:** Asking questions that surface the real problem rather than the stated one. See Chapter 16 for the full discovery framework.
- **Expectation management:** Being honest about what AI can and cannot do, and doing it in a way that maintains confidence rather than causing panic.
- **Executive communication:** Translating everything into business impact in minutes, not after slides are built.

### Layer 3: Fast Prototyping

The rhythm of FDE work is: understand the problem → build the smallest thing that proves or disproves the hypothesis → show it → adjust → repeat. This requires:

- A personal toolkit of battle-tested patterns you can assemble quickly (RAG scaffold, agent scaffold, structured output extractor, eval harness).
- Comfort with "good enough for now" — knowing which shortcuts compound into debt and which are genuinely throwaway.
- The ability to present rough work in a way that communicates progress and controls narrative, rather than triggering loss of confidence.

### Layer 4: Domain Learning on Demand

FDEs work in legal, healthcare, finance, manufacturing, retail, and every other enterprise sector. You will not be an expert in any of them before arriving. The skill is rapid domain acquisition: reading the right three documents, asking the right questions, and absorbing enough context to sound credible and ask good follow-ups within a few hours. The failure mode is pretending you know a domain you don't — customers detect this immediately and lose trust.

## A Typical FDE Day

Understanding what the job actually looks like day-to-day matters both for deciding if you want it and for answering "why FDE" questions in interviews convincingly.

**Morning — Customer Discovery Call (9–10:30am)**

You are on site at a mid-size financial services firm. This is day two of a five-day discovery sprint. This morning's call is with the head of the compliance operations team and two of her analysts. You have reviewed their current process documentation and identified three candidate use cases. Your goal for today is to narrow from three to one by understanding data availability, success criteria, and stakeholder buy-in for each. You ask structured questions (see Chapter 16), listen carefully, and take detailed notes. The conversation reveals that one of the three candidates — AI-assisted regulatory filing review — has a blocker: the documents are in a system that IT has not approved for third-party API access. You mentally remove it from the list.

**Mid-morning — Architecture Sketch and Data Access (10:30am–12pm)**

After the call you sketch the architecture for the two remaining candidates. For the more promising one (contract clause extraction), you ask the technical champion to pull a sample of 50 contracts in whatever format they are currently stored. They come back as scanned PDFs. You run a quick OCR quality test: 80% of pages parse cleanly, 20% have formatting artifacts. You note this as a risk item and estimate it will add two days of data cleaning to the scope. You update your preliminary scope doc accordingly.

**Lunch — Stakeholder Alignment (12–1pm)**

Lunch with the product owner. You share what you learned this morning and your preliminary recommendation: focus on contract clause extraction because the data is accessible, the success metric is clear (extraction accuracy on a defined field set), and there is a willing technical champion who will own it after you leave. You also surface the OCR risk. The product owner is relieved to have a clear recommendation. You agree to share a one-page scope doc before end of day.

**Afternoon — Prototype Build (1–4pm)**

Back at your laptop. You stand up a minimal extraction pipeline: PDF → OCR cleanup → Claude with a JSON schema prompt → Pydantic validation → simple review UI in Streamlit. By 4pm you have something that correctly extracts party names, effective dates, and payment terms from 40 of the 50 sample contracts. The 10 failures are in the documents with OCR artifacts. You document which field types fail and why.

**Late Afternoon — Demo and Scope Doc (4–5pm)**

Thirty-minute informal demo for the technical champion. You show the working prototype, walk through the 10 failure cases, and explain what it would take to fix them. She suggests two additional fields the business would value. You take notes. After she leaves, you write the scope doc: one-page, with the problem statement, success criteria (>90% extraction accuracy on 5 specified fields across a defined test set), in-scope and out-of-scope explicitly listed, dependencies (OCR cleanup pipeline, data access approval), risks, and a rough four-week timeline. You send it before 5:30pm.

::: {.callout-tip title="Why Same-Day Documentation Matters"}
Sending a scope document or meeting summary the same day it was created does several things: it signals professionalism and follow-through, it gives the customer a chance to correct misunderstandings while the conversation is still fresh, and it creates a written record that protects both parties if expectations drift later. This discipline, more than any single technical skill, separates FDEs who customers trust from ones they merely like.
:::

## Enterprise AI Challenges Every FDE Encounters

Beyond the technical architecture, every enterprise AI deployment runs into the same class of cross-cutting concerns. FDEs who understand these before they arrive are far more credible than those who encounter them for the first time on-site.

### Security and Compliance

Enterprise customers have legal and regulatory obligations that constrain what you can build. The most common:

- **Data residency:** Customer data may not leave a specific geography (EU data, HIPAA-covered records, government data). This constrains which APIs you can call and where models must run.
- **HIPAA:** Healthcare data requires Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) with all processors, audit logging, and access controls. OpenAI and Anthropic offer BAA-compliant enterprise tiers; verify this before designing the architecture.
- **SOC 2:** Customers will ask if your solution is SOC 2 compliant. This is about the infrastructure you deploy, not just the AI provider. Know what that means.
- **Model output auditing:** Some regulated industries require that every AI-generated output be loggable and reviewable. Design logging from day one.
- **Prompt injection:** In agentic systems that take user input and pass it to tools, adversarial inputs can manipulate the agent's behavior. This is a real attack surface in production systems.

### Latency and Scale

A prototype on your laptop handles one request at a time. Production handles hundreds of concurrent users. The gap:

- **Vector search at scale:** Local Chroma works for a POC. Pinecone, Weaviate, or pgvector with connection pooling for production.
- **LLM latency:** A GPT-4o call takes 2–8 seconds. For interactive use cases, this requires streaming (token-by-token output to the UI), caching (same question should not re-query the LLM), and clear UI affordances (loading states, progress indicators).
- **Throughput limits:** LLM API rate limits are real and will affect batch processing jobs. Model this in your architecture estimate.

### Integration with Existing Systems

Customers almost never want a standalone AI application — they want AI embedded in tools their teams already use. Common integration patterns:

- **Slack/Teams bots:** webhook integration, conversational context management, permission scoping.
- **CRM integration (Salesforce, HubSpot):** Salesforce's API is well-documented but has rate limits and a complex data model. Budget time for integration.
- **Document management (SharePoint, Confluence, Google Drive):** Each has its own API, permissions model, and file format. SharePoint's Graph API, in particular, has enough quirks to surprise you the first time.
- **Authentication:** Enterprise customers use SSO (SAML, OAuth2 with their identity provider). Your system must integrate with their IdP, not create standalone credentials.

### Change Management

Technology is easy. Getting people to change how they work is hard. The most technically excellent AI systems fail because:

- The people who use it every day were never involved in its design and do not trust it.
- No one trained the team on what the system can and cannot do, so early failures destroyed adoption.
- The workflow change required by the AI system added steps rather than removing them.

FDEs who think about adoption early — involving end users in design, building simple interfaces, running training sessions, identifying internal champions — have dramatically better engagement outcomes than those who focus solely on the technical build.

## Core Responsibilities and the Engagement Arc

A typical FDE engagement moves through four distinct phases: Discovery, Scoping, Build, and Handoff. Understanding this arc is essential for doing the job and for answering interview questions about it. The failure modes at each phase are distinct.

### Phase 1: Discovery

Discovery is structured listening and observation. You are not there to propose solutions yet — you are there to understand the problem deeply enough that you can propose the right one. This means conducting interviews at multiple levels, watching how the team actually does their work (not how they describe it), reviewing data artifacts, and building a mental model of what is painful and what it costs.

Good discovery surfaces the real problem, which is frequently not what was described in the initial brief. Customers often describe a desired solution ("we want a chatbot") rather than the underlying need ("our support queue SLA is being missed and leadership is unhappy"). Your job in discovery is to find the underlying need.

**Key discovery outputs:**
- A written problem statement (one paragraph, confirmed by the customer)
- A list of candidate use cases with rough feasibility assessment
- A data availability map: what data exists, where it lives, who owns it
- Preliminary success criteria
- A stakeholder map: who cares about this, who will block it, who will champion it

### Phase 2: Scoping

Scoping is the translation from problem to feasible plan. A good scope document answers four questions: What specifically will be built? What does success look like, measured how? What are the dependencies and risks? What is explicitly *not* in scope?

The last item is as important as the first. Scope creep is the primary execution risk on FDE engagements, and it almost always originates in a vague or absent scope document.

::: {.callout-warning title="Scope Conversations Are Uncomfortable — Do Them Anyway"}
Customers want to hear "yes, we can do everything." Telling them a smaller, focused solution delivered well is worth more than an ambitious solution delivered partially feels like you are diminishing your own value. It is the opposite. The FDE who sets accurate expectations and delivers against them builds more trust than one who promises everything and delivers 70%.
:::

### Phase 3: Build

The rhythm is: build something small that demonstrates the core value → show it to the customer → get feedback → adjust → repeat. The first working demo should exist within the first week, even if rough.

This is critical because customers' understanding of what they want evolves when they see a working system. Requirements that seemed clear in a document become ambiguous or change entirely when there is something real to react to. Daily stand-ups with the customer's technical champion are standard.

The anti-pattern is going dark for two weeks and revealing a polished solution — by then, course corrections are expensive.

### Phase 4: Handoff

A solution that works when you are on-site but stops working six months later has not delivered value. Good handoff includes:

- Documentation sufficient for the customer's team to understand, maintain, and extend the system
- A training session walking the team through how it works and what to do when it breaks
- Runbooks for common failure scenarios
- A clear escalation path back to your company for issues
- An agreed monitoring and alerting setup

The FDE's goal is to make themselves unnecessary as quickly as possible while ensuring the customer continues extracting value. Customers who feel abandoned after an engagement end do not renew contracts.

## Stakeholder Navigation

Every FDE engagement involves at least three types of stakeholders, and the failure mode of managing them as a homogeneous group is one of the most common reasons technically successful engagements feel like failures. The three archetypes are the technical champion, the product owner, and the economic buyer.

### The Technical Champion

This is typically the engineer, data scientist, or IT lead who will live with the system after you leave. They care about how it works, whether it is maintainable, and whether you understand what you are building.

**How to earn their trust:** Be technically rigorous in their presence. Ask about their existing stack in detail. Acknowledge trade-offs honestly. Never oversell what the model can do.

**What happens if you don't:** Technical champions who don't trust the FDE will quietly undermine the engagement — pointing out every failure in demos, raising concerns to management, and declining to maintain the system after handoff. Build this relationship first.

### The Product Owner

They are accountable for the project outcome and interface between the technical team and leadership. They care about timelines, feature scope, user experience, and whether the engagement is on track.

**What they need from you:** Clarity and predictability. Update them proactively. Frame technical decisions in terms of user experience and timeline impact. When you hit obstacles, come with a proposed solution, not just a problem.

**Critical rule:** A one-week delay that the product owner knew about for three days is manageable. A one-week delay revealed on the delivery date is a trust violation.

### The Economic Buyer

The executive who approved the budget. They have typically not read the technical spec, do not know what RAG means, and are measuring the engagement against the business case that justified the investment.

**What they need:** Confirmation that the investment was sound and the risk is managed. In executive-facing conversations: eliminate jargon entirely, anchor every statement to business impact, and be direct about risks and mitigation.

**The formula for executive communication:** State the business metric → state what you observed in the pilot → translate to dollars or hours → propose a clear next step with a go/no-go criterion.

::: {.callout-tip title="Managing Conflicting Stakeholder Requirements"}
The most common tension: the technical champion wants the "right" solution (latest model, custom retrieval pipeline), but the product owner needs a demo in two weeks. The solution is almost always decoupling — build a simple version first that the product owner can demo, with an architecture that can be upgraded. Make both stakeholders feel heard. Make both feel the outcome serves their interests.
:::

## The FDE Interview Format and What Interviewers Test

FDE interviews are distinctive from standard engineering interviews in their emphasis on applied judgment, communication, and customer scenario reasoning. Technical questions exist, but they are almost always embedded in a customer context.

"Design a distributed caching system" becomes "A customer's RAG retrieval is taking 8 seconds per query and they have 200 concurrent users. What do you do?"

The four main components of an FDE interview loop:

**1. Customer Scenario Round**
You are given a brief — two or three sentences about a company, industry, and vague AI objective — and asked to drive a discovery conversation, scope a solution, or design an architecture. Interviewers look for: Do you ask clarifying questions before jumping to solutions? Do you identify the right constraints? Do you scope to something deliverable? Can you communicate trade-offs clearly?

Common failure modes: rushing to a solution before understanding the problem (shows you listen poorly), proposing a trivial solution without acknowledging complexity (shows shallow depth), proposing a six-month project for a four-week engagement (shows you cannot scope).

**2. Communication Round**
You explain a technical concept to an interviewer playing a non-technical executive, or roleplay a difficult stakeholder conversation. Assessed on: clarity, composure, and whether you maintain technical integrity while being accessible. Jargon is penalized.

**3. Technical Round**
Covers LLM fundamentals, RAG architecture, agentic systems, and applied systems design. The depth expected is applied depth — "how do you build this and why do the design decisions matter" — not theoretical mastery. Know how to build things, not just how they work in academic settings.

**4. Live Coding / Take-Home POC**
Build a working POC: a RAG system over a document set, a tool-using agent, a streaming API handler. These test execution speed, code quality, and pragmatic trade-offs. Bring your own scaffolding — a battle-tested project template. The best candidates arrive with a toolkit, not a blank editor.

## FDE Cheat Sheet

- FDE = build real solutions for real customers, not demos for sales.
- The four engagement phases are Discovery → Scoping → Build → Handoff. Interview questions map to one of these phases.
- Discovery is listening first, proposing second. Never arrive with a predetermined solution.
- The real problem is rarely the stated problem. Use "five whys" to trace back from a desired solution to the underlying need.
- A scope document has four components: what is built, how success is measured, what is not in scope, and what the risks and dependencies are.
- The first working demo should exist within one week, even if rough. Daily feedback loops with the technical champion.
- The three stakeholder types (technical champion, product owner, economic buyer) each require a completely different communication approach.
- Technical champions need to see technical rigor. Product owners need clarity and predictability. Economic buyers need business-metric framing.
- Executive communication rule: eliminate jargon, anchor to business impact, be direct about risk.
- POC to production gap: reliability, latency/scale, security/access control, monitoring, data freshness. Never promise they are the same thing.
- Scope creep almost always originates in a vague scope document. Write one, get it confirmed, reference it.
- FDEs who over-engineer fail. The "right" technical solution and the "right" customer solution are frequently different.
- Security and compliance constraints (data residency, HIPAA, SOC 2) shape architecture. Identify them in discovery, not after you've designed the system.
- Change management determines adoption. Involve end users in design. Build simple interfaces. Run training sessions.
- Handoff is half the job. A system that works when you're on-site but fails three months later is a failed engagement.
- Same-day documentation of meetings signals professionalism and prevents expectation drift.
- Interview tip: when asked about your experience, narrate through the engagement arc (Discovery / Scoping / Build / Handoff).
- Your personal toolkit (RAG scaffold, agent scaffold, eval harness) is a legitimate competitive advantage. Build and refine it continuously.
- The FDE career path leads to: solution architecture, product management, founding roles, customer success leadership.

## Interview Questions

::: {.callout-important title="Forward Deployed Engineer"}

### Entry-Level Questions

**Q1. What is the difference between a Forward Deployed Engineer and a Sales Engineer?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
The core difference is accountability for outcomes. A sales engineer's primary job is to support the sales process — demonstrating existing product capabilities to prospective customers to help close a deal. Their success metric is deal closure. They work with what the product already does.

An FDE's primary job is to build new things that did not previously exist. They are deployed into a customer engagement after a deal is in motion or closed, with the mandate to produce working software specific to that customer's needs. Their success metric is customer outcomes — does the system they built actually solve the business problem?

In practice: a sales engineer will show you how their product handles document search. An FDE will build you a custom document search system integrated into your SharePoint instance, adapted to your access control model, with an evaluation suite tuned to your actual query distribution.

The other difference is technical depth. Sales engineers need enough depth to answer product questions convincingly. FDEs need enough depth to build production systems and debug customer-specific failure modes — the kind of depth that comes from actually building things, not from studying what a product can do.
:::

**Q2. You have your first discovery call with a new enterprise customer tomorrow. How do you prepare?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Preparation for a discovery call has three stages: pre-read, question planning, and logistics.

**Pre-read:** I review whatever briefing exists — sales call notes, the customer's website, their recent press releases, and any technical documentation they've shared. I want to arrive understanding their industry, their stated pain, and who will be on the call. I shouldn't spend the first 20 minutes of a discovery call asking things I could have learned in 10 minutes of research.

**Question planning:** I prepare a structured question set with five to seven core questions and follow-up branches for each likely answer. My core questions always cover: the specific workflow they want to improve, the current cost of the problem (time/money/error rate), what data they have and where it lives, what success looks like measured numerically, and what they've tried before. I don't plan to ask these in rigid order — I want a conversation, not an interview — but having them prepared means I don't lose track of what I need to learn.

**Logistics:** I confirm who is attending and their roles. A call with a VP and two analysts is structured differently from a call with a CTO. I prepare to take detailed notes — either I'm typing live or I'm recording and transcribing afterward. I plan to send a written summary with a preliminary problem statement and next steps within two hours of the call ending.

The mindset I bring: I am here to understand, not to impress. The worst first discovery calls I've seen are from engineers who arrived having already decided what they'd build.
:::

**Q3. What makes a GenAI solution "production-ready" as opposed to a POC?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
A POC proves the approach works. A production system proves the approach works reliably, at scale, for real users, under conditions you didn't anticipate in the demo. The gap between those is real and should not be minimized.

Five categories of work that separate POC from production:

**Reliability and error handling:** A POC fails gracefully in demos because I control the inputs. Production users find every edge case. The LLM will return malformed JSON. The vector DB will time out under load. The OCR pipeline will encounter a document format you've never seen. Production code handles these gracefully and logs them for investigation.

**Latency and scale:** A POC that works for one user on my laptop needs load testing and potentially caching, connection pooling, and infrastructure changes for 100 concurrent users.

**Security and access control:** Production needs proper authentication (SSO integration), authorization (document-level ACLs in the retrieval layer), and audit logging. A POC typically has none of these.

**Monitoring and observability:** If something breaks at 2am, someone needs to be paged, and there needs to be enough logging to diagnose it. Structured logs, health checks, alerting.

**Data freshness:** The POC used a static dataset. Production needs a pipeline that keeps the index current as documents are created, updated, and deleted.

I frame this to customers as: "The POC validated the approach. What we now have is a clear map of what production engineering requires." Then I scope the production phase with a concrete work estimate.
:::

### Mid-Level Questions

**Q4. How do you balance building the "ideal" solution versus what can ship in four weeks?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
My mental model: what is the minimum viable version that proves the value hypothesis? If I can prove value in four weeks with a simpler architecture, that is better than proving nothing with a more sophisticated one. The risk I am managing is not "did I build the perfect system" — it is "does the customer believe AI will help them." A working RAG system with basic hybrid retrieval that answers 70% of questions correctly in four weeks is more valuable than a perfectly fine-tuned system that is 40% done.

I separate what I call "load-bearing" design decisions — ones that would require a full rewrite to change — from "iterative" decisions that can be improved. I will not compromise on load-bearing choices: data model, API contract, security model, authentication integration. These are expensive to change. But I will use a simpler model, a smaller test set, and manual evaluation in week one rather than spending the first two weeks building an automated eval pipeline.

I am explicit with customers about this distinction: "What we'll ship in four weeks is a strong foundation. What we'll improve in the next iteration is X and Y, and here's why I'm deferring them."

The failure mode on the other side: under-building to the point where the solution requires a full rewrite to scale. The judgment call is which shortcuts compound and which are genuinely reversible. Hardcoded prompts are reversible. A wrong choice of data model for the knowledge base often is not.
:::

**Q5. Walk me through how you'd run a discovery session with a new enterprise customer. What are the first five questions you ask?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Before the session: review briefing materials, understand their industry, and plan my question set. I want to arrive already understanding their context so I can listen, not explain basic concepts.

My first five questions, in order:

**One:** "Can you walk me through a specific workflow that you believe AI could improve — what does a team member actually do today, step by step?" This anchors us in concrete operational reality rather than abstract aspirations. I need to understand the exact steps, not a summary.

**Two:** "What is the current cost of this problem — in time, money, or error rate?" This establishes business stakes and is the foundation for ROI framing later. Customers who cannot answer this do not have a well-defined problem yet.

**Three:** "What data do you have that relates to this workflow, and who owns it?" Data availability is usually the binding constraint. This question often reveals that what a customer says they have and what they actually have are quite different.

**Four:** "What does a successful outcome look like six months from now — specifically, what would be different?" This surfaces success criteria in business terms. Push for something measurable: a reduction in a named metric, a time savings, an error rate threshold.

**Five:** "What have you already tried, and what happened?" This reveals constraints, political history around previous tech projects that failed, and failure modes I need to avoid.

After these five, I usually know whether there is a viable project and roughly what shape it takes. I write up a brief summary with a preliminary problem statement and send it the same day for confirmation.
:::

**Q6. You're on-site and discover the customer's data quality is much worse than they described. What do you do?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
First, I need to understand the scope of the problem. Is this a data quality issue (inconsistent formatting, missing fields, duplicates) or a data existence issue (the data I need simply does not exist in the form I assumed)? Those are different problems. Data quality issues can often be fixed with cleaning pipelines — they are a cost and timeline impact, not necessarily a blocker. Data existence issues may require reconsidering the approach entirely.

I bring this finding to the technical champion first — not to alarm them, but to verify my understanding and explore options together. They often know about quality issues and have workarounds or undocumented data sources that can help.

Then I go to the product owner with a clear options analysis: here is what I found, here are three ways to respond — descope the solution to what the current data supports, invest two weeks in data cleaning before building, or pivot to a different data source. Here is my recommendation, and here is the revised timeline.

I never hide bad news, and I always come with options, not just problems. The customer will remember how you handled the crisis more than the crisis itself. An FDE who says "I found a data problem and here are three ways to handle it, my recommendation is X" builds more trust than an FDE who either hides the problem or delivers it without a path forward.
:::

### Senior-Level Questions

**Q7. A customer wants to "add AI to everything." How do you guide this conversation productively?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
"Add AI to everything" is a goals statement without a problem statement. My job is to help the customer translate aspiration into a concrete, prioritized plan — and to do that without dismissing the aspiration, because it often represents a genuine strategic commitment from leadership that has momentum behind it.

I start by getting specific: "That's a strong direction — can you walk me through which workflows today feel most manual, error-prone, or slow? I want to make sure we focus the initial investment where it creates the clearest business impact." This moves us from "AI everywhere" to a concrete list of candidate problems.

I then apply a quick prioritization framework against each candidate: (1) How measurable is the outcome — is there a clear metric that would move? (2) Is the data available and accessible? (3) Is there a willing technical champion who will own it after we're done? Problems that score well on all three become phase one. Problems that score poorly on one become phase two or get deprioritized.

I'm also honest that AI is not the right answer for every problem on the list. If a customer has a workflow that is slow because of an organizational approval bottleneck, AI won't fix that. Better to say "for this one, the root cause is the approval process, and the solution is probably workflow automation or process change, not AI specifically" — and focus our effort on the problems where AI has a genuine leverage advantage.

The goal by the end of this conversation is a short-list of two to three concrete, scoped candidates, with a shared understanding of why these are the right ones to start with.

> **What great looks like:** The candidate doesn't just narrow the list — they also reframe the customer's mental model from "AI project" to "business problem with AI as the enabler." The best FDEs leave discovery calls with the customer feeling like they've been genuinely helped to think more clearly, not just been handed a scope document.

> **Common Mistake:** Agreeing to "add AI to everything" without pushing back, then trying to scope everything in parallel. This leads to shallow work on multiple fronts, diluted impact, and a customer who eventually feels like they got consulting outputs rather than working software.
:::

**Q8. The technical champion loves your solution but the executive sponsor is skeptical about ROI. How do you handle that meeting?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
I prepare for that meeting by translating everything into the executive's language before I walk in. That means identifying the specific business metric the engagement was supposed to move, quantifying what the solution delivers against that metric, and anchoring it to money or time that has real meaning at their level.

Not: "The system achieves 84% retrieval accuracy." Instead: "Based on your team's current rate of two hours per analyst per day spent searching documents, and our observed reduction to 25 minutes in the pilot, this translates to 1.75 hours recovered per analyst per day across your 40-person team. At fully-loaded cost, that is approximately $X per year."

In the meeting itself, I acknowledge the skepticism directly rather than avoiding it: "I understand ROI on AI projects can be hard to evaluate — let me show you specifically how we measured impact in the pilot." I present the data, acknowledge what is and is not proven yet, and propose a clear next step with a defined go/no-go criterion.

I do not get defensive about the technology. Executives have seen many technology investments that delivered technically while failing to move the business metric they were supposed to move. If I can explain clearly why our solution moves the specific metric they care about, I differentiate myself from the pattern they have learned to be skeptical of.

> **What great looks like:** The candidate walks in having already calculated the dollar or hour impact, with the methodology visible and auditable. They acknowledge the limits of the pilot data honestly ("this is from 30 days and 40 users — here's our confidence interval") and frame uncertainty as "here's the next measurement we'd take" rather than "the results speak for themselves."

> **Common Mistake:** Leading with technical accomplishments in an executive meeting. The executive does not care that the retrieval recall improved from 71% to 88%. They care whether the project will generate more value than it cost. These are different conversations.
:::

**Q9. You've shipped a POC that works. The customer now wants to "scale it to production." What do you tell them about what that actually requires?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
I tell them that a POC proves the approach works, and a production system proves the approach works reliably, at scale, for real users, under conditions we didn't anticipate in the demo. The gap between those two things is real and should not be minimized — but it is also mappable and estimable.

I walk through five categories of work the POC does not address:

**Reliability and error handling:** The POC fails gracefully in my demos because I control the inputs. Production users will find every edge case. Every external call needs retry logic, timeouts, and graceful degradation.

**Latency and scale:** A POC that works for one user needs load testing and potentially caching, connection pooling, and infrastructure changes for hundreds of concurrent users. I'd want to run a load test before committing to a production timeline.

**Security and access control:** Production needs proper authentication (SSO integration with their IdP), authorization (document-level ACLs in the retrieval layer), and audit logging. None of these exist in the POC.

**Monitoring and observability:** If something breaks at 2am, there needs to be alerting, structured logging, and enough instrumentation to diagnose the failure. This is a non-trivial build.

**Data freshness:** The POC used a static dataset. Production needs a pipeline that keeps the index current as documents are created, updated, and deleted — with failure handling and reprocessing logic.

I frame all of this not as "the POC is not good enough" but as "the POC validated the approach, and now we have a clear map of what production engineering requires." Then I produce a concrete production-phase scope with an honest timeline estimate.

> **What great looks like:** The candidate enumerates specific gaps (not vague "there's more work"), sizes each one roughly in person-weeks, and offers a clear handoff plan that includes what the customer's own engineering team needs to own versus what remains an FDE engagement.

> **Common Mistake:** Underestimating the production gap to avoid an awkward conversation. Customers who hear "we're close to production" and then discover a three-month production engineering effort lose trust in ways that are very hard to recover from.
:::
