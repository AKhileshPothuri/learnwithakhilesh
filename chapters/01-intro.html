7.1 Generative vs. Discriminative Models

To understand generative AI, you first need to understand what it is not. Traditional machine learning systems — the kind that dominated the field from the 1990s through the early 2010s — are largely discriminative. A discriminative model learns to draw a boundary between categories in the data. Given a feature vector representing an email, a spam classifier learns where the boundary lies between “spam” and “not spam.” It outputs a conditional probability:

\[P(y \mid x)\]

The model never needs to understand what a legitimate email looks like in full — it only needs to know which side of the boundary any given email falls on. This is a powerful and efficient approach for classification and regression tasks, underpinning everything from logistic regression to modern convolutional neural networks like ResNet.

A generative model operates with a fundamentally different objective. Instead of learning the boundary between classes, it learns the underlying distribution of the data itself — the joint probability \(P(x, y)\), or in the case of unconditional generation, just \(P(x)\). A model that has learned \(P(x)\) understands, in a statistical sense, what “data in this domain” looks like. From that understanding, it can produce new examples consistent with the learned distribution.

The Core Analogy

Spam filter vs. email writer. A discriminative spam filter reads your email and outputs a label — spam or not-spam. GPT-4, by contrast, can write you a persuasive sales email, continue an unfinished draft, or translate brusque notes into professional prose. ResNet (discriminative) classifies an image as “cat” or “dog.” DALL-E (generative) produces an image of a cat riding a dog in the style of Van Gogh.

The inputs and outputs are inverted: discriminative models consume data and produce labels; generative models consume a specification or partial context and produce new data.

7.1.1 Probability Notation (Mid/FDE Level)

Model Type What It Models Trained To Maximize
Discriminative \(P(y \mid x)\) Correct class probability
Generative (joint) \(P(x, y)\) Joint data likelihood
Generative (causal LM) \(P(x) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{<t})\) Next-token log-likelihood
Generative (conditional) \(P(x \mid c)\) where \(c\) is a prompt/image Conditional likelihood

GPT-style models are trained to maximize \(\sum_t \log P(x_t \mid x_{<t})\), which is equivalent to modeling the full sequence distribution \(P(x)\) via the chain rule of probability. This factorization is why they can generate novel text: they have learned a distribution over sequences from which new sequences can be sampled.

Common Misconception

“Generate” does not mean “hallucinate,” though hallucination is a real failure mode. Generating text means sampling from a learned probability distribution over sequences. When that distribution is well-calibrated, the outputs are accurate and useful. Hallucination occurs when the model assigns high probability to plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content — a failure of calibration, not an inherent property of generation.

7.1.2 BERT vs. GPT: Two Sides of the Transformer Family

A critical nuance often missed in interviews: both BERT and GPT are transformer-based, but they represent opposite ends of the discriminative/generative spectrum.

  • BERT uses a bidirectional encoder trained with masked language modeling (predict masked tokens using context from both directions). It is fine-tuned for discriminative tasks: question answering, classification, NER. You cannot natively “generate” from BERT.
  • GPT uses a causal (autoregressive) decoder trained to predict the next token given all previous tokens. It is generative: you prompt it and it continues the sequence. GPT-style models are the basis of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini.

The architectural distinction — encoder vs. decoder — has deep practical implications for deployment, fine-tuning, and capability (covered in Chapter 3).

7.2 Why Now? The Three Enabling Ingredients

The techniques underlying modern generative AI are not entirely new. Artificial neural networks date to the 1950s. Language modeling — predicting the probability of the next word — is a decades-old NLP problem. So why did the current wave of capable generative AI emerge in 2022 and not 2002 or 2012?

The answer lies in the convergence of three distinct enabling factors: a new architecture, an explosion in compute, and the availability of web-scale data. Each was necessary but not sufficient.

7.2.1 1. The Transformer Architecture (2017)

Vaswani et al.’s “Attention Is All You Need” (2017) introduced the transformer, replacing recurrent neural networks (RNNs) as the dominant architecture for sequential data. Before transformers, RNNs processed sequences one token at a time, maintaining a hidden state updated at each step. This sequential dependency meant training could not be parallelized across time — you had to process token 1 before token 2, token 2 before token 3.

The transformer replaced recurrence entirely with self-attention: every token attends to every other token simultaneously with learned weights. Because there is no sequential dependency, the entire sequence is processed in parallel across GPU cores. This single change unlocked a 10-100× improvement in training efficiency and enabled enormous model scales.

Analogy: RNN vs Transformer

An RNN reads a book sequentially — you must finish chapter 5 before chapter 6 can begin. A transformer reads the entire book simultaneously, with every page “looking at” every other page to build context. The parallel reader finishes in a fraction of the time.

7.2.2 2. GPU-Scale Compute

The 2012 AlexNet moment signaled that GPU-accelerated training could make previously infeasible model scales tractable. In the decade that followed, AI hardware exploded: NVIDIA’s A100 and H100 GPUs deliver tens of teraflops of half-precision performance; hyperscale cloud providers assembled clusters of thousands of chips connected by high-bandwidth interconnects. Training GPT-3 (2020) cost an estimated $4-12 million in compute alone and required thousands of A100-equivalent GPUs running for months. That infrastructure simply did not exist before roughly 2018-2020.

7.2.3 3. Web-Scale Data

Transformers are data-hungry in a way RNNs were not. By 2020, the internet had produced approximately 1 trillion tokens of publicly crawlable English text in Common Crawl alone — plus billions of lines of code on GitHub, millions of books, and the entirety of Wikipedia in dozens of languages.

7.2.4 Why Convergence Mattered

Year What Was Available What Was Missing Result
2012 GPU compute Transformer, web-scale data Good CV models, weak NLP
2015 GPU compute, early web data Transformer LSTM-based NLP, limited
2017 Transformer architecture Sufficient compute + data Small GPT-1 level models
2020 All three RLHF alignment GPT-3: emergent few-shot learning
2022 All three + RLHF — ChatGPT: the consumer breakout

The 2022 ChatGPT moment added one more ingredient — RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) — to align model outputs with human preferences. But the fundamental capability had already emerged from the three-way convergence.

7.3 Foundation Models: What Makes Them “Foundation”

The term “foundation model” was coined by the Stanford HAI group in their 2021 paper “On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models.” It captures something specific and important about the current AI architecture.

A foundation model is a large-scale model pretrained on broad, diverse data at significant compute expense that serves as a starting point adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks without retraining from scratch.

Three defining properties:

Scale. Foundation models have billions to trillions of parameters trained on hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens. This scale enables emergent capabilities not present in smaller models.

Generality. They were not designed for any single task. GPT-4, trained on text, can answer questions, write code, translate languages, summarize documents, and reason through novel problems — without task-specific retraining for each.

Adaptability via emergence. Foundation models exhibit emergent abilities: capabilities that appear suddenly at certain scales and were not explicitly trained for. Wei et al. (2022) documented dozens of tasks where model performance was near-random below a threshold scale and jumped to near-human performance above it. Multi-step arithmetic, logical reasoning chains, and multi-language translation all show this pattern.

Emergence ≠ Magic

Emergence is an empirical observation, not a design guarantee. The fact that a capability emerged at 100B parameters does not mean it will be reliable, calibrated, or safe. Capability evaluation on actual target tasks — not extrapolation from benchmark curves — is mandatory before deployment.

7.3.1 The Pre-Train → Fine-Tune → Align Paradigm

This is the central workflow in modern LLM development, and every level of interview will probe your understanding of it.

Web-scale text + code
        ↓
   [Pretraining]
   Predict next token on ~1T+ tokens
   Learn language, facts, reasoning patterns
   Cost: $1M–$100M+
        ↓
   [Base Model]
   Can complete text, but raw/unaligned
        ↓
   [Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)]
   Train on human-written (prompt, response) pairs
   Teaches the model to follow instructions
   Cost: $10K–$1M
        ↓
   [Alignment / RLHF]
   Reward model trained on human preference comparisons
   PPO or DPO optimizes the LLM toward preferred outputs
   Teaches helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty
   Cost: $10K–$100K
        ↓
   [Aligned Chat Model]
   What you interact with as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini

Each stage has different data requirements, cost structures, and failure modes. As an FDE, you will typically be working with aligned models from providers — but understanding what happened upstream explains the model’s behavior, biases, and limitations.

7.4.2 APIs and SDKs Layer

The OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google Gemini APIs expose capabilities through simple HTTP endpoints. They provide abstractions like system prompts, function/tool calling, structured output modes, and fine-tuning endpoints. API pricing ranges from fractions of a cent to several dollars per million tokens depending on model capability.

7.4.3 Orchestration and Tooling Layer

Libraries like LangChain and LlamaIndex provide abstractions for common GenAI application patterns: retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), multi-step chains, agent loops, structured output parsing, and memory management. DSPy treats prompt engineering as a compilation problem. Vector databases (Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma, pgvector) provide the semantic search infrastructure that RAG pipelines require.

7.4.4 Applications Layer

Developer tools (Cursor, GitHub Copilot), search and knowledge products (Perplexity, Glean), legal platforms (Harvey, Casetext), enterprise productivity (Microsoft Copilot), and thousands of vertical SaaS products.

Where FDEs Live

A Forward Deployed Engineer role sits primarily at the orchestration and application layers: you are not training models, but you are deploying them into enterprise environments, customizing through prompting and fine-tuning, integrating with enterprise data sources, and ensuring production reliability, latency, and accuracy requirements are met. Understanding the full stack — including layers you don’t directly build — is essential for diagnosing problems and communicating tradeoffs.

7.5 GenAI Capabilities and Limitations

Before deploying GenAI in production, you need a clear-eyed inventory of what it is genuinely good at and where it systematically fails.

7.5.1 Core Capabilities

Text generation and transformation. Drafting, editing, summarizing, translating, classifying, and extracting structured data from unstructured text. This is where LLMs are most mature and highest-confidence.

Code generation. Modern models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1) can write, explain, debug, and translate code across dozens of languages. GitHub Copilot reports 30-50% of code accepted in production. Accuracy degrades on novel frameworks and very long functions.

Multimodal understanding. Models like GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro ingest images, audio, and video alongside text. This enables document parsing (tables, charts), visual question answering, and audio transcription.

Reasoning (chain-of-thought). With proper prompting or in reasoning-specialized models (o1, o3, Claude’s extended thinking), LLMs can work through multi-step problems in math, logic, and planning. Performance varies significantly by domain and problem structure.

Broad world knowledge. LLMs trained on internet-scale data have implicit knowledge across science, history, law, medicine, and technology up to their knowledge cutoff.

7.5.2 Systematic Limitations

Production Gotchas — Read Before You Deploy

1. Hallucination. LLMs generate text that is fluent and confident but factually wrong. The mechanism: the model is optimizing token prediction, not factual accuracy. A plausible-sounding but incorrect token can have lower cross-entropy loss than a hedged but accurate statement. Mitigation: RAG grounding, output verification, human review for high-stakes facts.

2. Knowledge cutoff. Model weights encode the world as of their training cutoff (e.g., early 2024 for many current models). They have no knowledge of events after that date unless provided in context. Mitigation: retrieval-augmented generation, tool-use to query live data sources.

3. Context window limits. Current context windows range from ~16K (smaller models) to ~1M tokens (Gemini 1.5 Pro). Very long documents still require chunking strategies that can lose cross-document reasoning. Even models with large context windows show degraded performance (“lost in the middle”) on information in the center of long prompts.

4. No persistent memory across sessions. By default, each API call is stateless. The model has no memory of previous conversations. Mitigation: explicitly inject relevant context, use vector-database memory systems.

5. Cost at scale. Token-based pricing means high-volume production use can be expensive. GPT-4o costs roughly $5-15 per million output tokens. A product generating 10M tokens/day costs $50-150K/month in inference alone. Mitigation: model routing (use smaller models for simple tasks), caching, prompt compression.

6. Latency. Large models have meaningful time-to-first-token (TTFT) and generation latency. GPT-4 class models: 1-3 seconds TTFT, 30-100 tokens/second. Mitigation: streaming outputs, smaller models for latency-sensitive paths, speculative decoding.

7. Non-determinism. Model outputs are sampled, so the same prompt produces different responses across calls (unless temperature=0). This makes unit testing and regression testing of AI-powered systems non-trivial.

7.6 Explaining GenAI to Non-Technical Stakeholders

One of the most important skills tested in FDE interviews — and often asked explicitly — is the ability to explain complex AI concepts clearly to non-technical audiences. Interviewers ask this because FDEs spend large fractions of their time communicating with customer executives, legal and compliance teams, and business stakeholders.

7.6.1 The Two-Framing Approach

Framing 1: “Auto-complete for everything.” Your phone’s keyboard predicts the next word as you type — ChatGPT does the same thing, but trained on trillions of words, with a model containing billions of parameters, completing entire documents, analyses, and conversations. This framing conveys the statistical nature of the output: just as your phone might suggest a wrong word, language models can produce incorrect text. The limitation: it undersells emergent capabilities, so use it as an entry point, not the full explanation.

Framing 2: “An extremely well-read intern.” Imagine an intern who has read virtually everything ever published — every textbook, every Wikipedia article, every news story, every codebase on GitHub — with extraordinarily good pattern recognition for how language and ideas connect. They can draft a contract, explain a scientific concept, write code, or summarize a document faster than any human. But this intern has critical limitations: not connected to live information, cannot reliably self-fact-check, and will answer confidently even when wrong, because confident fluency is exactly what they learned from their training data.

What to Always Convey to Non-Technical Stakeholders
  • LLMs predict text — they do not retrieve it from a database like search engines do
  • They can be wrong confidently, which is different from being obviously uncertain
  • They do not have live access to information unless connected to external tools
  • They are excellent at language tasks but poor at precise arithmetic, verified facts, and real-time information
  • Successful production deployments keep humans in the loop for high-stakes decisions

7.6.2 The Honest Business Framing

Setting accurate expectations in the first customer conversation is the difference between a successful deployment and a failed one. Overselling leads to production trust failures when hallucinations emerge. Underselling leads to missed adoption. The correct frame: “AI dramatically reduces the cost of tasks involving language generation, summarization, and extraction. It does not eliminate the need for human judgment on high-stakes outputs.”

7.9 Cheat Sheet

  • Generative vs discriminative: discriminative models \(P(y|x)\) classify; generative models learn \(P(x)\) and can sample new data
  • GPT = autoregressive decoder (causal, generates tokens left-to-right); BERT = bidirectional encoder (discriminative, cannot generate natively)
  • Three ingredients for modern LLMs: transformer architecture (2017), GPU compute scale, web-scale data — all three needed simultaneously
  • Transformers beat RNNs because self-attention parallelizes across the full sequence; RNNs process sequentially and struggle with long-range dependencies
  • Foundation model properties: scale, generality, adaptability — enables one model to cover many tasks without task-specific retraining
  • Pre-train → SFT → RLHF: pretraining builds world knowledge, SFT teaches instruction-following format, RLHF aligns outputs with human preferences
  • Emergent abilities appear discontinuously at scale — cannot reliably predict them by extrapolation from smaller models
  • Next-token prediction is a deceptively general proxy: to predict the next token well across diverse text, the model must learn grammar, facts, reasoning, and discourse structure
  • Hallucination cause: the training objective (minimize cross-entropy on tokens) is not identical to “produce factually accurate statements” — fluent wrongness can have lower loss than hedged accuracy
  • Knowledge cutoff: model weights are frozen; no access to post-training events without retrieval tools
  • Context window limits: information in the center of long prompts is underutilized — “lost in the middle” effect documented empirically
  • Cost arithmetic matters: at scale, token pricing dominates — always calculate expected monthly inference cost before committing to a model tier
  • FDE scoping questions: what is input/output, what is cost-of-error, is there a human in the loop, what does the baseline process cost?
  • Stack position: Entry builds on models, Mid designs systems, FDE deploys and adapts for customer contexts — all require understanding every layer
  • RAG: the primary mitigation for hallucination and knowledge cutoff in production — retrieves source documents and provides them as context for grounded generation

7.10 Interview Questions

7.10.1 Entry Level

Entry Level

Q1. What is generative AI and how does it differ from traditional machine learning?

Model Answer

Traditional machine learning models are predominantly discriminative — they learn to map inputs to outputs by learning a conditional probability \(P(y \mid x)\). A spam classifier outputs a label. A sentiment model outputs positive/negative. These models only need to learn where the boundary between classes lies; they never need to understand what “typical spam” looks like in full.

Generative AI models learn the underlying distribution of the data itself — \(P(x)\) or \(P(x, y)\) — and can sample new examples from that distribution. A generative text model has learned what text looks like at a statistical level, and can produce new text by sampling from that learned distribution.

The practical distinction: discriminative models consume data and produce labels or predictions; generative models consume a specification, partial input, or prompt and produce new data — text, images, audio, or code. ResNet is discriminative (classifies images); DALL-E is generative (creates images). BERT is discriminative (classifies text); GPT-4 is generative (produces text). The key insight for interviews: generative models are not magic — they are sophisticated statistical models that assign probabilities to sequences and can be sampled to produce new sequences.

Q2. Name three real-world applications of generative AI and the type of model behind each.

Model Answer

Code completion (GitHub Copilot, Cursor): powered by large causal language models (decoder-only transformers). These models are trained on billions of lines of code and predict the next token in a code context, manifesting as full function completions and docstring generation.

Text-to-image generation (DALL-E 3, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion): uses diffusion models — a class of generative model that learns to reverse a gradual noising process. The model starts with random noise and iteratively denoises it, conditioned on a text prompt, until a coherent image emerges.

Enterprise document summarization / chat (Glean, Notion AI, custom RAG systems): uses large decoder-only language models augmented with retrieval. The model reads retrieved document chunks and synthesizes a coherent response. Understanding that different applications often combine multiple model types (a language model for generation, an embedding model for retrieval, a vision model for images) is important for mid-level and FDE candidates.

Q3. What is a foundation model and what distinguishes it from a task-specific ML model?

Model Answer

A foundation model is a large-scale model pretrained on broad, diverse data that can be adapted to many downstream tasks. Contrast with task-specific models trained from scratch on labeled data for one narrow problem (a spam classifier, a sentiment detector).

Three key distinctions — scope, training cost, and adaptation mechanism. A task-specific ResNet for X-ray classification is trained entirely on X-ray images with labels and has no knowledge of anything else. GPT-4 was trained on essentially all digitized human knowledge, giving it broad capabilities across language, reasoning, and code.

The adaptation mechanism is also fundamentally different. Task-specific models require labeled data and retraining for each new task. Foundation models can be adapted through prompting (zero-shot or few-shot examples in context), fine-tuning on a small labeled dataset, or retrieval-augmentation — all far cheaper than retraining. The practical implication for any GenAI role: you are almost always building on top of a foundation model, not training from scratch.

Q4. What was the significance of the Transformer paper (2017)?

Model Answer

Vaswani et al.’s “Attention Is All You Need” (2017) introduced the transformer architecture, which replaced recurrent neural networks as the dominant architecture for sequence modeling. The core innovation was self-attention: instead of processing sequences one token at a time (as RNNs do), every token in the sequence attends to every other token simultaneously using learned attention weights. This eliminated the sequential dependency in RNNs that prevented parallelization.

The practical consequence was enormous: transformer training could be parallelized across the entire sequence length, making it feasible to train on orders of magnitude more data using GPU clusters. RNNs also struggled with long-range dependencies — gradients vanished over long sequences — while transformers handle long-range context through direct attention connections between any two positions.

The 2017 paper is effectively the inflection point of the modern AI era: every major LLM in production today is a descendant of that architecture.

Q5. What is “emergent behavior” in LLMs and give an example?

Model Answer

Emergent behavior in LLMs refers to capabilities that appear suddenly and unpredictably at certain scales — capabilities that were not present in smaller models and were not explicitly trained for. Wei et al.’s 2022 paper “Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models” documented dozens of tasks where performance was near-random below a threshold scale and jumped to near-human performance above it.

The canonical example is multi-step arithmetic. Below approximately 10 billion parameters, LLMs perform multi-step math at chance level. Above roughly 100 billion parameters, the same models suddenly solve them reliably. No training objective explicitly taught arithmetic — it emerged from sufficient exposure to mathematical text and reasoning patterns at sufficient scale.

For practitioners, the practical implication is critical: you cannot reliably predict which capabilities a model will have by extrapolating from smaller models. Capability evaluation on actual target tasks is mandatory before deployment.

Q6. List four major limitations of LLMs in production.

Model Answer

Hallucination: LLMs generate fluent, confident, factually incorrect output. The training objective (minimize cross-entropy on tokens) does not penalize factual errors directly — plausible-sounding wrong answers can have lower loss than hedged accurate ones. Mitigation: RAG, human review loops.

Knowledge cutoff: Model weights are frozen at training time. Events after the cutoff date are unknown unless provided in context. Mitigation: retrieval-augmented generation, tool use.

Context window limits: Even 1M-token context windows (Gemini 1.5 Pro) face the “lost in the middle” effect — models perform best on information at the beginning and end of context, and worse on information in the middle. Mitigation: smart chunking and retrieval strategies.

Cost and latency: Large models are expensive ($5-15/million output tokens for GPT-4 class) and have meaningful latency (1-3 seconds time-to-first-token). At production scale, this dominates cost structure. Mitigation: model routing, smaller models for simple tasks, caching.

7.10.2 Mid Level

Mid Level

Q7. Explain the difference between generative and discriminative models using probability notation.

Model Answer

A discriminative model directly models the conditional probability \(P(y \mid x)\) — the probability of a label \(y\) given observed features \(x\). It learns the boundary between classes without needing to model what typical examples of each class look like. Logistic regression, SVMs, BERT fine-tuned for classification, and ResNet all fall here.

A generative model models the joint probability \(P(x, y)\) or, in the unsupervised case, the marginal \(P(x)\). From the joint, you can derive \(P(y \mid x) = P(x, y) / P(x)\) via Bayes’ theorem — so generative models can technically classify, but their primary capability is sampling: given the learned distribution \(P(x)\), draw new samples \(x^* \sim P(x)\) that look like training data.

GPT-style models are trained to maximize \(\sum_t \log P(x_t \mid x_{<t})\), which is equivalent to modeling \(P(x)\) over sequences via the chain rule: \(P(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{<t})\). The interviewer distinction to nail: discriminative models are often more accurate for their specific task, but they cannot produce new data; generative models can produce new data at the cost of modeling a harder, higher-dimensional distribution.

Q8. What are the three technical developments that made the current LLM wave possible, and why did they need to converge?

Model Answer

The three developments are: (1) the transformer architecture (2017), enabling parallelizable training on long sequences; (2) GPU-scale compute, specifically large GPU clusters with high-bandwidth interconnects enabling training runs of hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours; and (3) web-scale text data, including Common Crawl, GitHub, curated corpora like The Pile and RedPajama.

Each was necessary but insufficient alone. Transformers without compute produce small models with modest capabilities — GPT-1 (2018) had 117M parameters. Compute without transformers would have just scaled slow, hard-to-parallelize RNNs that plateau in quality because of gradient issues over long sequences. Data without architecture and compute produces an untrainable dataset.

The 2020 GPT-3 paper was the first demonstration of all three converging: 175B parameters trained on ~300B tokens produced emergent capabilities including few-shot learning that were qualitatively absent in smaller models. Common Crawl had been accumulating since 2008; GPU hardware hit the necessary performance/cost ratio around 2018-2020; the transformer arrived in 2017. Before 2017, the data was there but the architecture and hardware weren’t. By 2020, all three were available simultaneously.

Q9. What does it mean when we say an LLM “predicts the next token”? How does that mechanism lead to seemingly intelligent outputs?

Model Answer

At each inference step, an LLM takes the sequence of tokens processed so far and produces a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary (typically 50,000-100,000 tokens). The “prediction” is this distribution: each token gets a probability, and together they sum to 1. The training objective: maximize the probability assigned to the actual next token in the training corpus, equivalent to minimizing cross-entropy loss.

To predict the next token accurately across a diverse corpus — news articles, scientific papers, code, fiction, legal documents — a model must implicitly learn the grammar, vocabulary, facts, reasoning patterns, and discourse structure of every domain in its training data. A model that accurately predicts the next token in a physics textbook has necessarily learned something about physics.

Next-token prediction is a deceptively general proxy task. Unlike supervised tasks that teach a specific mapping, predicting text requires learning virtually everything that makes text coherent. The “intelligence” emerges from the pressure to minimize prediction loss across a distribution so broad that the only way to succeed is to internalize deep regularities about language, knowledge, and reasoning. This is also why the model can be wrong: the objective is probabilistic accuracy, not factual correctness, and the model will confidently predict a plausible-sounding but incorrect token if that is what the training distribution supports.

Q10. Explain in-context learning — why can an LLM learn from examples in the prompt without gradient updates?

Model Answer

In-context learning (ICL) refers to the ability of large language models to perform new tasks when given a few input-output examples in the prompt, without any change to the model’s weights. You show the model three sentiment examples and it classifies the fourth — no fine-tuning, no gradient descent.

The leading mechanistic explanation (Akyürek et al., 2022; von Oswald et al., 2023) is that ICL implements a form of implicit gradient descent within the forward pass. The attention mechanism in transformers can, in principle, compute updates to internal representations that simulate what gradient descent on the examples would produce, using only forward-pass computation. The key evidence: ICL performance scales with model size and training diversity, and models make errors consistent with what a gradient-descent learner would predict.

A more intuitive framing: pretraining on diverse text exposed the model to millions of task demonstrations. When you provide examples in the prompt, the model recognizes the pattern (“this is a classification task where the format is input → label”) and retrieves the relevant computation from its weights. ICL works better for task formats that appeared frequently in pretraining data and degrades for unusual or novel task structures. The practical implication: few-shot examples in the prompt are often the most effective and cheapest way to adapt model behavior, and example quality and selection strategy (cover edge cases, use diverse examples) matters significantly.

Q11. Compare RAG vs. fine-tuning for adapting a foundation model to a new domain. When would you use each?

Model Answer

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) augments the model at inference time by retrieving relevant documents and inserting them into the context window. The model weights are unchanged. Fine-tuning updates the model weights on domain-specific data, changing how the model generates text even without retrieval context.

Use RAG when: your domain knowledge is dynamic (updated frequently), you need citations and source attribution, you have a large corpus of proprietary documents, you want to minimize cost and infrastructure, or you need fast iteration without model retraining. RAG handles factual knowledge that changes over time naturally — you just update the index, not the model.

Use fine-tuning when: you need a specific response format or style the base model doesn’t follow, you’re adapting to a specialized domain where the base model has weak priors (e.g., medical coding, proprietary technical jargon), you want to make the model more efficient on a narrow task (smaller model with fine-tuning can match larger model on specific tasks), or you need consistent behavioral changes that prompting alone can’t achieve reliably.

In practice, the best production systems often combine both: fine-tune the model on domain data for style and format consistency, then use RAG to ground factual claims in live retrieval. The critical mistake to avoid: reaching for fine-tuning first when RAG would solve the problem with a fraction of the effort and no model maintenance overhead.

7.10.3 Forward Deployed Engineer

Forward Deployed Engineer

Q12. A customer’s CEO asks: “How does ChatGPT actually work?” Give a 2-minute explanation for a non-technical executive.

Model Answer

“Think about the autocomplete on your phone — it suggests the next word as you type, based on what words typically follow what you’ve already written. ChatGPT does essentially the same thing, but at a scale that’s hard to imagine. It was trained on roughly a trillion words of text from the internet, books, code, and academic papers — more than any human could read in thousands of lifetimes. In doing so, it learned the patterns of how language works across every topic covered in that data.

When you ask it a question, it’s not looking up your answer in a database the way Google does. It’s generating an answer word-by-word, picking what word is most likely to come next given everything you’ve said and everything it learned. Because it learned from so much text on so many topics, those generated words turn into surprisingly accurate, coherent answers.

The limitation is exactly what you’d expect: it learned from text, not from verified facts. So it can write confidently even when it’s wrong — like a very eloquent person who sometimes misremembers things. It also has no live information unless you give it access to external tools. For your business, this means it’s extraordinarily powerful for tasks where you need to generate, summarize, or transform text — but for tasks where precision matters, we build guardrails and verification steps into the workflow.”

The key elements: intuitive analogy, honest about limitations, tied to business implications.

Q13. A skeptical customer says “AI just makes stuff up — why should we trust it for our business?” How do you respond?

Model Answer

I validate the concern before addressing it — dismissing it signals naivety. “You’re absolutely right that hallucination is a real and documented problem. I wouldn’t recommend deploying AI in any business-critical context without explicitly accounting for it. The question isn’t whether AI can be wrong — it can — but whether we can engineer a system where the failure rate and failure mode are acceptable for your specific use case.”

Then I distinguish use cases: for tasks where the AI summarizes documents the customer already owns, extracts structured data from forms, or drafts text a human will review before it goes out, the hallucination risk is manageable with humans in the loop for high-stakes decisions. For fully autonomous high-stakes decisions (patient diagnoses, financial trades), the bar is much higher and requires different architecture.

Then I explain the architectural mitigations: retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds the model’s answers in retrieved source documents, dramatically reducing hallucination on factual queries; fine-tuning on domain-specific data shifts behavior toward their domain; confidence scoring and abstention mechanisms flag uncertain responses for human review; evaluation pipelines measure hallucination rates on representative samples before deployment.

I close with: “The companies using AI successfully in production aren’t trusting it blindly — they’re engineering systems that use AI for what it’s genuinely good at while keeping humans responsible for what it isn’t. Let me show you how that looks for your use case specifically.”

Q14. You’re scoping a GenAI project for a customer. What questions do you ask in the first 30 minutes to determine if GenAI is even the right tool?

Model Answer

I start by asking what the customer is actually trying to achieve and what their current process looks like — not what AI solution they want, because customers often arrive with a solution before the problem is properly defined. Specifically:

Problem definition: What is the input and what is the desired output? Is the output language-based, structured data, images, or decisions? Is the task generative (creating new content), extractive (finding or summarizing existing content), or classificatory (routing or labeling)?

Quality and cost-of-error: What happens if the system produces a wrong answer? Is there a human in the loop for review, or does output go directly to customers or downstream automated systems? High-stakes, low-review workflows demand very different architecture than internal productivity tools.

Data inventory: What data do you have that’s relevant to this task? Is it structured or unstructured? Is there labeled ground truth, enabling evaluation? How frequently does it change?

Baseline economics: Is there a current manual process? What does it cost in time and headcount? This sizes the value of automation and establishes the quality bar (the AI must be better or cheaper than the current approach to be worth deploying).

Task type test: Is this actually a language problem? If the customer wants to predict numerical outcomes from structured tabular data, a classical ML model (XGBoost, logistic regression) will almost certainly outperform an LLM and cost a fraction of the price. GenAI is the right tool when input or output is language, or when the task requires broad world knowledge that would be expensive to encode in a rules-based system.

Q15. How do you decide whether a GenAI solution is even the right tool for a given problem?

What great looks like: A senior candidate doesn’t just know GenAI — they have a framework for when not to use it. They compare GenAI against classical ML, deterministic rules, and manual processes. They reason about the cost-of-error for the specific use case, not generically. They identify when the “AI solution” a customer wants is actually a data pipeline problem, a rules problem, or a product design problem.

Common Mistake: Answering this question by listing GenAI capabilities (what it can do) instead of the decision framework (when to use it vs. alternatives). Interviewers are testing your judgment as a practitioner, not your ability to recite model capabilities.

Model Answer

My framework is a four-part decision tree:

1. Is the input/output language or media? If yes, GenAI is potentially appropriate. If no (e.g., predicting churn from structured user data, forecasting sales from time series), classical ML is almost always cheaper, faster, and more accurate for that specific task. Don’t shoehorn an LLM into a tabular regression problem.

2. What is the cost-of-error and review structure? GenAI with a human reviewer in the loop can operate at higher error rates than autonomous GenAI. A system that drafts emails for human review tolerates 5% hallucination rate. A system that auto-sends customer communications cannot. Error tolerance determines whether you need a 70B+ model, a smaller fine-tuned model, or RAG grounding — and whether GenAI is appropriate at all.

3. What is the baseline? If the current process is manual and slow, GenAI’s 80% accuracy at 100× speed might be a massive win. If there is an existing rule-based system with 99.5% accuracy, GenAI’s 95% accuracy is a regression. Always establish the baseline before proposing GenAI.

4. Is the knowledge dynamic? If the task requires up-to-date factual knowledge (current prices, live inventory, recent news), a static model without retrieval is likely to fail in production. RAG or tool-use architecture is required.

If all four checks pass: GenAI is likely appropriate. Proceed to choosing between RAG, fine-tuning, prompting, or a combination based on the data and quality requirements.

Q16. A non-technical executive asks “why can’t we just use GPT-3.5 instead of GPT-4 for this use case?”

Model Answer

I frame the answer around capability, reliability, and cost — not version numbers. “Both are language models from OpenAI, but they differ substantially in what they can reliably do. Think of GPT-3.5 as a capable junior analyst who can draft text and answer questions reasonably well, but struggles with complex multi-step tasks, makes logical errors on harder problems, and often misses nuanced instructions. GPT-4 is more like a senior analyst — it follows complex instructions more reliably, reasons through multi-step problems more accurately, and makes significantly fewer errors on tasks requiring judgment.”

The concrete differences: GPT-4 shows roughly 40% fewer factual errors on knowledge benchmarks, handles longer and more complex instructions without losing track of requirements, performs significantly better on reasoning-intensive tasks (coding, math, legal analysis), and supports multimodal inputs (images). For simple tasks — summarizing a short document, classifying sentiment, extracting a structured field — GPT-3.5-class models may be entirely sufficient at a fraction of the cost.

The honest business framing: GPT-3.5 costs roughly 10-20× less than GPT-4 per token. If the use case is high-volume simple-task processing where a 5% error rate is acceptable, GPT-3.5 might be the right economic choice. If the customer is using AI to support high-stakes decisions or customer-facing responses where errors damage trust, GPT-4’s reliability improvement pays for itself quickly. The correct answer is: “Let’s benchmark both models on a representative sample of your actual data and measure error rates — then the cost decision is clear.”

## Generative vs. Discriminative Models

To understand generative AI, you first need to understand what it is not. Traditional machine learning systems — the kind that dominated the field from the 1990s through the early 2010s — are largely **discriminative**. A discriminative model learns to draw a boundary between categories in the data. Given a feature vector representing an email, a spam classifier learns where the boundary lies between "spam" and "not spam." It outputs a conditional probability:

$$P(y \mid x)$$

The model never needs to understand what a legitimate email looks like in full — it only needs to know which side of the boundary any given email falls on. This is a powerful and efficient approach for classification and regression tasks, underpinning everything from logistic regression to modern convolutional neural networks like ResNet.

A **generative model** operates with a fundamentally different objective. Instead of learning the boundary between classes, it learns the **underlying distribution of the data itself** — the joint probability $P(x, y)$, or in the case of unconditional generation, just $P(x)$. A model that has learned $P(x)$ understands, in a statistical sense, what "data in this domain" looks like. From that understanding, it can produce new examples consistent with the learned distribution.

::: {.callout-tip title="The Core Analogy"}
**Spam filter vs. email writer.** A discriminative spam filter reads your email and outputs a label — spam or not-spam. GPT-4, by contrast, can write you a persuasive sales email, continue an unfinished draft, or translate brusque notes into professional prose. ResNet (discriminative) classifies an image as "cat" or "dog." DALL-E (generative) produces an image of a cat riding a dog in the style of Van Gogh.

The inputs and outputs are inverted: discriminative models consume data and produce labels; generative models consume a specification or partial context and produce new data.
:::

### Probability Notation (Mid/FDE Level)

| Model Type | What It Models | Trained To Maximize |
|---|---|---|
| Discriminative | $P(y \mid x)$ | Correct class probability |
| Generative (joint) | $P(x, y)$ | Joint data likelihood |
| Generative (causal LM) | $P(x) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ | Next-token log-likelihood |
| Generative (conditional) | $P(x \mid c)$ where $c$ is a prompt/image | Conditional likelihood |

GPT-style models are trained to maximize $\sum_t \log P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, which is equivalent to modeling the full sequence distribution $P(x)$ via the chain rule of probability. This factorization is why they can generate novel text: they have learned a distribution over sequences from which new sequences can be sampled.

::: {.callout-warning title="Common Misconception"}
"Generate" does not mean "hallucinate," though hallucination is a real failure mode. Generating text means sampling from a learned probability distribution over sequences. When that distribution is well-calibrated, the outputs are accurate and useful. Hallucination occurs when the model assigns high probability to plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content — a failure of calibration, not an inherent property of generation.
:::

### BERT vs. GPT: Two Sides of the Transformer Family

A critical nuance often missed in interviews: both BERT and GPT are transformer-based, but they represent opposite ends of the discriminative/generative spectrum.

- **BERT** uses a **bidirectional encoder** trained with masked language modeling (predict masked tokens using context from both directions). It is fine-tuned for discriminative tasks: question answering, classification, NER. You cannot natively "generate" from BERT.
- **GPT** uses a **causal (autoregressive) decoder** trained to predict the next token given all previous tokens. It is generative: you prompt it and it continues the sequence. GPT-style models are the basis of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini.

The architectural distinction — encoder vs. decoder — has deep practical implications for deployment, fine-tuning, and capability (covered in Chapter 3).

## Why Now? The Three Enabling Ingredients

The techniques underlying modern generative AI are not entirely new. Artificial neural networks date to the 1950s. Language modeling — predicting the probability of the next word — is a decades-old NLP problem. So why did the current wave of capable generative AI emerge in 2022 and not 2002 or 2012?

The answer lies in the **convergence of three distinct enabling factors**: a new architecture, an explosion in compute, and the availability of web-scale data. Each was necessary but not sufficient.

### 1. The Transformer Architecture (2017)

Vaswani et al.'s "Attention Is All You Need" (2017) introduced the transformer, replacing recurrent neural networks (RNNs) as the dominant architecture for sequential data. Before transformers, RNNs processed sequences one token at a time, maintaining a hidden state updated at each step. This sequential dependency meant training could not be parallelized across time — you had to process token 1 before token 2, token 2 before token 3.

The transformer replaced recurrence entirely with **self-attention**: every token attends to every other token simultaneously with learned weights. Because there is no sequential dependency, the entire sequence is processed in parallel across GPU cores. This single change unlocked a 10-100× improvement in training efficiency and enabled enormous model scales.

::: {.callout-tip title="Analogy: RNN vs Transformer"}
An RNN reads a book sequentially — you must finish chapter 5 before chapter 6 can begin. A transformer reads the entire book simultaneously, with every page "looking at" every other page to build context. The parallel reader finishes in a fraction of the time.
:::

### 2. GPU-Scale Compute

The 2012 AlexNet moment signaled that GPU-accelerated training could make previously infeasible model scales tractable. In the decade that followed, AI hardware exploded: NVIDIA's A100 and H100 GPUs deliver tens of teraflops of half-precision performance; hyperscale cloud providers assembled clusters of thousands of chips connected by high-bandwidth interconnects. Training GPT-3 (2020) cost an estimated $4-12 million in compute alone and required thousands of A100-equivalent GPUs running for months. That infrastructure simply did not exist before roughly 2018-2020.

### 3. Web-Scale Data

Transformers are data-hungry in a way RNNs were not. By 2020, the internet had produced approximately 1 trillion tokens of publicly crawlable English text in Common Crawl alone — plus billions of lines of code on GitHub, millions of books, and the entirety of Wikipedia in dozens of languages.

### Why Convergence Mattered

| Year | What Was Available | What Was Missing | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 | GPU compute | Transformer, web-scale data | Good CV models, weak NLP |
| 2015 | GPU compute, early web data | Transformer | LSTM-based NLP, limited |
| 2017 | Transformer architecture | Sufficient compute + data | Small GPT-1 level models |
| 2020 | All three | RLHF alignment | GPT-3: emergent few-shot learning |
| 2022 | All three + RLHF | — | ChatGPT: the consumer breakout |

The 2022 ChatGPT moment added one more ingredient — **RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) — to align model outputs with human preferences. But the fundamental capability had already emerged from the three-way convergence.

## Foundation Models: What Makes Them "Foundation"

The term "foundation model" was coined by the Stanford HAI group in their 2021 paper "On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models." It captures something specific and important about the current AI architecture.

A **foundation model** is a large-scale model pretrained on broad, diverse data at significant compute expense that serves as a **starting point** adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks without retraining from scratch.

Three defining properties:

**Scale.** Foundation models have billions to trillions of parameters trained on hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens. This scale enables emergent capabilities not present in smaller models.

**Generality.** They were not designed for any single task. GPT-4, trained on text, can answer questions, write code, translate languages, summarize documents, and reason through novel problems — without task-specific retraining for each.

**Adaptability via emergence.** Foundation models exhibit emergent abilities: capabilities that appear suddenly at certain scales and were not explicitly trained for. Wei et al. (2022) documented dozens of tasks where model performance was near-random below a threshold scale and jumped to near-human performance above it. Multi-step arithmetic, logical reasoning chains, and multi-language translation all show this pattern.

::: {.callout-warning title="Emergence ≠ Magic"}
Emergence is an empirical observation, not a design guarantee. The fact that a capability emerged at 100B parameters does not mean it will be reliable, calibrated, or safe. Capability evaluation on actual target tasks — not extrapolation from benchmark curves — is mandatory before deployment.
:::

### The Pre-Train → Fine-Tune → Align Paradigm

This is the central workflow in modern LLM development, and every level of interview will probe your understanding of it.

```
Web-scale text + code
        ↓
   [Pretraining]
   Predict next token on ~1T+ tokens
   Learn language, facts, reasoning patterns
   Cost: $1M–$100M+
        ↓
   [Base Model]
   Can complete text, but raw/unaligned
        ↓
   [Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)]
   Train on human-written (prompt, response) pairs
   Teaches the model to follow instructions
   Cost: $10K–$1M
        ↓
   [Alignment / RLHF]
   Reward model trained on human preference comparisons
   PPO or DPO optimizes the LLM toward preferred outputs
   Teaches helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty
   Cost: $10K–$100K
        ↓
   [Aligned Chat Model]
   What you interact with as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini
```

Each stage has different data requirements, cost structures, and failure modes. As an FDE, you will typically be working with aligned models from providers — but understanding what happened upstream explains the model's behavior, biases, and limitations.

DSPy treats prompt engineering as a compilation problem. Vector databases (Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma, pgvector) provide the semantic search infrastructure that RAG pipelines require.

### Applications Layer

Developer tools (Cursor, GitHub Copilot), search and knowledge products (Perplexity, Glean), legal platforms (Harvey, Casetext), enterprise productivity (Microsoft Copilot), and thousands of vertical SaaS products.

::: {.callout-tip title="Where FDEs Live"}
A Forward Deployed Engineer role sits primarily at the **orchestration and application layers**: you are not training models, but you are deploying them into enterprise environments, customizing through prompting and fine-tuning, integrating with enterprise data sources, and ensuring production reliability, latency, and accuracy requirements are met. Understanding the full stack — including layers you don't directly build — is essential for diagnosing problems and communicating tradeoffs.
:::

## GenAI Capabilities and Limitations

Before deploying GenAI in production, you need a clear-eyed inventory of what it is genuinely good at and where it systematically fails.

### Core Capabilities

**Text generation and transformation.** Drafting, editing, summarizing, translating, classifying, and extracting structured data from unstructured text. This is where LLMs are most mature and highest-confidence.

**Code generation.** Modern models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1) can write, explain, debug, and translate code across dozens of languages. GitHub Copilot reports 30-50% of code accepted in production. Accuracy degrades on novel frameworks and very long functions.

**Multimodal understanding.** Models like GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro ingest images, audio, and video alongside text. This enables document parsing (tables, charts), visual question answering, and audio transcription.

**Reasoning (chain-of-thought).** With proper prompting or in reasoning-specialized models (o1, o3, Claude's extended thinking), LLMs can work through multi-step problems in math, logic, and planning. Performance varies significantly by domain and problem structure.

**Broad world knowledge.** LLMs trained on internet-scale data have implicit knowledge across science, history, law, medicine, and technology up to their knowledge cutoff.

### Systematic Limitations

::: {.callout-warning title="Production Gotchas — Read Before You Deploy"}
**1. Hallucination.** LLMs generate text that is fluent and confident but factually wrong. The mechanism: the model is optimizing token prediction, not factual accuracy. A plausible-sounding but incorrect token can have lower cross-entropy loss than a hedged but accurate statement. Mitigation: RAG grounding, output verification, human review for high-stakes facts.

**2. Knowledge cutoff.** Model weights encode the world as of their training cutoff (e.g., early 2024 for many current models). They have no knowledge of events after that date unless provided in context. Mitigation: retrieval-augmented generation, tool-use to query live data sources.

**3. Context window limits.** Current context windows range from ~16K (smaller models) to ~1M tokens (Gemini 1.5 Pro). Very long documents still require chunking strategies that can lose cross-document reasoning. Even models with large context windows show degraded performance ("lost in the middle") on information in the center of long prompts.

**4. No persistent memory across sessions.** By default, each API call is stateless. The model has no memory of previous conversations. Mitigation: explicitly inject relevant context, use vector-database memory systems.

**5. Cost at scale.** Token-based pricing means high-volume production use can be expensive. GPT-4o costs roughly $5-15 per million output tokens. A product generating 10M tokens/day costs $50-150K/month in inference alone. Mitigation: model routing (use smaller models for simple tasks), caching, prompt compression.

**6. Latency.** Large models have meaningful time-to-first-token (TTFT) and generation latency. GPT-4 class models: 1-3 seconds TTFT, 30-100 tokens/second. Mitigation: streaming outputs, smaller models for latency-sensitive paths, speculative decoding.

**7. Non-determinism.** Model outputs are sampled, so the same prompt produces different responses across calls (unless temperature=0). This makes unit testing and regression testing of AI-powered systems non-trivial.
:::

## Explaining GenAI to Non-Technical Stakeholders

One of the most important skills tested in FDE interviews — and often asked explicitly — is the ability to explain complex AI concepts clearly to non-technical audiences. Interviewers ask this because FDEs spend large fractions of their time communicating with customer executives, legal and compliance teams, and business stakeholders.

### The Two-Framing Approach

**Framing 1: "Auto-complete for everything."** Your phone's keyboard predicts the next word as you type — ChatGPT does the same thing, but trained on trillions of words, with a model containing billions of parameters, completing entire documents, analyses, and conversations. This framing conveys the statistical nature of the output: just as your phone might suggest a wrong word, language models can produce incorrect text. The limitation: it undersells emergent capabilities, so use it as an entry point, not the full explanation.

**Framing 2: "An extremely well-read intern."** Imagine an intern who has read virtually everything ever published — every textbook, every Wikipedia article, every news story, every codebase on GitHub — with extraordinarily good pattern recognition for how language and ideas connect. They can draft a contract, explain a scientific concept, write code, or summarize a document faster than any human. But this intern has critical limitations: not connected to live information, cannot reliably self-fact-check, and will answer confidently even when wrong, because confident fluency is exactly what they learned from their training data.

::: {.callout-tip title="What to Always Convey to Non-Technical Stakeholders"}
- LLMs predict text — they do not retrieve it from a database like search engines do
- They can be wrong confidently, which is different from being obviously uncertain
- They do not have live access to information unless connected to external tools
- They are excellent at language tasks but poor at precise arithmetic, verified facts, and real-time information
- Successful production deployments keep humans in the loop for high-stakes decisions
:::

### The Honest Business Framing

Setting accurate expectations in the first customer conversation is the difference between a successful deployment and a failed one. Overselling leads to production trust failures when hallucinations emerge. Underselling leads to missed adoption. The correct frame: "AI dramatically reduces the cost of tasks involving language generation, summarization, and extraction. It does not eliminate the need for human judgment on high-stakes outputs."

## Cheat Sheet

- **Generative vs discriminative**: discriminative models $P(y|x)$ classify; generative models learn $P(x)$ and can sample new data
- **GPT = autoregressive decoder** (causal, generates tokens left-to-right); **BERT = bidirectional encoder** (discriminative, cannot generate natively)
- **Three ingredients for modern LLMs**: transformer architecture (2017), GPU compute scale, web-scale data — all three needed simultaneously
- **Transformers beat RNNs** because self-attention parallelizes across the full sequence; RNNs process sequentially and struggle with long-range dependencies
- **Foundation model properties**: scale, generality, adaptability — enables one model to cover many tasks without task-specific retraining
- **Pre-train → SFT → RLHF**: pretraining builds world knowledge, SFT teaches instruction-following format, RLHF aligns outputs with human preferences
- **Emergent abilities** appear discontinuously at scale — cannot reliably predict them by extrapolation from smaller models
- **Next-token prediction** is a deceptively general proxy: to predict the next token well across diverse text, the model must learn grammar, facts, reasoning, and discourse structure
- **Hallucination cause**: the training objective (minimize cross-entropy on tokens) is not identical to "produce factually accurate statements" — fluent wrongness can have lower loss than hedged accuracy
- **Knowledge cutoff**: model weights are frozen; no access to post-training events without retrieval tools
- **Context window limits**: information in the center of long prompts is underutilized — "lost in the middle" effect documented empirically
- **Cost arithmetic matters**: at scale, token pricing dominates — always calculate expected monthly inference cost before committing to a model tier
- **FDE scoping questions**: what is input/output, what is cost-of-error, is there a human in the loop, what does the baseline process cost?
- **Stack position**: Entry builds on models, Mid designs systems, FDE deploys and adapts for customer contexts — all require understanding every layer
- **RAG**: the primary mitigation for hallucination and knowledge cutoff in production — retrieves source documents and provides them as context for grounded generation

## Interview Questions

### Entry Level {.callout-tip}

::: {.callout-tip title="Entry Level"}

**Q1. What is generative AI and how does it differ from traditional machine learning?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Traditional machine learning models are predominantly discriminative — they learn to map inputs to outputs by learning a conditional probability $P(y \mid x)$. A spam classifier outputs a label. A sentiment model outputs positive/negative. These models only need to learn where the boundary between classes lies; they never need to understand what "typical spam" looks like in full.

Generative AI models learn the underlying distribution of the data itself — $P(x)$ or $P(x, y)$ — and can sample new examples from that distribution. A generative text model has learned what text looks like at a statistical level, and can produce new text by sampling from that learned distribution.

The practical distinction: discriminative models consume data and produce labels or predictions; generative models consume a specification, partial input, or prompt and produce new data — text, images, audio, or code. ResNet is discriminative (classifies images); DALL-E is generative (creates images). BERT is discriminative (classifies text); GPT-4 is generative (produces text). The key insight for interviews: generative models are not magic — they are sophisticated statistical models that assign probabilities to sequences and can be sampled to produce new sequences.
:::

**Q2. Name three real-world applications of generative AI and the type of model behind each.**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Code completion** (GitHub Copilot, Cursor): powered by large causal language models (decoder-only transformers). These models are trained on billions of lines of code and predict the next token in a code context, manifesting as full function completions and docstring generation.

**Text-to-image generation** (DALL-E 3, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion): uses diffusion models — a class of generative model that learns to reverse a gradual noising process. The model starts with random noise and iteratively denoises it, conditioned on a text prompt, until a coherent image emerges.

**Enterprise document summarization / chat** (Glean, Notion AI, custom RAG systems): uses large decoder-only language models augmented with retrieval. The model reads retrieved document chunks and synthesizes a coherent response. Understanding that different applications often combine multiple model types (a language model for generation, an embedding model for retrieval, a vision model for images) is important for mid-level and FDE candidates.
:::

**Q3. What is a foundation model and what distinguishes it from a task-specific ML model?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
A foundation model is a large-scale model pretrained on broad, diverse data that can be adapted to many downstream tasks. Contrast with task-specific models trained from scratch on labeled data for one narrow problem (a spam classifier, a sentiment detector).

Three key distinctions — scope, training cost, and adaptation mechanism. A task-specific ResNet for X-ray classification is trained entirely on X-ray images with labels and has no knowledge of anything else. GPT-4 was trained on essentially all digitized human knowledge, giving it broad capabilities across language, reasoning, and code.

The adaptation mechanism is also fundamentally different. Task-specific models require labeled data and retraining for each new task. Foundation models can be adapted through prompting (zero-shot or few-shot examples in context), fine-tuning on a small labeled dataset, or retrieval-augmentation — all far cheaper than retraining. The practical implication for any GenAI role: you are almost always building on top of a foundation model, not training from scratch.
:::

**Q4. What was the significance of the Transformer paper (2017)?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Vaswani et al.'s "Attention Is All You Need" (2017) introduced the transformer architecture, which replaced recurrent neural networks as the dominant architecture for sequence modeling. The core innovation was self-attention: instead of processing sequences one token at a time (as RNNs do), every token in the sequence attends to every other token simultaneously using learned attention weights. This eliminated the sequential dependency in RNNs that prevented parallelization.

The practical consequence was enormous: transformer training could be parallelized across the entire sequence length, making it feasible to train on orders of magnitude more data using GPU clusters. RNNs also struggled with long-range dependencies — gradients vanished over long sequences — while transformers handle long-range context through direct attention connections between any two positions.

The 2017 paper is effectively the inflection point of the modern AI era: every major LLM in production today is a descendant of that architecture.
:::

**Q5. What is "emergent behavior" in LLMs and give an example?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Emergent behavior in LLMs refers to capabilities that appear suddenly and unpredictably at certain scales — capabilities that were not present in smaller models and were not explicitly trained for. Wei et al.'s 2022 paper "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models" documented dozens of tasks where performance was near-random below a threshold scale and jumped to near-human performance above it.

The canonical example is multi-step arithmetic. Below approximately 10 billion parameters, LLMs perform multi-step math at chance level. Above roughly 100 billion parameters, the same models suddenly solve them reliably. No training objective explicitly taught arithmetic — it emerged from sufficient exposure to mathematical text and reasoning patterns at sufficient scale.

For practitioners, the practical implication is critical: you cannot reliably predict which capabilities a model will have by extrapolating from smaller models. Capability evaluation on actual target tasks is mandatory before deployment.
:::

**Q6. List four major limitations of LLMs in production.**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Hallucination**: LLMs generate fluent, confident, factually incorrect output. The training objective (minimize cross-entropy on tokens) does not penalize factual errors directly — plausible-sounding wrong answers can have lower loss than hedged accurate ones. Mitigation: RAG, human review loops.

**Knowledge cutoff**: Model weights are frozen at training time. Events after the cutoff date are unknown unless provided in context. Mitigation: retrieval-augmented generation, tool use.

**Context window limits**: Even 1M-token context windows (Gemini 1.5 Pro) face the "lost in the middle" effect — models perform best on information at the beginning and end of context, and worse on information in the middle. Mitigation: smart chunking and retrieval strategies.

**Cost and latency**: Large models are expensive ($5-15/million output tokens for GPT-4 class) and have meaningful latency (1-3 seconds time-to-first-token). At production scale, this dominates cost structure. Mitigation: model routing, smaller models for simple tasks, caching.
:::

### Mid Level {.callout-warning}

::: {.callout-warning title="Mid Level"}

**Q7. Explain the difference between generative and discriminative models using probability notation.**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
A discriminative model directly models the conditional probability $P(y \mid x)$ — the probability of a label $y$ given observed features $x$. It learns the boundary between classes without needing to model what typical examples of each class look like. Logistic regression, SVMs, BERT fine-tuned for classification, and ResNet all fall here.

A generative model models the joint probability $P(x, y)$ or, in the unsupervised case, the marginal $P(x)$. From the joint, you can derive $P(y \mid x) = P(x, y) / P(x)$ via Bayes' theorem — so generative models can technically classify, but their primary capability is sampling: given the learned distribution $P(x)$, draw new samples $x^* \sim P(x)$ that look like training data.

GPT-style models are trained to maximize $\sum_t \log P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, which is equivalent to modeling $P(x)$ over sequences via the chain rule: $P(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. The interviewer distinction to nail: discriminative models are often more accurate for their specific task, but they cannot produce new data; generative models can produce new data at the cost of modeling a harder, higher-dimensional distribution.
:::

**Q8. What are the three technical developments that made the current LLM wave possible, and why did they need to converge?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
The three developments are: (1) the transformer architecture (2017), enabling parallelizable training on long sequences; (2) GPU-scale compute, specifically large GPU clusters with high-bandwidth interconnects enabling training runs of hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours; and (3) web-scale text data, including Common Crawl, GitHub, curated corpora like The Pile and RedPajama.

Each was necessary but insufficient alone. Transformers without compute produce small models with modest capabilities — GPT-1 (2018) had 117M parameters. Compute without transformers would have just scaled slow, hard-to-parallelize RNNs that plateau in quality because of gradient issues over long sequences. Data without architecture and compute produces an untrainable dataset.

The 2020 GPT-3 paper was the first demonstration of all three converging: 175B parameters trained on ~300B tokens produced emergent capabilities including few-shot learning that were qualitatively absent in smaller models. Common Crawl had been accumulating since 2008; GPU hardware hit the necessary performance/cost ratio around 2018-2020; the transformer arrived in 2017. Before 2017, the data was there but the architecture and hardware weren't. By 2020, all three were available simultaneously.
:::

**Q9. What does it mean when we say an LLM "predicts the next token"? How does that mechanism lead to seemingly intelligent outputs?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
At each inference step, an LLM takes the sequence of tokens processed so far and produces a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary (typically 50,000-100,000 tokens). The "prediction" is this distribution: each token gets a probability, and together they sum to 1. The training objective: maximize the probability assigned to the actual next token in the training corpus, equivalent to minimizing cross-entropy loss.

To predict the next token accurately across a diverse corpus — news articles, scientific papers, code, fiction, legal documents — a model must implicitly learn the grammar, vocabulary, facts, reasoning patterns, and discourse structure of every domain in its training data. A model that accurately predicts the next token in a physics textbook has necessarily learned something about physics.

Next-token prediction is a deceptively general proxy task. Unlike supervised tasks that teach a specific mapping, predicting text requires learning virtually everything that makes text coherent. The "intelligence" emerges from the pressure to minimize prediction loss across a distribution so broad that the only way to succeed is to internalize deep regularities about language, knowledge, and reasoning. This is also why the model can be wrong: the objective is probabilistic accuracy, not factual correctness, and the model will confidently predict a plausible-sounding but incorrect token if that is what the training distribution supports.
:::

**Q10. Explain in-context learning — why can an LLM learn from examples in the prompt without gradient updates?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
In-context learning (ICL) refers to the ability of large language models to perform new tasks when given a few input-output examples in the prompt, without any change to the model's weights. You show the model three sentiment examples and it classifies the fourth — no fine-tuning, no gradient descent.

The leading mechanistic explanation (Akyürek et al., 2022; von Oswald et al., 2023) is that ICL implements a form of implicit gradient descent within the forward pass. The attention mechanism in transformers can, in principle, compute updates to internal representations that simulate what gradient descent on the examples would produce, using only forward-pass computation. The key evidence: ICL performance scales with model size and training diversity, and models make errors consistent with what a gradient-descent learner would predict.

A more intuitive framing: pretraining on diverse text exposed the model to millions of task demonstrations. When you provide examples in the prompt, the model recognizes the pattern ("this is a classification task where the format is input → label") and retrieves the relevant computation from its weights. ICL works better for task formats that appeared frequently in pretraining data and degrades for unusual or novel task structures. The practical implication: few-shot examples in the prompt are often the most effective and cheapest way to adapt model behavior, and example quality and selection strategy (cover edge cases, use diverse examples) matters significantly.
:::

**Q11. Compare RAG vs. fine-tuning for adapting a foundation model to a new domain. When would you use each?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) augments the model at inference time by retrieving relevant documents and inserting them into the context window. The model weights are unchanged. Fine-tuning updates the model weights on domain-specific data, changing how the model generates text even without retrieval context.

Use RAG when: your domain knowledge is dynamic (updated frequently), you need citations and source attribution, you have a large corpus of proprietary documents, you want to minimize cost and infrastructure, or you need fast iteration without model retraining. RAG handles factual knowledge that changes over time naturally — you just update the index, not the model.

Use fine-tuning when: you need a specific response format or style the base model doesn't follow, you're adapting to a specialized domain where the base model has weak priors (e.g., medical coding, proprietary technical jargon), you want to make the model more efficient on a narrow task (smaller model with fine-tuning can match larger model on specific tasks), or you need consistent behavioral changes that prompting alone can't achieve reliably.

In practice, the best production systems often combine both: fine-tune the model on domain data for style and format consistency, then use RAG to ground factual claims in live retrieval. The critical mistake to avoid: reaching for fine-tuning first when RAG would solve the problem with a fraction of the effort and no model maintenance overhead.
:::

**Q12. A customer's CEO asks: "How does ChatGPT actually work?" Give a 2-minute explanation for a non-technical executive.**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
"Think about the autocomplete on your phone — it suggests the next word as you type, based on what words typically follow what you've already written. ChatGPT does essentially the same thing, but at a scale that's hard to imagine. It was trained on roughly a trillion words of text from the internet, books, code, and academic papers — more than any human could read in thousands of lifetimes. In doing so, it learned the patterns of how language works across every topic covered in that data.

When you ask it a question, it's not looking up your answer in a database the way Google does. It's generating an answer word-by-word, picking what word is most likely to come next given everything you've said and everything it learned. Because it learned from so much text on so many topics, those generated words turn into surprisingly accurate, coherent answers.

The limitation is exactly what you'd expect: it learned from text, not from verified facts. So it can write confidently even when it's wrong — like a very eloquent person who sometimes misremembers things. It also has no live information unless you give it access to external tools. For your business, this means it's extraordinarily powerful for tasks where you need to generate, summarize, or transform text — but for tasks where precision matters, we build guardrails and verification steps into the workflow."

The key elements: intuitive analogy, honest about limitations, tied to business implications.

**Q13. A skeptical customer says "AI just makes stuff up — why should we trust it for our business?" How do you respond?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
I validate the concern before addressing it — dismissing it signals naivety. "You're absolutely right that hallucination is a real and documented problem. I wouldn't recommend deploying AI in any business-critical context without explicitly accounting for it. The question isn't whether AI can be wrong — it can — but whether we can engineer a system where the failure rate and failure mode are acceptable for your specific use case."

Then I distinguish use cases: for tasks where the AI summarizes documents the customer already owns, extracts structured data from forms, or drafts text a human will review before it goes out, the hallucination risk is manageable with humans in the loop for high-stakes decisions. For fully autonomous high-stakes decisions (patient diagnoses, financial trades), the bar is much higher and requires different architecture.

Then I explain the architectural mitigations: retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds the model's answers in retrieved source documents, dramatically reducing hallucination on factual queries; fine-tuning on domain-specific data shifts behavior toward their domain; confidence scoring and abstention mechanisms flag uncertain responses for human review; evaluation pipelines measure hallucination rates on representative samples before deployment.

I close with: "The companies using AI successfully in production aren't trusting it blindly — they're engineering systems that use AI for what it's genuinely good at while keeping humans responsible for what it isn't. Let me show you how that looks for your use case specifically."
:::

**Q14. You're scoping a GenAI project for a customer. What questions do you ask in the first 30 minutes to determine if GenAI is even the right tool?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
I start by asking what the customer is actually trying to achieve and what their current process looks like — not what AI solution they want, because customers often arrive with a solution before the problem is properly defined. Specifically:

**Problem definition**: What is the input and what is the desired output? Is the output language-based, structured data, images, or decisions? Is the task generative (creating new content), extractive (finding or summarizing existing content), or classificatory (routing or labeling)?

**Quality and cost-of-error**: What happens if the system produces a wrong answer? Is there a human in the loop for review, or does output go directly to customers or downstream automated systems? High-stakes, low-review workflows demand very different architecture than internal productivity tools.

**Data inventory**: What data do you have that's relevant to this task? Is it structured or unstructured? Is there labeled ground truth, enabling evaluation? How frequently does it change?

**Baseline economics**: Is there a current manual process? What does it cost in time and headcount? This sizes the value of automation and establishes the quality bar (the AI must be better or cheaper than the current approach to be worth deploying).

**Task type test**: Is this actually a language problem? If the customer wants to predict numerical outcomes from structured tabular data, a classical ML model (XGBoost, logistic regression) will almost certainly outperform an LLM and cost a fraction of the price. GenAI is the right tool when input or output is language, or when the task requires broad world knowledge that would be expensive to encode in a rules-based system.
:::

**Q15. How do you decide whether a GenAI solution is even the right tool for a given problem?**

> **What great looks like:** A senior candidate doesn't just know GenAI — they have a framework for when *not* to use it. They compare GenAI against classical ML, deterministic rules, and manual processes. They reason about the cost-of-error for the specific use case, not generically. They identify when the "AI solution" a customer wants is actually a data pipeline problem, a rules problem, or a product design problem.

> **Common Mistake:** Answering this question by listing GenAI capabilities (what it can do) instead of the decision framework (when to use it vs. alternatives). Interviewers are testing your judgment as a practitioner, not your ability to recite model capabilities.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
My framework is a four-part decision tree:

**1. Is the input/output language or media?** If yes, GenAI is potentially appropriate. If no (e.g., predicting churn from structured user data, forecasting sales from time series), classical ML is almost always cheaper, faster, and more accurate for that specific task. Don't shoehorn an LLM into a tabular regression problem.

**2. What is the cost-of-error and review structure?** GenAI with a human reviewer in the loop can operate at higher error rates than autonomous GenAI. A system that drafts emails for human review tolerates 5% hallucination rate. A system that auto-sends customer communications cannot. Error tolerance determines whether you need a 70B+ model, a smaller fine-tuned model, or RAG grounding — and whether GenAI is appropriate at all.

**3. What is the baseline?** If the current process is manual and slow, GenAI's 80% accuracy at 100× speed might be a massive win. If there is an existing rule-based system with 99.5% accuracy, GenAI's 95% accuracy is a regression. Always establish the baseline before proposing GenAI.

**4. Is the knowledge dynamic?** If the task requires up-to-date factual knowledge (current prices, live inventory, recent news), a static model without retrieval is likely to fail in production. RAG or tool-use architecture is required.

If all four checks pass: GenAI is likely appropriate. Proceed to choosing between RAG, fine-tuning, prompting, or a combination based on the data and quality requirements.
:::

**Q16. A non-technical executive asks "why can't we just use GPT-3.5 instead of GPT-4 for this use case?"**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
I frame the answer around capability, reliability, and cost — not version numbers. "Both are language models from OpenAI, but they differ substantially in what they can reliably do. Think of GPT-3.5 as a capable junior analyst who can draft text and answer questions reasonably well, but struggles with complex multi-step tasks, makes logical errors on harder problems, and often misses nuanced instructions. GPT-4 is more like a senior analyst — it follows complex instructions more reliably, reasons through multi-step problems more accurately, and makes significantly fewer errors on tasks requiring judgment."

The concrete differences: GPT-4 shows roughly 40% fewer factual errors on knowledge benchmarks, handles longer and more complex instructions without losing track of requirements, performs significantly better on reasoning-intensive tasks (coding, math, legal analysis), and supports multimodal inputs (images). For simple tasks — summarizing a short document, classifying sentiment, extracting a structured field — GPT-3.5-class models may be entirely sufficient at a fraction of the cost.

The honest business framing: GPT-3.5 costs roughly 10-20× less than GPT-4 per token. If the use case is high-volume simple-task processing where a 5% error rate is acceptable, GPT-3.5 might be the right economic choice. If the customer is using AI to support high-stakes decisions or customer-facing responses where errors damage trust, GPT-4's reliability improvement pays for itself quickly. The correct answer is: "Let's benchmark both models on a representative sample of your actual data and measure error rates — then the cost decision is clear."
