14  PEFT, LoRA & QLoRA

Note

Who this chapter is for: Entry / Mid / Senior / FDE

What you’ll be able to answer after reading this:

  • Why Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) exists and the exact memory arithmetic that motivates it
  • How LoRA works mathematically — the low-rank decomposition, the scaling factor, and the initialization trick
  • How to choose rank, alpha, dropout, and target modules for any task
  • What QLoRA adds (NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers) and why it matters
  • How to compare PEFT methods: LoRA vs. adapters vs. prefix tuning vs. DoRA vs. IA3
  • How to merge adapters, hot-swap them, and design multi-tenant serving architectures
  • Memory back-of-envelope calculations for 7B, 13B, and 70B models

14.1 Why Full Fine-Tuning Isn’t Always Practical

Full fine-tuning is the gold standard: update every weight in the model on your task-specific data, no compromises. The problem is arithmetic.

For a model with \(N\) parameters trained with the Adam optimizer at FP16/FP32 mixed precision:

Memory Component Size
Model weights (FP16) \(2N\) bytes
Gradient copies (FP16) \(2N\) bytes
Adam first moment — FP32 \(4N\) bytes
Adam second moment — FP32 \(4N\) bytes
Total (minimum, no activations) \(\approx 12N\) bytes

For a 7B parameter model: \(12 \times 7 \times 10^9 \approx 84\text{ GB}\) — before a single activation is stored. Add activations (which scale with batch size × sequence length × hidden dimension) and you are looking at 100–130 GB for a reasonable batch size. That means at minimum two A100 80GB GPUs with tensor parallelism, just to run one training step.

Scale to 13B: ~160 GB minimum. Scale to 70B: ~840 GB — roughly ten A100s. Most teams do not have this.

The storage problem compounds the compute problem. If you need one fine-tuned model per customer, per language, or per product line, full fine-tuning produces one complete model copy per variant. A 7B FP16 model is ~14 GB on disk. One hundred customer-specific variants: 1.4 TB of model storage, separate infrastructure to serve each. Neither option is operationally or financially sane at scale.

These two forces — training memory and deployment storage — are exactly why PEFT methods exist: train less than 1% of the parameters, get comparable quality, pay megabytes not gigabytes per variant.

Tip

Analogy. Full fine-tuning rewrites the entire textbook for a new course. PEFT is inserting sticky notes on the relevant pages: the book stays unchanged, you only manage the notes. At deployment, you can either leave the sticky notes in (unmerged adapter) or copy their content into the margins (merged model) — either way, the book itself is intact.

14.2 The PEFT Family: What Your Options Are

PEFT is not a single method. It is a family of approaches, each with different parameter efficiency, quality, and inference overhead tradeoffs.

Method Trainable Params Inference Overhead Quality vs. Full FT Memory Reduction Best For
LoRA 0.1–1% None (after merge) Within 1–3% 60–80% General-purpose fine-tuning; multi-tenant serving
QLoRA 0.1–1% None (after merge) Within 1–2% of LoRA 80–90%+ Large models (30B+) on single GPUs
Adapter Layers 1–5% +5–15% latency per layer Within 1–3% 50–70% Multi-task, task-switching at inference
Prefix Tuning <0.1% Sequence length overhead 2–5% gap at small scale 70–80% Frozen model deployment, low-resource
Prompt Tuning <0.01% None Poor at <10B params 90%+ Very large models only (100B+)
DoRA 0.1–1% None (after merge) Marginally better than LoRA 60–80% LoRA-hard tasks; fine control of direction vs. magnitude
IA3 <0.01% None Slightly below LoRA 95%+ Extreme parameter budget; fast deployment

LoRA has won in practice. The combination of strong quality, no inference overhead after merging, small adapter files, and ecosystem support (Hugging Face PEFT, vLLM, TGI, LoRAX, S-LoRA) makes it the default choice. The other methods are worth knowing for interviews and specific edge cases, but LoRA is what you will encounter in production.

Warning

Common Misconception: “Prefix tuning is just as good as LoRA.” Prefix tuning prepends trainable virtual tokens to every layer’s key-value sequence. It works reasonably well at very large scale (100B+ parameters) but significantly underperforms LoRA on models below 10B. If someone asks why LoRA became dominant over prefix tuning, the answer is empirical: LoRA is consistently better at the model sizes most teams actually use.

14.3 LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation

14.3.1 The Core Mathematical Insight

LoRA begins with an observation from the optimization literature: when you fine-tune a pretrained model, the weight update \(\Delta W\) has low intrinsic rank. The important variation in the update lies in a small subspace, not spread across the full dimensionality of the weight matrix.

Formally, instead of learning a full update \(\Delta W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\) (which requires \(d \times k\) parameters), LoRA parameterizes it as:

\[\Delta W = BA\]

where \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) and \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\), with \(r \ll \min(d, k)\).

The adapted forward pass becomes:

\[h = Wx + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot BAx\]

where \(W\) is frozen and only \(B\) and \(A\) receive gradient updates during training.

At inference time, if you have a single adapter, you merge:

\[W_{\text{final}} = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot BA\]

The merged model is a standard weight matrix — structurally identical to the original, with zero inference overhead.

Parameter count comparison. For a weight matrix of shape \(d \times k\) (e.g., \(4096 \times 4096\) in a 7B model):

  • Full fine-tuning: \(d \times k = 16{,}777{,}216\) parameters per matrix
  • LoRA with \(r=16\): \(r(d + k) = 16 \times 8192 = 131{,}072\) parameters per matrix
  • Reduction: 128× for that matrix, or about 0.78% of the original count
Tip

Why does low rank work? Think of fine-tuning as teaching a student who already knows most of what they need to know. You don’t need to rewrite their entire mental model — you need to shift a few key conceptual directions. Low-rank decomposition captures those few directions explicitly. The regularization effect is a bonus: constraining updates to a low-rank subspace prevents the model from chasing noise in the fine-tuning data.

14.3.2 The Scaling Factor Alpha

The \(\frac{\alpha}{r}\) scaling factor controls how much the adapter influences the output relative to the frozen base weights. This deserves careful attention.

If \(\alpha = r\), the scaling factor is \(1.0\) — the adapter contributes at the same effective magnitude as the base weights. If \(\alpha = 2r\), the scaling is \(2.0\). The conventional default is \(\alpha = 2r\) (e.g., \(r=16, \alpha=32\)), which has been empirically validated across many tasks.

Why not just absorb the scaling into the learning rate? Two reasons:

  1. Hyperparameter transfer across ranks: If you hold \(\alpha\) fixed and change \(r\), the effective learning rate of the adapter changes proportionally. If you hold \(\alpha/r\) fixed (e.g., always set \(\alpha = 2r\)), you can change \(r\) without retuning your learning rate. This makes rank ablations cleaner.
  2. Initialization stability: \(B\) is initialized from a Gaussian; \(A\) is initialized to zero, so \(\Delta W = BA = 0\) at the start of training. The model begins from exact pretrained behavior. The \(\alpha/r\) scaling does not affect this property — it only affects how quickly the adapter grows during training.
Warning

Common Misconception: “Alpha is just a second learning rate — set it and forget it.” Alpha interacts with both the learning rate and the rank. A run with \(r=8, \alpha=16\) has the same scaling (\(\alpha/r = 2\)) as \(r=32, \alpha=64\), but very different parameter counts and effective capacity. When comparing LoRA configurations, always report both \(r\) and \(\alpha\) — reporting only one is ambiguous.

14.3.3 The Initialization Trick

This detail is often skipped but is mechanically important:

  • A is initialized from \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) (small random values)
  • B is initialized to zero

Result: \(\Delta W = BA = \mathbf{0}\) at training step zero. The model starts from exactly the pretrained checkpoint’s behavior. Without this, random initialization of both A and B would introduce a random perturbation to every adapted weight matrix before training even begins, potentially disrupting the base model’s capabilities. The zero initialization of B is what makes LoRA safe to add to any pretrained model without degrading it before training starts.

14.3.4 Where to Inject Adapters

LoRA adapters can in principle be applied to any weight matrix. In practice, the question is which layers matter most for the target task.

Attention projection matrices are the standard target. In a multi-head attention layer, the four matrices are: - \(W_Q\) (query projection) — determines what the model “searches for” - \(W_K\) (key projection) — determines what the model “offers to be found” - \(W_V\) (value projection) — determines what information gets aggregated - \(W_O\) (output projection) — mixes head outputs back into the residual stream

The original LoRA paper targeted only \(W_Q\) and \(W_V\), finding that \(W_K\) added little. Subsequent work established that adding all four attention projections reliably improves quality for most tasks.

MLP layers (the two feedforward linear projections per transformer block, typically named gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj in LLaMA-family models) store factual knowledge. For tasks requiring new factual associations, domain-specific knowledge, or complex multi-step reasoning, including MLP layers in the target set meaningfully improves quality. For simple format or style adaptation, attention projections alone are usually sufficient.

Priority order for target module selection:

  1. Minimum viable: q_proj, v_proj
  2. Standard: q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj
  3. Comprehensive: all attention projections + gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj

Start at level 2. If quality is insufficient at your chosen rank, expand to level 3 before increasing rank — it often helps more and costs less.

14.4 Choosing LoRA Hyperparameters

14.4.1 Rank Selection Guidance

Use Case Recommended Rank Alpha Notes
Simple format/style change 4–8 8–16 Low-rank sufficient; smaller adapter
Single-task instruction following 8–16 16–32 Default starting point
Complex domain adaptation 16–32 32–64 Start at 16, increase if validation plateaus
Multi-step reasoning fine-tuning 32–64 64–128 Add MLP layers to targets
Code generation with new APIs 16–32 32–64 MLP layers critical
Very small dataset (<500 examples) 4–8 8–16 Low rank = less overfitting
Large diverse dataset (>50K examples) 32–64 64–128 Higher rank can generalize

The rank ablation process:

  1. Start at \(r=16\), \(\alpha=32\), targeting all attention projections
  2. Train and measure validation quality
  3. Try \(r=8\) — if validation quality is similar, use \(r=8\) (fewer params, less overfitting risk)
  4. Try \(r=32\) — if validation quality improves materially, consider \(r=32\)
  5. If \(r=32\) still insufficient: expand target modules to include MLP layers first, then try \(r=64\)
  6. Do not use \(r=128+\) without empirical evidence — rarely helps over \(r=64\)
Warning

Production Gotcha: High rank without dropout on small datasets. Using \(r=128\) on a dataset of 500 examples is a common way to overfit catastrophically. The adapter has 128 dimensions of freedom per weight matrix — far more than the dataset can constrain. If you need high expressivity on a small dataset, use \(r=16\) with LoRA dropout of \(0.1\)–\(0.15\), not \(r=128\) with no dropout.

14.4.2 Full Hyperparameter Reference

lora_config = LoraConfig(
    r=16,                 # Rank: start here. Higher = more expressive, more params.
    lora_alpha=32,        # Alpha: keep alpha = 2*r as default. Scaling = alpha/r = 2.0
    target_modules=[      # Start with attention, add MLP for hard tasks
        "q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj",    # Attention projections
        "gate_proj", "up_proj", "down_proj",        # MLP projections (LLaMA family)
    ],
    lora_dropout=0.05,    # 0.05-0.1 for regularization; 0.0 for large datasets
    bias="none",          # "none" is fine; "all" or "lora_only" rarely helps
    task_type=TaskType.CAUSAL_LM,
)

These are approximate figures including model weights, optimizer states, and gradients, but excluding activation memory (which scales with batch size and sequence length). LoRA figures assume full-precision frozen base weights with only adapter parameters in the optimizer.

Tip

Back-of-envelope for LoRA memory. Frozen base model at FP16: \(2N\) bytes. LoRA trainable params \(\approx 0.5–1\%\) of \(N\), call it \(P\). Adam optimizer for those params in FP32: \(8P\) bytes. Total \(\approx 2N + 8P \approx 2N\) (since \(P \ll N\)). For a 7B model: \(2 \times 7 \times 10^9 \approx 14\) GB weights + a few GB for adapter optimizer states + activations. The base model dominates.

14.6 QLoRA: Fine-Tune 70B on One GPU

QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) stacks three independent innovations on top of LoRA. Each one reduces memory usage. Together they make 70B fine-tuning on a single A100 80GB practical.

14.6.1 Innovation 1: NF4 (Normal Float 4-bit) Quantization

Standard 4-bit integer quantization divides the weight range into 16 equally spaced buckets. The problem: transformer weights are not uniformly distributed — they follow an approximately normal (bell-shaped) distribution. Uniform quantization wastes precision in the tails and crowds it in the center.

NF4 fixes this by placing the 16 quantization levels at positions corresponding to equal-area quantiles of the standard normal distribution. Each level covers the same probability mass, so the expected quantization error is minimized for normally-distributed values.

Result: Base model weights stored at 0.5 bytes per parameter (4 bits) instead of 2 bytes (FP16). A 70B model’s weights shrink from 140 GB to approximately 35 GB. Computation happens in BF16 (the weights are dequantized on-the-fly during the forward pass), so numerical precision during computation is maintained.

The dequantization overhead is real: QLoRA forward passes run approximately 10–20% slower than pure FP16. For training where the bottleneck is often data loading and optimizer steps, this is acceptable.

14.6.2 Innovation 2: Double Quantization

When you quantize a block of weights to NF4, you need to store a scaling constant per block to enable dequantization. These constants are in FP32. For a 70B model with block size 64, there are \(\approx 70B / 64 \approx 1.1\) billion constants — about 4 GB at FP32.

Double quantization applies a second quantization pass to these constants, from FP32 to 8-bit float. This reduces constant storage from ~4 GB to ~0.5 GB — a 3.5 GB saving. Modest in absolute terms, but meaningful when fitting a 70B model into 80 GB.

14.6.3 Innovation 3: Paged Optimizers

Adam optimizer states (first and second moment vectors) for the LoRA adapter parameters are stored in GPU VRAM during training. If peak memory demand (model + activations + optimizer) exceeds available VRAM, standard training crashes with an OOM error.

Paged optimizers register Adam states as CUDA unified memory — memory that the CUDA driver can automatically page to CPU RAM when the GPU is under pressure. When the page-out happens, subsequent accesses trigger a PCIe transfer back to GPU. This adds latency proportional to how often paging occurs, but it converts hard crashes into graceful slowdowns.

In practice, paged optimizers rarely trigger on well-configured training runs. Their value is as a safety margin when experimenting with large batch sizes, long sequences, or gradient checkpointing settings near the VRAM limit.

14.6.4 QLoRA Quality vs. Full Fine-Tuning

QLoRA’s quality relative to full fine-tuning is the critical question for practitioners.

The original QLoRA paper demonstrated that QLoRA-trained Guanaco models achieved MMLU scores within 1–2% of ChatGPT at the time, despite training on a single GPU. Subsequent replication studies across multiple model families (LLaMA, Mistral, Falcon) consistently showed:

  • QLoRA ≈ LoRA at FP16 (within ~0.5–1% on most benchmarks)
  • LoRA at FP16 ≈ full fine-tuning (within ~1–3% at rank 16–32)
  • Therefore, QLoRA is within ~1–3% of full fine-tuning quality on most standard tasks

When QLoRA quality may not be sufficient: tasks requiring precise numerical computation, highly fine-grained factual accuracy (the quantization noise can introduce small errors), or when the base model itself is already highly optimized and the fine-tuning dataset is very small (where quantization noise matters more relative to the signal).

Warning

Production Gotcha: Merging QLoRA adapters. QLoRA trains on a 4-bit quantized base model. The adapter learns to compensate for the quantization noise in that specific base. Do not merge the adapter into the 4-bit model. The correct deployment path is: (1) load the base model fresh at FP16/BF16, (2) merge the adapter with merge_and_unload(), (3) optionally re-quantize the merged FP16 model for deployment using GPTQ or AWQ. Merging into the 4-bit base reintroduces quantization error that the adapter did not train against.

14.7 Merging, Serving, and Multi-Adapter Architecture

14.7.1 When to Merge vs. Keep Separate

Scenario Recommendation Reason
Single adapter, latency-sensitive serving Merge Zero inference overhead; use any inference framework
Multiple adapters, one base model, dynamic traffic Keep separate Hot-swap with LoRAX or S-LoRA; base model in memory once
Customer-specific models at scale (>10 tenants) Keep separate + adapter registry 30–100 MB per adapter vs. 14 GB+ per full model
Offline batch inference Merge Simplest; no runtime adapter management
A/B testing different adapters Keep separate Swap at request time; no redeployment
Continual learning / adapter updates Keep separate Update only the adapter, not the base model

14.7.2 The Merger Operation

\[W_{\text{final}} = W_{\text{original}} + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot B \cdot A\]

This matrix addition is performed for every adapted weight matrix. The result is structurally a standard weight matrix — indistinguishable from a model that was fully fine-tuned to those weight values. Inference speed, memory footprint, and compatibility with inference frameworks are identical to the original base model.

# Merge adapter into base model for deployment
merged_model = peft_model.merge_and_unload()
merged_model.save_pretrained("./deployed-model")
tokenizer.save_pretrained("./deployed-model")

# Load adapter separately (multi-tenant hot-swap pattern)
from peft import PeftModel
base_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B")
tenant_a_model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base_model, "./adapters/tenant-a/")
tenant_b_model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base_model, "./adapters/tenant-b/")

14.7.3 Multi-LoRA Serving

vLLM natively supports LoRA hot-swapping — you load the base model once and specify per-request which adapter to use via the lora_request parameter. S-LoRA (academic) and LoRAX (Predibase) demonstrate batching requests across different adapters, amortizing base model compute. At scale:

  • One A100 80GB serves a 7B base model
  • 100 tenant adapters at ~30 MB each = ~3 GB total adapter storage in CPU RAM
  • At request time: identify tenant → load adapter → forward pass
  • Throughput: comparable to single-model serving for mixed-tenant traffic

Storage economics. 100 tenants with full fine-tuning: \(100 \times 14\text{ GB} = 1.4\text{ TB}\) of model storage. 100 tenants with LoRA: \(14\text{ GB base} + 100 \times 30\text{ MB} \approx 17\text{ GB total}\). The economics are not close.

14.8 Advanced PEFT Methods

14.8.1 DoRA: Weight-Decomposed LoRA

DoRA decomposes each weight matrix \(W\) into a magnitude component \(m\) (a scalar per column) and a direction component \(V\) (the normalized weight matrix):

\[W = m \cdot \frac{V}{\|V\|}\]

During fine-tuning, DoRA trains the magnitude component directly and applies LoRA to the direction component. This separation allows the model to independently control “how much” vs. “in which direction” each weight column moves.

When DoRA outperforms LoRA: tasks where the fine-tuning data requires significant changes to the scale of weight activations (e.g., adapting a model trained mostly on short outputs to produce very long, structured outputs). In these cases, the pure directional adaptation of LoRA is insufficient, and DoRA’s independent magnitude control helps. The improvements are marginal (1–3%) on standard benchmarks but more pronounced on specific transfer tasks with large distributional shift.

When DoRA is not worth the overhead: most standard fine-tuning tasks. DoRA adds implementation complexity and the HuggingFace PEFT implementation is somewhat slower than standard LoRA. Default to LoRA; consider DoRA only when you have empirical evidence that LoRA’s quality ceiling is too low for your specific task.

14.8.2 IA3: Infused Adapter by Inhibiting and Amplifying Inner Activations

IA3 introduces learned scaling vectors for key, value, and feedforward activations. Instead of adding a low-rank matrix update to weights, it multiplies activations by a learned vector \(l\):

\[h' = l \odot h\]

This is extremely parameter-efficient — typically less than 0.01% of the base model’s parameters. The tradeoff is quality: IA3 consistently underperforms LoRA on complex tasks, though it approaches LoRA quality on simpler tasks.

When to consider IA3: extreme parameter budget constraints (e.g., serving on edge hardware where even 30 MB adapters matter), or rapid prototyping where training speed matters more than peak quality.

14.9 Complete Python Code: LoRA and QLoRA Training

14.9.1 Full QLoRA Setup with PEFT + TRL

#| label: qlora-full-example
#| eval: false

from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model, TaskType, PeftModel
from transformers import (
    AutoModelForCausalLM,
    AutoTokenizer,
    BitsAndBytesConfig,
    TrainingArguments,
)
from trl import SFTTrainer, SFTConfig
import torch

# -------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load base model in 4-bit (QLoRA)
# -------------------------------------------------------
bnb_config = BitsAndBytesConfig(
    load_in_4bit=True,
    bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",             # NormalFloat4 — optimal for normal-distributed weights
    bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16, # Dequantize to BF16 for computation
    bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,        # Double quantization: saves ~0.37 bits/param extra
)

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct",   # Always prefer Instruct for chat tasks
    quantization_config=bnb_config,
    device_map="auto",                     # Auto-distribute across available GPUs + CPU
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
)
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct")
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token # Required for SFTTrainer
tokenizer.padding_side = "right"          # Pad on right for causal LM

# -------------------------------------------------------
# 2. LoRA configuration
# -------------------------------------------------------
lora_config = LoraConfig(
    task_type=TaskType.CAUSAL_LM,
    r=16,                                  # Rank: start here, tune up/down
    lora_alpha=32,                         # Alpha = 2*r → scaling factor = 2.0
    target_modules=[                       # All attention + MLP for comprehensive adaptation
        "q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj",
        "gate_proj", "up_proj", "down_proj",
    ],
    lora_dropout=0.05,                     # Regularization; increase to 0.1 for small datasets
    bias="none",                           # Don't train biases (rarely helps)
    use_rslora=False,                      # Rank-stabilized LoRA — try if training unstable
)

# Wrap model with LoRA adapters
model = get_peft_model(model, lora_config)
model.print_trainable_parameters()
# Expected output for 8B model: trainable params: ~41M || all params: ~8B || trainable%: ~0.52%

# -------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Training configuration

14.9.3 Rank-Stabilized LoRA (rsLoRA)

Standard LoRA scales the adapter output by \(\frac{\alpha}{r}\). As rank increases, the per-update magnitude grows, making high-rank training potentially unstable. rsLoRA changes the scaling to \(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{r}}\), which keeps the update magnitude stable across ranks. Use use_rslora=True in LoraConfig when training at ranks above 64 or when you observe training instability with high-rank configurations.

14.10 Production Gotchas

Warning

Gotcha 1: Fine-tuning a base model, not an instruct model. If you start from a raw base model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B) and fine-tune for a chat task, you get a task-specialized model that has never learned instruction following. The result follows the task format but lacks conversational coherence, safety behaviors, and general instruction compliance. Always start from an instruction-tuned checkpoint (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) unless you have a specific reason not to (e.g., you are doing full SFT + RLHF from scratch).

Warning

Gotcha 2: Not applying the chat template. Every instruction-tuned model has a specific chat template with special tokens (<|im_start|>, [INST], etc.). If your fine-tuning examples don’t use the same template, the adapter learns a different input format than the base model was trained with. The result: the fine-tuned model degrades on standard chat prompts because the adapter now “expects” your custom format. Fix: use tokenizer.apply_chat_template() on all training examples.

Warning

Gotcha 3: Merging into a quantized model. As noted above — merging a LoRA adapter into a 4-bit quantized model reintroduces quantization error the adapter did not train to compensate for. Always merge into FP16/BF16 base, then optionally requantize for deployment.

Warning

Gotcha 4: Saving only the adapter when you need the merged model for CI/CD. Many deployment pipelines expect a complete model directory, not a base model + adapter pair. If your CI/CD tool cannot handle PeftModel.from_pretrained(), you need to pre-merge before pushing. Document your deployment artifact format explicitly.

Warning

Gotcha 5: Using device_map="auto" during training with gradient checkpointing. device_map="auto" places different layers on different devices. Gradient checkpointing with multi-device layer placement can cause silent correctness issues in some versions of Transformers. If you see anomalous training curves with this combination, pin all layers to a single GPU or use accelerate with explicit device placement.

14.12 Interview Questions

14.12.1 🟢 Entry Level

Q1. What is PEFT and why was it developed?

Model Answer

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning is a family of techniques that adapt a pretrained model to a new task by training only a small subset of additional parameters while keeping the base model frozen. It was developed to address two practical problems:

Memory: Full fine-tuning stores model weights, optimizer states (Adam requires 2× the parameter count), and gradients simultaneously. For a 7B model this totals ~84 GB — far beyond a single GPU. PEFT reduces this by training only ~0.1–1% of parameters.

Storage and deployment: Full fine-tuning produces one complete model copy per task or customer. At 14 GB per 7B model, 100 variants requires 1.4 TB of storage and separate serving infrastructure. PEFT adapters are 10–100 MB per variant; all variants share one base model deployment.

The dominant PEFT method today is LoRA, which achieves near-full-fine-tuning quality with no inference overhead after the adapter is merged into the base model.

Weak answer: “PEFT saves memory.” (True but incomplete — misses the storage/deployment angle and the mechanism.)

Strong answer: The memory arithmetic above, plus the deployment economics, plus naming LoRA as the dominant method and why (no inference overhead).

Q2. What does LoRA stand for and what is the core idea?

Model Answer

LoRA stands for Low-Rank Adaptation. The core idea: when you fine-tune a pretrained model, the weight updates \(\Delta W\) have low intrinsic dimensionality. Rather than storing and computing a full \(d \times k\) update matrix, LoRA decomposes it as \(\Delta W = BA\) where \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) and \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\) with \(r \ll \min(d, k)\).

The parameter count for the update drops from \(d \times k\) to \(r(d + k)\). For a 4096×4096 matrix with \(r=16\): from 16.7M parameters to 131K — a 128× reduction. Only \(A\) and \(B\) receive gradient updates during training; the original \(W\) is frozen. At inference, the adapter can be merged: \(W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA\), producing a standard weight matrix with no overhead.

Weak answer: “LoRA adds small matrices to the model.” (Misses the math, the motivation, and the merge step.)

Strong answer: The decomposition math, parameter count comparison, the fact that \(W\) is frozen, and the merge operation.

Q3. How do you choose LoRA rank \(r\)?

Model Answer

Start at \(r=16\), \(\alpha=32\) (the validated default). Then run a validation sweep:

  • Try \(r=8\): if validation quality is similar, use \(r=8\) — fewer parameters, less overfitting risk, smaller adapter files.
  • Try \(r=32\): if \(r=16\) quality is insufficient, increase rank. Add MLP layers to target modules first, since that often helps more than increasing rank alone.
  • Avoid \(r > 64\) without empirical evidence it improves over \(r=32\)–\(64\).

Signal from training curves: if training loss barely decreases, rank is likely insufficient (or learning rate is too low). If training loss drops much faster than validation loss, rank is too high for the dataset size — reduce rank or add dropout.

Q4. What is the difference between LoRA and QLoRA?

Model Answer

LoRA is the adapter method: decompose weight updates as \(BA\), train only \(B\) and \(A\). It can be applied to any model loaded at any precision.

QLoRA is LoRA applied to a 4-bit quantized base model, enabling fine-tuning of large models (30B, 70B) on a single GPU. It adds three innovations: 1. NF4 quantization: stores base model weights at 4 bits using a quantization scheme optimized for normally-distributed values, reducing a 70B model from 140 GB to ~35 GB 2. Double quantization: quantizes the NF4 scaling constants themselves, saving an additional ~3–3.5 GB 3. Paged optimizers: Adam states in unified memory that can page to CPU RAM, preventing OOM crashes

Quality: QLoRA is within ~1–2% of LoRA at FP16 on most benchmarks — the quantization noise is small relative to the adapter’s corrective capacity.

Q5. What does merge_and_unload() do?

Model Answer

It performs the matrix addition \(W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA\) for every adapted weight matrix, then removes the LoRA wrapper layers. The output is a standard model object — structurally identical to the original base model — with the adapter’s learned adjustments baked in.

After merging: the model has zero inference overhead compared to an unmerged adapter, can be served by any inference framework without LoRA-specific support, and is smaller in memory (no separate \(A\), \(B\) matrices to track).

When NOT to merge: if you need multiple adapters on the same base model (multi-tenant serving), keep them separate and hot-swap at request time.

Q6. Which weight matrices does LoRA typically target and why?

Model Answer

Standard targets are the attention projection matrices: q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj. Query and value projections determine what a token attends to and what information gets aggregated — these are where task-specific adaptation most visibly occurs.

The original LoRA paper targeted only Q and V, finding K added little benefit. Current best practice is all four attention projections. For tasks requiring new factual knowledge or complex reasoning, add MLP layers (gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj in LLaMA-family models) — MLP layers store factual associations and need to adapt for domain-heavy tasks.

Priority order: q_proj+v_proj (minimum) → all attention projections (standard) → all attention + MLP (comprehensive).

14.12.2 🟡 Mid Level

Q1. Explain the LoRA scaling factor \(\alpha/r\). Why not just adjust the learning rate?

Model Answer

The \(\alpha/r\) factor scales the adapter’s contribution to the output: \(h = Wx + \frac{\alpha}{r} BAx\). Think of it as a second learning rate multiplier that applies specifically to adapter outputs relative to base model outputs.

Why not just use the learning rate? Two reasons:

  1. Rank-independent hyperparameter transfer: if you hold \(\alpha/r\) constant (e.g., always set \(\alpha = 2r\)), you can change rank without retuning learning rate — the effective scaling stays at 2.0. If you absorbed scaling into the learning rate, changing rank would change the effective step size and require retuning.

  2. Initialization correctness: \(A\) is initialized from a Gaussian; \(B\) is initialized to zero, so \(\Delta W = BA = 0\) at step 0. The scaling factor doesn’t affect this — it only influences how fast the adapter grows once training starts. The zero initialization of B is the key: it ensures the model starts from exact pretrained behavior regardless of the scaling factor.

Practical tuning: think of \(\alpha/r\) and the optimizer LR as two multiplicative controls. If adapter is under-fitting (loss not decreasing), raise LR first, then \(\alpha\). If over-adapting (forgetting base capabilities), reduce both together.

Follow-up: “What happens if you set \(\alpha = r\)?” - Scaling = 1.0. The adapter contributes at the same magnitude as the base weights at initialization of training. Valid choice; just changes convergence dynamics. Default of \(\alpha = 2r\) (scaling = 2.0) empirically trains more efficiently for most tasks.

Q2. Walk me through the memory breakdown for QLoRA fine-tuning a 7B model.

Model Answer

With QLoRA (NF4 + LoRA):

Component Size
Base model weights (NF4, 4-bit) \(0.5 \times 7B \approx 3.5\) GB
Activation buffers (NF4 → BF16 dequant) ~1–2 GB
LoRA trainable params (FP32) \(P \approx 0.01 \times 7B \times 4 \approx 280\) MB
Adam optimizer states for LoRA (FP32, 2× params) ~560 MB
Activations (batch=4, seq=2048, gradient checkpointing) ~2–4 GB
Total ~8–12 GB

This fits comfortably on a single RTX 3090 (24 GB) or A10G (24 GB).

Key point: the base model at NF4 is 3.5 GB, not 14 GB (FP16). That’s the core win. Double quantization saves another ~0.3–0.5 GB. Paged optimizers don’t reduce the baseline — they prevent OOM crashes at peak memory demand.

Without QLoRA (LoRA at FP16): - Base model at FP16: 14 GB - LoRA optimizer states: ~560 MB - Activations: ~2–4 GB - Total: ~17–20 GB — needs an A100 40GB or similar

Full fine-tuning: - Weights + optimizer + gradients: ~84 GB — needs 2+ A100 80GB

Q3. How does QLoRA achieve near-full-fine-tuning quality despite using 4-bit weights?

Model Answer

Two mechanisms work together:

NF4 minimizes quantization error: NF4 places quantization levels at equal-area quantiles of the normal distribution. Transformer weights are approximately normally distributed, so NF4 minimizes the expected quantization error for this distribution — better than uniform int4, which wastes precision in the tails.

LoRA adapter compensates for quantization noise: Even with optimal quantization, NF4 introduces small errors relative to FP16. The LoRA adapter, trained on top of the quantized model, learns to compensate for these errors. The adapter’s learned \(BA\) matrices absorb the task-specific signal plus any correction needed for quantization artifacts. This is why QLoRA quality is close to LoRA at FP16 rather than significantly worse.

The critical caveat: when merging a QLoRA-trained adapter for deployment, do NOT merge into the 4-bit base. Reload the base at FP16 and merge there. Merging into the 4-bit model loses the adapter’s quantization-error compensation.

Follow-up: “Does NF4 quantization work for all models, or only transformer language models?” NF4 is optimal for normally-distributed weights. Most large neural networks (including vision transformers, multimodal models) have approximately normal weight distributions, so NF4 works broadly. However, some specialized architectures or very small models may have non-normal weight distributions, where NF4 provides less benefit over uniform int4.

Q4. What is the difference between LoRA adapter layers and classic Houlsby adapter layers?

Model Answer

Classic Houlsby adapters (2019) insert small feedforward modules between the attention and MLP sub-layers of each transformer block. The module is typically: layer norm → down-project → non-linearity → up-project → residual. Only the adapter module parameters are trained.

Inference overhead: at inference, the adapter module adds two matrix multiplications per layer per forward pass. For a 32-layer transformer, that’s 64 extra matrix multiplications. For latency-sensitive applications, this is non-negligible — typically 5–15% slower than the base model.

LoRA’s key advantage: LoRA merges into the base weight matrices, so the merged model has zero inference overhead. This is why LoRA displaced adapter layers in most production systems after 2022.

When adapter layers are still used: multi-task scenarios where you need to switch between tasks at inference time without reloading the model, and where the latency overhead is acceptable. For most modern fine-tuning use cases, LoRA is the better choice.

Code comparison:

# Adapter layer (Houlsby-style) — adds latency at inference
# Typically configured via adapter-transformers library
model.add_adapter("my_adapter", config=HoulsbyConfig(reduction_factor=16))
model.train_adapter("my_adapter")  # Only train adapter params

# LoRA — zero inference overhead after merge
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
lora_config = LoraConfig(r=16, lora_alpha=32, ...)
model = get_peft_model(model, lora_config)
# After training: model.merge_and_unload() removes all overhead

Q5. How do you handle catastrophic forgetting with LoRA?

Model Answer

LoRA inherently mitigates forgetting because the base model weights are frozen — the model cannot forget what’s encoded in the base weights directly. The forgetting that occurs with LoRA is subtler:

What actually gets “forgotten” with LoRA: 1. Instruction-following behavior if you fine-tune a base model instead of an instruction-tuned model 2. General capabilities if the LoRA adapter learns representations that compete with the base model’s instruction-following patterns (rank too high + overfitted adapter) 3. Format consistency if the training data doesn’t match the model’s chat template

Mitigations: - Always fine-tune from an instruction-tuned checkpoint for chat tasks - Use a diverse training mix: task-specific examples + a small proportion (~5–10%) of general instruction-following examples (replay buffer) - Keep rank low enough that the adapter acts as a targeted adjustment, not a broad override (start at \(r=16\), not \(r=128\)) - Evaluate not just on the fine-tuned task but on a general instruction-following benchmark after training

Follow-up: “What’s a replay buffer in this context?” A small set of general-purpose instruction-following examples mixed into the fine-tuning training data. Typically 5–10% of the batch. It reminds the adapter that the model needs to maintain general chat behavior, not just perform the new task.

Q6. Compare LoRA vs. full fine-tuning on a 7B model: when would you choose full fine-tuning despite the cost?

Model Answer

Default to LoRA. Choose full fine-tuning in these specific circumstances:

1. Fundamental distribution shift — if you’re teaching the model a genuinely new modality, a language it barely knows, or a reasoning paradigm that requires deep architectural changes across all weights. LoRA’s low-rank constraint may be insufficient for the scale of adaptation needed.

2. Continued pretraining — if you’re extending pretraining on large-scale domain data (hundreds of GBs of text) to deeply bake domain knowledge into the base weights. LoRA is too constrained for this scale of change.

3. Precision-critical numerical applications — if quantization noise (even at FP16) is unacceptable (e.g., scientific simulation, financial modeling where small numerical errors compound), full FP32 fine-tuning is the safe choice.

4. When the quality gap matters — LoRA’s ~1–3% quality gap is usually negligible, but for tasks where marginal quality has high value (competitive benchmarks, high-stakes medical applications), full fine-tuning provides that ceiling.

In practice: most teams should exhaust LoRA options (expand target modules, increase rank, tune alpha, improve data quality) before concluding that full fine-tuning is necessary. The compute cost difference is an order of magnitude.

14.12.3 🔴 Senior Level

Q1. Explain the LoRA math. Why does low-rank decomposition work for fine-tuning?

🎯 What great looks like: Explains the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis, connects it to the Aghajanyan et al. (2021) empirical finding, walks through the \(\Delta W = BA\) decomposition with correct matrix dimensions, explains the \(\alpha/r\) scaling and why it enables rank-independent hyperparameter transfer, and covers the zero-initialization of B. Bonus: explains that the regularization effect of low-rank constraint is a feature, not just a limitation.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Explaining LoRA as “just adding small matrices” without addressing why low-rank works. The question is about the theoretical justification — the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis — not just the mechanics.

Model Answer

The theoretical foundation: intrinsic dimensionality of fine-tuning. Aghajanyan et al. (2021) showed empirically that fine-tuning a pretrained language model can be performed effectively in a very low-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space. For a model with millions of parameters, the essential optimization direction for a new task may require moving along only hundreds or thousands of dimensions. This is the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis: the loss landscape for fine-tuning has low effective dimensionality, concentrated in a small subspace.

The mathematical implication. If the optimal weight update \(\Delta W^*\) for a task has intrinsic rank \(r^*\), then any full-rank update \(\Delta W\) trained to convergence will find a solution where the important variation lies in an \(r^*\)-dimensional subspace — the remaining dimensions converge to near-zero. LoRA makes this explicit: by constraining \(\Delta W = BA\) with \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) and \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\), the optimizer is forced to find the task-relevant subspace from the start. If \(r \geq r^*\), LoRA can represent the optimal update exactly. Empirically, \(r^* \ll \min(d, k)\) for most fine-tuning tasks, which is why \(r = 16\) or \(r = 32\) captures most of the quality achievable with full fine-tuning.

The scaling factor. The forward pass is \(h = Wx + \frac{\alpha}{r} BAx\). The \(\frac{\alpha}{r}\) term ensures that changing \(r\) without changing \(\alpha\) scales the adapter’s contribution inversely with rank — preventing high-rank adapters from dominating the base model output. Setting \(\alpha = 2r\) (scaling = 2.0) is the conventional default, validated empirically across many tasks and model families. This parameterization enables rank-independent hyperparameter transfer: you can change \(r\) without retuning the learning rate if you keep \(\alpha = 2r\).

The initialization. \(A\) is initialized from \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\); \(B\) is initialized to zero. Therefore \(\Delta W = BA = 0\) at training step zero. The model begins from exact pretrained behavior — the base model’s capabilities are not perturbed before training starts. As training proceeds, \(B\) departs from zero along the directions most relevant for the task, guided by gradient signal. This initialization is necessary for stable training and for preserving base model capabilities during the early training phase.

The regularization interpretation. Low-rank constraint is not merely a parameter reduction trick — it’s a form of structural regularization. A full-rank update gives the optimizer freedom to modify representations arbitrarily, including in directions that encode noise or dataset-specific artifacts. Constraining to rank \(r\) forces the optimizer to find compact, efficient adaptations: the model can only change representations along the \(r\) most task-relevant directions. This often results in less overfitting than full fine-tuning on small datasets, beyond the formal fact that base weights are frozen.

Q2. How does LoRA rank affect model capacity vs. memory? Walk through the calculation for a 7B model.

🎯 What great looks like: Knows the weight matrix dimensions for LLaMA-family 7B models (~4096×4096 for attention, ~4096×11008 for MLP), correctly computes the parameter count for a given rank and target module set, walks through the memory formula, and connects rank to expressive capacity with a concrete bound (rank \(r\) means updates in a \(r\)-dimensional subspace of the weight space).

LLaMA-3.1-8B architecture reference: - Hidden dimension: \(d = 4096\) - Attention heads: 32, head dimension: 128 - Attention projection shapes: \(q/k/v/o\): all \(4096 \times 4096\) - MLP: gate_proj and up_proj are \(4096 \times 14336\); down_proj is \(14336 \times 4096\) - Number of transformer layers: 32

Trainable parameter calculation for \(r=16\), attention-only:

Per attention projection: \(r(d + k) = 16 \times (4096 + 4096) = 131{,}072\) params

Four attention projections per layer: \(4 \times 131{,}072 = 524{,}288\) params/layer

32 layers: \(32 \times 524{,}288 = 16{,}777{,}216\) trainable params ≈ 16.8M params, or 0.21% of 8B

Adding MLP layers (\(r=16\)):

gate_proj + up_proj: \(r(4096 + 14336) = 16 \times 18432 = 294{,}912\) params each

down_proj: \(r(14336 + 4096) = 16 \times 18432 = 294{,}912\) params

Three MLP projections per layer: \(3 \times 294{,}912 = 884{,}736\) params/layer

Combined attention + MLP: \(16.8 + 28.3 \approx 45M\) params, or ~0.56% of 8B.

Memory for adapter optimizer states (Adam, FP32):

Adam stores 2 moment vectors per trainable parameter at FP32 (4 bytes each).

\(45M \times 2 \times 4 \approx 360\) MB for optimizer states — negligible compared to the 14 GB base model.

Capacity bound: with rank \(r=16\) and 7 target matrix shapes, updates live in at most a \(16 \times 7 \times 32 = 3584\)-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space. This constrains the optimizer but is empirically sufficient to capture most task-relevant adaptations given that fine-tuning intrinsic rank is typically far below 100.

Effect of increasing rank:

Doubling to \(r=32\) doubles the trainable parameter count (~90M) and the optimizer state memory (~720 MB). Quality improvement: modest, typically 1–2% on most benchmarks, with diminishing returns above \(r=32\). Overfitting risk: increases proportionally — important on small datasets.

Q3. When would you keep LoRA weights separate vs. merge them into the base model? Give a production scenario for each.

🎯 What great looks like: Goes beyond “merge for latency, keep separate for multiple adapters” to give specific production scenarios with concrete tradeoffs — including the serving architecture details (how hot-swapping actually works), the storage economics, and the failure modes of each approach.

Model Answer

Scenario A: Merge — single-adapter, latency-critical serving

A company fine-tunes one model for internal document Q&A and deploys it on a single endpoint. All traffic uses the same fine-tuned behavior. The correct approach:

  1. Train with QLoRA on the 7B base
  2. Reload base at FP16, merge with merge_and_unload()
  3. Deploy the merged model with vLLM or TensorRT-LLM

Scenario B: Keep separate — multi-tenant SaaS with per-customer adapters

A startup offers a “customize your AI assistant” product. Each customer fine-tunes their own persona and knowledge base. There are 50+ customer adapters, and a customer’s requests should always use their adapter.

Merging is operationally infeasible: it requires storing 50 full-size models (700 GB+ for 7B), loading a different model per customer, and cold-loading models for customers who haven’t been active recently. Latency: 10–60 seconds to load a 7B model from disk.

With separate adapters: - One base model (14 GB at FP16) loaded once, permanently warm in GPU HBM - 50 adapters × ~30 MB = ~1.5 GB — keep all in CPU RAM - Per-request: identify customer adapter, load adapter matrices into GPU (~5–10 ms PCIE transfer), run forward pass

Systems like vLLM’s native LoRA support or Predibase’s LoRAX batch requests from multiple customers together, sharing base model compute across the batch while applying different adapters per-sequence. S-LoRA demonstrated that a single A100 can serve 100+ concurrent adapters with <10% throughput penalty vs. a single merged model.

Operational consideration: when to regenerate adapters. With separate adapters, you can retrain only the changed customer’s adapter when they update their data — the base model and all other customers’ adapters are untouched. With merged models, updating one customer requires regenerating and redeploying their full model.

Edge case: if you use quantization for deployment (GPTQ/AWQ), you cannot merge into the quantized model — you must merge at FP16 first, then quantize the merged model. Factor this into your deployment pipeline.

Q4. Compare DoRA vs. LoRA. In what scenarios would you choose DoRA?

🎯 What great looks like: Correctly explains the weight decomposition (magnitude + direction), explains why this gives DoRA more flexibility than LoRA, cites tasks where this matters empirically, and gives an honest assessment of when LoRA is still the better default (simpler, faster, less implementation complexity for similar quality).

⚠️ Common Mistake: Presenting DoRA as strictly better than LoRA. The empirical evidence is nuanced — DoRA has marginal benefits on some tasks but is not consistently better across the board, and it adds complexity and training overhead.

Model Answer

The DoRA decomposition. DoRA decomposes each weight matrix \(W\) as:

\[W = m \cdot \frac{V}{\|V\|}\]

where \(m\) is a scalar magnitude vector (one value per column) and \(V / \|V\|\) is the direction matrix (normalized columns). During fine-tuning: - \(m\) is trained directly as a learnable parameter (very few parameters — one per output dimension) - The direction component is adapted using standard LoRA: \(\Delta V = BA\)

This gives DoRA two independent channels of adaptation: how much each weight column contributes (magnitude) and what direction it points (direction via LoRA).

Why this might help. Standard LoRA updates the weight as \(W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA\), which couples magnitude and direction changes. If the optimal fine-tuning update primarily requires changing the magnitude of some weight columns without significantly rotating them (or vice versa), LoRA must “waste” rank capacity on the coupled representation. DoRA separates these, allowing more efficient use of the \(r\) dimensional budget.

When DoRA empirically outperforms LoRA: - Tasks with large distributional shifts between pretraining and fine-tuning data (the model needs to significantly scale some output directions, not just rotate them) - Instruction tuning tasks requiring consistent output formatting with dramatically different length distributions - The original DoRA paper showed improvements on commonsense reasoning and visual instruction tuning benchmarks

When LoRA is still the right default: - Most standard fine-tuning tasks show less than 1–2% difference between DoRA and LoRA - DoRA adds implementation complexity: the magnitude decomposition requires custom initialization and the PEFT library’s DoRA implementation has been slower and less stable than LoRA historically - DoRA uses more memory per layer (additional magnitude vectors) — minor but non-zero - The Hugging Face PEFT library supports DoRA (use_dora=True in LoraConfig) but it’s less battle-tested than LoRA

My recommendation: default to LoRA. Consider DoRA only if: (a) you’ve tuned LoRA rank and target modules and still have a quality gap, and (b) you have empirical evidence on your specific task that DoRA closes that gap. Run a controlled A/B (same rank, same target modules, same training data) before committing to DoRA in production.

Q5. Back-of-envelope: how much GPU memory do you need to QLoRA fine-tune a 7B model on a single GPU?

Model Answer

Let’s work through each component for a 7B model with QLoRA (\(r=16\), all attention + MLP layers):

1. Base model weights (NF4, 4-bit): \[7 \times 10^9 \times 0.5 \text{ bytes/param} = 3.5 \text{ GB}\]

2. NF4 dequantization buffer (BF16, for active computation): Transformer inference dequantizes one layer at a time. Buffer ≈ one layer’s weights at BF16. For 7B with 32 layers: \(3.5\text{ GB} / 32 \approx 0.11\) GB active buffer. With full layer prefetching: ~0.5–1 GB.

3. LoRA trainable parameters (FP32): From Q2 above: ~45M trainable parameters for all attention + MLP at \(r=16\). \[45 \times 10^6 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 180 \text{ MB}\]

4. Adam optimizer states for LoRA parameters (FP32, first + second moment): \[45 \times 10^6 \times 2 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 360 \text{ MB}\]

5. Gradients for LoRA parameters (FP32): \[45 \times 10^6 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 180 \text{ MB}\]

6. Activations (the variable component): Activations scale as: batch_size × sequence_length × hidden_dim × num_layers × ~4 bytes (BF16 intermediate). For batch=4, seq=2048, hidden=4096, 32 layers: \(4 \times 2048 \times 4096 \times 32 \times 2 \approx 2.1\) GB without checkpointing.

With gradient checkpointing (recompute activations during backward pass): reduces activation memory by ~\(\sqrt{\text{layers}}\) factor, to approximately 0.5–0.8 GB at the cost of ~30% more compute.

Total (with gradient checkpointing): | Component | Memory | |—|—| | NF4 weights | 3.5 GB | | Dequant buffer | ~0.5 GB | | LoRA params + optimizer + gradients | ~0.7 GB | | Activations (with checkpointing) | ~0.5–1 GB | | Framework overhead (CUDA context, kernels) | ~1–2 GB | | Total | ~7–9 GB |

Hardware sufficiency: This fits on an RTX 4090 (24 GB) with large headroom, or an A10G (24 GB). Even an RTX 3090 (24 GB) or RTX 3080 Ti (12 GB) can run this with aggressive gradient checkpointing and batch size 1. Without gradient checkpointing: add 2–4 GB for activations, still fits on 24 GB hardware with batch size 4.

Sanity check: bitsandbytes + PEFT documentation reports 4.8 GB for a 7B model with batch=1, seq=256, gradient checkpointing. At batch=4, seq=2048, my estimate of 7–9 GB is consistent with that baseline.

## Why Full Fine-Tuning Isn't Always Practical

Full fine-tuning is the gold standard: update every weight in the model on your task-specific data, no compromises. The problem is arithmetic.

For a model with $N$ parameters trained with the Adam optimizer at FP16/FP32 mixed precision:

| Memory Component | Size |
|---|---|
| Model weights (FP16) | $2N$ bytes |
| Gradient copies (FP16) | $2N$ bytes |
| Adam first moment — FP32 | $4N$ bytes |
| Adam second moment — FP32 | $4N$ bytes |
| **Total (minimum, no activations)** | **$\approx 12N$ bytes** |

For a 7B parameter model: $12 \times 7 \times 10^9 \approx 84\text{ GB}$ — before a single activation is stored. Add activations (which scale with batch size × sequence length × hidden dimension) and you are looking at 100–130 GB for a reasonable batch size. That means at minimum two A100 80GB GPUs with tensor parallelism, just to run one training step.

Scale to 13B: ~160 GB minimum. Scale to 70B: ~840 GB — roughly ten A100s. Most teams do not have this.

**The storage problem compounds the compute problem.** If you need one fine-tuned model per customer, per language, or per product line, full fine-tuning produces one complete model copy per variant. A 7B FP16 model is ~14 GB on disk. One hundred customer-specific variants: 1.4 TB of model storage, separate infrastructure to serve each. Neither option is operationally or financially sane at scale.

These two forces — training memory and deployment storage — are exactly why PEFT methods exist: **train less than 1% of the parameters, get comparable quality, pay megabytes not gigabytes per variant.**

::: {.callout-tip}
**Analogy.** Full fine-tuning rewrites the entire textbook for a new course. PEFT is inserting sticky notes on the relevant pages: the book stays unchanged, you only manage the notes. At deployment, you can either leave the sticky notes in (unmerged adapter) or copy their content into the margins (merged model) — either way, the book itself is intact.
:::

## The PEFT Family: What Your Options Are

PEFT is not a single method. It is a family of approaches, each with different parameter efficiency, quality, and inference overhead tradeoffs.

| Method | Trainable Params | Inference Overhead | Quality vs. Full FT | Memory Reduction | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **LoRA** | 0.1–1% | None (after merge) | Within 1–3% | 60–80% | General-purpose fine-tuning; multi-tenant serving |
| **QLoRA** | 0.1–1% | None (after merge) | Within 1–2% of LoRA | 80–90%+ | Large models (30B+) on single GPUs |
| **Adapter Layers** | 1–5% | +5–15% latency per layer | Within 1–3% | 50–70% | Multi-task, task-switching at inference |
| **Prefix Tuning** | <0.1% | Sequence length overhead | 2–5% gap at small scale | 70–80% | Frozen model deployment, low-resource |
| **Prompt Tuning** | <0.01% | None | Poor at <10B params | 90%+ | Very large models only (100B+) |
| **DoRA** | 0.1–1% | None (after merge) | Marginally better than LoRA | 60–80% | LoRA-hard tasks; fine control of direction vs. magnitude |
| **IA3** | <0.01% | None | Slightly below LoRA | 95%+ | Extreme parameter budget; fast deployment |

**LoRA has won in practice.** The combination of strong quality, no inference overhead after merging, small adapter files, and ecosystem support (Hugging Face PEFT, vLLM, TGI, LoRAX, S-LoRA) makes it the default choice. The other methods are worth knowing for interviews and specific edge cases, but LoRA is what you will encounter in production.

::: {.callout-warning}
**Common Misconception: "Prefix tuning is just as good as LoRA."**
Prefix tuning prepends trainable virtual tokens to every layer's key-value sequence. It works reasonably well at very large scale (100B+ parameters) but significantly underperforms LoRA on models below 10B. If someone asks why LoRA became dominant over prefix tuning, the answer is empirical: LoRA is consistently better at the model sizes most teams actually use.
:::

## LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation

### The Core Mathematical Insight

LoRA begins with an observation from the optimization literature: when you fine-tune a pretrained model, the weight update $\Delta W$ has low **intrinsic rank**. The important variation in the update lies in a small subspace, not spread across the full dimensionality of the weight matrix.

Formally, instead of learning a full update $\Delta W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ (which requires $d \times k$ parameters), LoRA parameterizes it as:

$$\Delta W = BA$$

where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, with $r \ll \min(d, k)$.

The adapted forward pass becomes:

$$h = Wx + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot BAx$$

where $W$ is frozen and only $B$ and $A$ receive gradient updates during training.

At inference time, if you have a single adapter, you merge:

$$W_{\text{final}} = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot BA$$

The merged model is a standard weight matrix — structurally identical to the original, with zero inference overhead.

**Parameter count comparison.** For a weight matrix of shape $d \times k$ (e.g., $4096 \times 4096$ in a 7B model):

- Full fine-tuning: $d \times k = 16{,}777{,}216$ parameters per matrix
- LoRA with $r=16$: $r(d + k) = 16 \times 8192 = 131{,}072$ parameters per matrix
- Reduction: **128×** for that matrix, or about **0.78%** of the original count

::: {.callout-tip}
**Why does low rank work?** Think of fine-tuning as teaching a student who already knows most of what they need to know. You don't need to rewrite their entire mental model — you need to shift a few key conceptual directions. Low-rank decomposition captures those few directions explicitly. The regularization effect is a bonus: constraining updates to a low-rank subspace prevents the model from chasing noise in the fine-tuning data.
:::

### The Scaling Factor Alpha

The $\frac{\alpha}{r}$ scaling factor controls how much the adapter influences the output relative to the frozen base weights. This deserves careful attention.

If $\alpha = r$, the scaling factor is $1.0$ — the adapter contributes at the same effective magnitude as the base weights. If $\alpha = 2r$, the scaling is $2.0$. The conventional default is $\alpha = 2r$ (e.g., $r=16, \alpha=32$), which has been empirically validated across many tasks.

**Why not just absorb the scaling into the learning rate?** Two reasons:

1. **Hyperparameter transfer across ranks:** If you hold $\alpha$ fixed and change $r$, the effective learning rate of the adapter changes proportionally. If you hold $\alpha/r$ fixed (e.g., always set $\alpha = 2r$), you can change $r$ without retuning your learning rate. This makes rank ablations cleaner.
2. **Initialization stability:** $B$ is initialized from a Gaussian; $A$ is initialized to **zero**, so $\Delta W = BA = 0$ at the start of training. The model begins from exact pretrained behavior. The $\alpha/r$ scaling does not affect this property — it only affects how quickly the adapter grows during training.

::: {.callout-warning}
**Common Misconception: "Alpha is just a second learning rate — set it and forget it."**
Alpha interacts with both the learning rate and the rank. A run with $r=8, \alpha=16$ has the same scaling ($\alpha/r = 2$) as $r=32, \alpha=64$, but very different parameter counts and effective capacity. When comparing LoRA configurations, always report both $r$ and $\alpha$ — reporting only one is ambiguous.
:::

### The Initialization Trick

This detail is often skipped but is mechanically important:

- **A** is initialized from $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ (small random values)
- **B** is initialized to **zero**

Result: $\Delta W = BA = \mathbf{0}$ at training step zero. The model starts from exactly the pretrained checkpoint's behavior. Without this, random initialization of both A and B would introduce a random perturbation to every adapted weight matrix before training even begins, potentially disrupting the base model's capabilities. The zero initialization of B is what makes LoRA safe to add to any pretrained model without degrading it before training starts.

### Where to Inject Adapters

LoRA adapters can in principle be applied to any weight matrix. In practice, the question is which layers matter most for the target task.

**Attention projection matrices** are the standard target. In a multi-head attention layer, the four matrices are:
- $W_Q$ (query projection) — determines what the model "searches for"
- $W_K$ (key projection) — determines what the model "offers to be found"
- $W_V$ (value projection) — determines what information gets aggregated
- $W_O$ (output projection) — mixes head outputs back into the residual stream

The original LoRA paper targeted only $W_Q$ and $W_V$, finding that $W_K$ added little. Subsequent work established that adding all four attention projections reliably improves quality for most tasks.

**MLP layers** (the two feedforward linear projections per transformer block, typically named `gate_proj`, `up_proj`, `down_proj` in LLaMA-family models) store factual knowledge. For tasks requiring new factual associations, domain-specific knowledge, or complex multi-step reasoning, including MLP layers in the target set meaningfully improves quality. For simple format or style adaptation, attention projections alone are usually sufficient.

**Priority order for target module selection:**

1. Minimum viable: `q_proj`, `v_proj`
2. Standard: `q_proj`, `k_proj`, `v_proj`, `o_proj`
3. Comprehensive: all attention projections + `gate_proj`, `up_proj`, `down_proj`

Start at level 2. If quality is insufficient at your chosen rank, expand to level 3 before increasing rank — it often helps more and costs less.

## Choosing LoRA Hyperparameters

### Rank Selection Guidance

| Use Case | Recommended Rank | Alpha | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple format/style change | 4–8 | 8–16 | Low-rank sufficient; smaller adapter |
| Single-task instruction following | 8–16 | 16–32 | Default starting point |
| Complex domain adaptation | 16–32 | 32–64 | Start at 16, increase if validation plateaus |
| Multi-step reasoning fine-tuning | 32–64 | 64–128 | Add MLP layers to targets |
| Code generation with new APIs | 16–32 | 32–64 | MLP layers critical |
| Very small dataset (<500 examples) | 4–8 | 8–16 | Low rank = less overfitting |
| Large diverse dataset (>50K examples) | 32–64 | 64–128 | Higher rank can generalize |

**The rank ablation process:**

1. Start at $r=16$, $\alpha=32$, targeting all attention projections
2. Train and measure validation quality
3. Try $r=8$ — if validation quality is similar, use $r=8$ (fewer params, less overfitting risk)
4. Try $r=32$ — if validation quality improves materially, consider $r=32$
5. If $r=32$ still insufficient: expand target modules to include MLP layers first, then try $r=64$
6. Do not use $r=128+$ without empirical evidence — rarely helps over $r=64$

::: {.callout-warning}
**Production Gotcha: High rank without dropout on small datasets.**
Using $r=128$ on a dataset of 500 examples is a common way to overfit catastrophically. The adapter has 128 dimensions of freedom per weight matrix — far more than the dataset can constrain. If you need high expressivity on a small dataset, use $r=16$ with LoRA dropout of $0.1$–$0.15$, not $r=128$ with no dropout.
:::

## QLoRA: Fine-Tune 70B on One GPU

QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) stacks three independent innovations on top of LoRA. Each one reduces memory usage. Together they make 70B fine-tuning on a single A100 80GB practical.

### Innovation 1: NF4 (Normal Float 4-bit) Quantization

Standard 4-bit integer quantization divides the weight range into 16 equally spaced buckets. The problem: transformer weights are not uniformly distributed — they follow an approximately normal (bell-shaped) distribution. Uniform quantization wastes precision in the tails and crowds it in the center.

NF4 fixes this by placing the 16 quantization levels at positions corresponding to equal-area quantiles of the standard normal distribution. Each level covers the same probability mass, so the expected quantization error is minimized for normally-distributed values.

**Result:** Base model weights stored at 0.5 bytes per parameter (4 bits) instead of 2 bytes (FP16). A 70B model's weights shrink from 140 GB to approximately 35 GB. Computation happens in BF16 (the weights are dequantized on-the-fly during the forward pass), so numerical precision during computation is maintained.

The dequantization overhead is real: QLoRA forward passes run approximately 10–20% slower than pure FP16. For training where the bottleneck is often data loading and optimizer steps, this is acceptable.

### Innovation 2: Double Quantization

When you quantize a block of weights to NF4, you need to store a scaling constant per block to enable dequantization. These constants are in FP32. For a 70B model with block size 64, there are $\approx 70B / 64 \approx 1.1$ billion constants — about **4 GB at FP32**.

Double quantization applies a second quantization pass to these constants, from FP32 to 8-bit float. This reduces constant storage from ~4 GB to ~0.5 GB — a 3.5 GB saving. Modest in absolute terms, but meaningful when fitting a 70B model into 80 GB.

### Innovation 3: Paged Optimizers

Adam optimizer states (first and second moment vectors) for the LoRA adapter parameters are stored in GPU VRAM during training. If peak memory demand (model + activations + optimizer) exceeds available VRAM, standard training crashes with an OOM error.

Paged optimizers register Adam states as **CUDA unified memory** — memory that the CUDA driver can automatically page to CPU RAM when the GPU is under pressure. When the page-out happens, subsequent accesses trigger a PCIe transfer back to GPU. This adds latency proportional to how often paging occurs, but it converts hard crashes into graceful slowdowns.

In practice, paged optimizers rarely trigger on well-configured training runs. Their value is as a safety margin when experimenting with large batch sizes, long sequences, or gradient checkpointing settings near the VRAM limit.

### QLoRA Quality vs. Full Fine-Tuning

QLoRA's quality relative to full fine-tuning is the critical question for practitioners.

The original QLoRA paper demonstrated that QLoRA-trained Guanaco models achieved MMLU scores within 1–2% of ChatGPT at the time, despite training on a single GPU. Subsequent replication studies across multiple model families (LLaMA, Mistral, Falcon) consistently showed:

- QLoRA ≈ LoRA at FP16 (within ~0.5–1% on most benchmarks)
- LoRA at FP16 ≈ full fine-tuning (within ~1–3% at rank 16–32)
- Therefore, QLoRA is within ~1–3% of full fine-tuning quality on most standard tasks

**When QLoRA quality may not be sufficient:** tasks requiring precise numerical computation, highly fine-grained factual accuracy (the quantization noise can introduce small errors), or when the base model itself is already highly optimized and the fine-tuning dataset is very small (where quantization noise matters more relative to the signal).

::: {.callout-warning}
**Production Gotcha: Merging QLoRA adapters.**
QLoRA trains on a 4-bit quantized base model. The adapter learns to compensate for the quantization noise in that specific base. **Do not merge the adapter into the 4-bit model.** The correct deployment path is: (1) load the base model fresh at FP16/BF16, (2) merge the adapter with `merge_and_unload()`, (3) optionally re-quantize the merged FP16 model for deployment using GPTQ or AWQ. Merging into the 4-bit base reintroduces quantization error that the adapter did not train against.
:::

## Merging, Serving, and Multi-Adapter Architecture

### When to Merge vs. Keep Separate

| Scenario | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Single adapter, latency-sensitive serving | Merge | Zero inference overhead; use any inference framework |
| Multiple adapters, one base model, dynamic traffic | Keep separate | Hot-swap with LoRAX or S-LoRA; base model in memory once |
| Customer-specific models at scale (>10 tenants) | Keep separate + adapter registry | 30–100 MB per adapter vs. 14 GB+ per full model |
| Offline batch inference | Merge | Simplest; no runtime adapter management |
| A/B testing different adapters | Keep separate | Swap at request time; no redeployment |
| Continual learning / adapter updates | Keep separate | Update only the adapter, not the base model |

### The Merger Operation

$$W_{\text{final}} = W_{\text{original}} + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot B \cdot A$$

This matrix addition is performed for every adapted weight matrix. The result is structurally a standard weight matrix — indistinguishable from a model that was fully fine-tuned to those weight values. Inference speed, memory footprint, and compatibility with inference frameworks are identical to the original base model.

```python
# Merge adapter into base model for deployment
merged_model = peft_model.merge_and_unload()
merged_model.save_pretrained("./deployed-model")
tokenizer.save_pretrained("./deployed-model")

# Load adapter separately (multi-tenant hot-swap pattern)
from peft import PeftModel
base_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B")
tenant_a_model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base_model, "./adapters/tenant-a/")
tenant_b_model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base_model, "./adapters/tenant-b/")
```

### Multi-LoRA Serving

vLLM natively supports LoRA hot-swapping — you load the base model once and specify per-request which adapter to use via the `lora_request` parameter. S-LoRA (academic) and LoRAX (Predibase) demonstrate batching requests across different adapters, amortizing base model compute. At scale:

- One A100 80GB serves a 7B base model
- 100 tenant adapters at ~30 MB each = ~3 GB total adapter storage in CPU RAM
- At request time: identify tenant → load adapter → forward pass
- Throughput: comparable to single-model serving for mixed-tenant traffic

**Storage economics.** 100 tenants with full fine-tuning: $100 \times 14\text{ GB} = 1.4\text{ TB}$ of model storage. 100 tenants with LoRA: $14\text{ GB base} + 100 \times 30\text{ MB} \approx 17\text{ GB total}$. The economics are not close.

## Advanced PEFT Methods

### DoRA: Weight-Decomposed LoRA

DoRA decomposes each weight matrix $W$ into a **magnitude** component $m$ (a scalar per column) and a **direction** component $V$ (the normalized weight matrix):

$$W = m \cdot \frac{V}{\|V\|}$$

During fine-tuning, DoRA trains the magnitude component directly and applies LoRA to the direction component. This separation allows the model to independently control "how much" vs. "in which direction" each weight column moves.

**When DoRA outperforms LoRA:** tasks where the fine-tuning data requires significant changes to the scale of weight activations (e.g., adapting a model trained mostly on short outputs to produce very long, structured outputs). In these cases, the pure directional adaptation of LoRA is insufficient, and DoRA's independent magnitude control helps. The improvements are marginal (1–3%) on standard benchmarks but more pronounced on specific transfer tasks with large distributional shift.

**When DoRA is not worth the overhead:** most standard fine-tuning tasks. DoRA adds implementation complexity and the HuggingFace PEFT implementation is somewhat slower than standard LoRA. Default to LoRA; consider DoRA only when you have empirical evidence that LoRA's quality ceiling is too low for your specific task.

### IA3: Infused Adapter by Inhibiting and Amplifying Inner Activations

IA3 introduces learned scaling vectors for key, value, and feedforward activations. Instead of adding a low-rank matrix update to weights, it multiplies activations by a learned vector $l$:

$$h' = l \odot h$$

This is extremely parameter-efficient — typically less than 0.01% of the base model's parameters. The tradeoff is quality: IA3 consistently underperforms LoRA on complex tasks, though it approaches LoRA quality on simpler tasks.

**When to consider IA3:** extreme parameter budget constraints (e.g., serving on edge hardware where even 30 MB adapters matter), or rapid prototyping where training speed matters more than peak quality.

### Rank-Stabilized LoRA (rsLoRA)

Standard LoRA scales the adapter output by $\frac{\alpha}{r}$. As rank increases, the per-update magnitude grows, making high-rank training potentially unstable. rsLoRA changes the scaling to $\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{r}}$, which keeps the update magnitude stable across ranks. Use `use_rslora=True` in `LoraConfig` when training at ranks above 64 or when you observe training instability with high-rank configurations.

## Production Gotchas

::: {.callout-warning}
**Gotcha 1: Fine-tuning a base model, not an instruct model.**
If you start from a raw base model (e.g., `Llama-3.1-8B`) and fine-tune for a chat task, you get a task-specialized model that has never learned instruction following. The result follows the task format but lacks conversational coherence, safety behaviors, and general instruction compliance. Always start from an instruction-tuned checkpoint (`Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct`) unless you have a specific reason not to (e.g., you are doing full SFT + RLHF from scratch).
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
**Gotcha 2: Not applying the chat template.**
Every instruction-tuned model has a specific chat template with special tokens (`<|im_start|>`, `[INST]`, etc.). If your fine-tuning examples don't use the same template, the adapter learns a different input format than the base model was trained with. The result: the fine-tuned model degrades on standard chat prompts because the adapter now "expects" your custom format. Fix: use `tokenizer.apply_chat_template()` on all training examples.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
**Gotcha 3: Merging into a quantized model.**
As noted above — merging a LoRA adapter into a 4-bit quantized model reintroduces quantization error the adapter did not train to compensate for. Always merge into FP16/BF16 base, then optionally requantize for deployment.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
**Gotcha 4: Saving only the adapter when you need the merged model for CI/CD.**
Many deployment pipelines expect a complete model directory, not a base model + adapter pair. If your CI/CD tool cannot handle `PeftModel.from_pretrained()`, you need to pre-merge before pushing. Document your deployment artifact format explicitly.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
**Gotcha 5: Using `device_map="auto"` during training with gradient checkpointing.**
`device_map="auto"` places different layers on different devices. Gradient checkpointing with multi-device layer placement can cause silent correctness issues in some versions of Transformers. If you see anomalous training curves with this combination, pin all layers to a single GPU or use `accelerate` with explicit device placement.
:::

- For $r=8$: conservative, good for simple tasks and small datasets; for $r=32$–$64$: complex domain adaptation
- Target modules priority: `q_proj+v_proj` → all attention projections → + MLP layers
- Add MLP layers when task requires new factual associations or multi-step reasoning
- LoRA dropout 0.05–0.1 for datasets under ~5K examples; 0.0 for large datasets
- Never use $r > 64$ without empirical evidence it helps over $r=32$
- QLoRA = NF4 quantization + double quantization + paged optimizers + LoRA
- NF4 = 4-bit quantization optimal for normal-distributed weights; stores weights at 0.5 bytes/param
- Double quantization saves ~3–3.5 GB for 70B model by quantizing the quantization constants
- Paged optimizers: Adam states in CUDA unified memory; pages to CPU on GPU memory pressure
- QLoRA memory for 70B: ~40–48 GB — fits on one A100 80GB
- QLoRA quality vs. full fine-tuning: within ~1–3% on most benchmarks
- Merge LoRA: load base at FP16 → `merge_and_unload()` → save → optionally requantize

## Interview Questions

### 🟢 Entry Level

**Q1. What is PEFT and why was it developed?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning is a family of techniques that adapt a pretrained model to a new task by training only a small subset of additional parameters while keeping the base model frozen. It was developed to address two practical problems:

**Memory:** Full fine-tuning stores model weights, optimizer states (Adam requires 2× the parameter count), and gradients simultaneously. For a 7B model this totals ~84 GB — far beyond a single GPU. PEFT reduces this by training only ~0.1–1% of parameters.

**Storage and deployment:** Full fine-tuning produces one complete model copy per task or customer. At 14 GB per 7B model, 100 variants requires 1.4 TB of storage and separate serving infrastructure. PEFT adapters are 10–100 MB per variant; all variants share one base model deployment.

The dominant PEFT method today is LoRA, which achieves near-full-fine-tuning quality with no inference overhead after the adapter is merged into the base model.

**Weak answer:** "PEFT saves memory." (True but incomplete — misses the storage/deployment angle and the mechanism.)

**Strong answer:** The memory arithmetic above, plus the deployment economics, plus naming LoRA as the dominant method and why (no inference overhead).
:::

**Q2. What does LoRA stand for and what is the core idea?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
LoRA stands for **Low-Rank Adaptation**. The core idea: when you fine-tune a pretrained model, the weight updates $\Delta W$ have low intrinsic dimensionality. Rather than storing and computing a full $d \times k$ update matrix, LoRA decomposes it as $\Delta W = BA$ where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ with $r \ll \min(d, k)$.

The parameter count for the update drops from $d \times k$ to $r(d + k)$. For a 4096×4096 matrix with $r=16$: from 16.7M parameters to 131K — a 128× reduction. Only $A$ and $B$ receive gradient updates during training; the original $W$ is frozen. At inference, the adapter can be merged: $W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$, producing a standard weight matrix with no overhead.

**Weak answer:** "LoRA adds small matrices to the model." (Misses the math, the motivation, and the merge step.)

**Strong answer:** The decomposition math, parameter count comparison, the fact that $W$ is frozen, and the merge operation.
:::

**Q3. How do you choose LoRA rank $r$?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Start at $r=16$, $\alpha=32$ (the validated default). Then run a validation sweep:

- **Try $r=8$:** if validation quality is similar, use $r=8$ — fewer parameters, less overfitting risk, smaller adapter files.
- **Try $r=32$:** if $r=16$ quality is insufficient, increase rank. Add MLP layers to target modules first, since that often helps more than increasing rank alone.
- **Avoid $r > 64$** without empirical evidence it improves over $r=32$–$64$.

Signal from training curves: if training loss barely decreases, rank is likely insufficient (or learning rate is too low). If training loss drops much faster than validation loss, rank is too high for the dataset size — reduce rank or add dropout.
:::

**Q4. What is the difference between LoRA and QLoRA?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
LoRA is the adapter method: decompose weight updates as $BA$, train only $B$ and $A$. It can be applied to any model loaded at any precision.

QLoRA is LoRA applied to a 4-bit quantized base model, enabling fine-tuning of large models (30B, 70B) on a single GPU. It adds three innovations:
1. **NF4 quantization:** stores base model weights at 4 bits using a quantization scheme optimized for normally-distributed values, reducing a 70B model from 140 GB to ~35 GB
2. **Double quantization:** quantizes the NF4 scaling constants themselves, saving an additional ~3–3.5 GB
3. **Paged optimizers:** Adam states in unified memory that can page to CPU RAM, preventing OOM crashes

Quality: QLoRA is within ~1–2% of LoRA at FP16 on most benchmarks — the quantization noise is small relative to the adapter's corrective capacity.
:::

**Q5. What does `merge_and_unload()` do?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
It performs the matrix addition $W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$ for every adapted weight matrix, then removes the LoRA wrapper layers. The output is a standard model object — structurally identical to the original base model — with the adapter's learned adjustments baked in.

After merging: the model has zero inference overhead compared to an unmerged adapter, can be served by any inference framework without LoRA-specific support, and is smaller in memory (no separate $A$, $B$ matrices to track).

When NOT to merge: if you need multiple adapters on the same base model (multi-tenant serving), keep them separate and hot-swap at request time.
:::

**Q6. Which weight matrices does LoRA typically target and why?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Standard targets are the attention projection matrices: `q_proj`, `k_proj`, `v_proj`, `o_proj`. Query and value projections determine what a token attends to and what information gets aggregated — these are where task-specific adaptation most visibly occurs.

The original LoRA paper targeted only Q and V, finding K added little benefit. Current best practice is all four attention projections. For tasks requiring new factual knowledge or complex reasoning, add MLP layers (`gate_proj`, `up_proj`, `down_proj` in LLaMA-family models) — MLP layers store factual associations and need to adapt for domain-heavy tasks.

Priority order: `q_proj+v_proj` (minimum) → all attention projections (standard) → all attention + MLP (comprehensive).
:::

### 🟡 Mid Level

**Q1. Explain the LoRA scaling factor $\alpha/r$. Why not just adjust the learning rate?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
The $\alpha/r$ factor scales the adapter's contribution to the output: $h = Wx + \frac{\alpha}{r} BAx$. Think of it as a second learning rate multiplier that applies specifically to adapter outputs relative to base model outputs.

**Why not just use the learning rate?** Two reasons:

1. **Rank-independent hyperparameter transfer:** if you hold $\alpha/r$ constant (e.g., always set $\alpha = 2r$), you can change rank without retuning learning rate — the effective scaling stays at 2.0. If you absorbed scaling into the learning rate, changing rank would change the effective step size and require retuning.

2. **Initialization correctness:** $A$ is initialized from a Gaussian; $B$ is initialized to zero, so $\Delta W = BA = 0$ at step 0. The scaling factor doesn't affect this — it only influences how fast the adapter grows once training starts. The zero initialization of B is the key: it ensures the model starts from exact pretrained behavior regardless of the scaling factor.

**Practical tuning:** think of $\alpha/r$ and the optimizer LR as two multiplicative controls. If adapter is under-fitting (loss not decreasing), raise LR first, then $\alpha$. If over-adapting (forgetting base capabilities), reduce both together.

**Follow-up:** "What happens if you set $\alpha = r$?"
- Scaling = 1.0. The adapter contributes at the same magnitude as the base weights at initialization of training. Valid choice; just changes convergence dynamics. Default of $\alpha = 2r$ (scaling = 2.0) empirically trains more efficiently for most tasks.
:::

**Q2. Walk me through the memory breakdown for QLoRA fine-tuning a 7B model.**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
With QLoRA (NF4 + LoRA):

| Component | Size |
|---|---|
| Base model weights (NF4, 4-bit) | $0.5 \times 7B \approx 3.5$ GB |
| Activation buffers (NF4 → BF16 dequant) | ~1–2 GB |
| LoRA trainable params (FP32) | $P \approx 0.01 \times 7B \times 4 \approx 280$ MB |
| Adam optimizer states for LoRA (FP32, 2× params) | ~560 MB |
| Activations (batch=4, seq=2048, gradient checkpointing) | ~2–4 GB |
| **Total** | **~8–12 GB** |

This fits comfortably on a single RTX 3090 (24 GB) or A10G (24 GB).

Key point: the base model at NF4 is 3.5 GB, not 14 GB (FP16). That's the core win. Double quantization saves another ~0.3–0.5 GB. Paged optimizers don't reduce the baseline — they prevent OOM crashes at peak memory demand.

**Without QLoRA (LoRA at FP16):**
- Base model at FP16: 14 GB
- LoRA optimizer states: ~560 MB
- Activations: ~2–4 GB
- **Total: ~17–20 GB** — needs an A100 40GB or similar

**Full fine-tuning:**
- Weights + optimizer + gradients: ~84 GB — needs 2+ A100 80GB
:::

**Q3. How does QLoRA achieve near-full-fine-tuning quality despite using 4-bit weights?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Two mechanisms work together:

**NF4 minimizes quantization error:** NF4 places quantization levels at equal-area quantiles of the normal distribution. Transformer weights are approximately normally distributed, so NF4 minimizes the expected quantization error for this distribution — better than uniform int4, which wastes precision in the tails.

**LoRA adapter compensates for quantization noise:** Even with optimal quantization, NF4 introduces small errors relative to FP16. The LoRA adapter, trained on top of the quantized model, learns to compensate for these errors. The adapter's learned $BA$ matrices absorb the task-specific signal plus any correction needed for quantization artifacts. This is why QLoRA quality is close to LoRA at FP16 rather than significantly worse.

**The critical caveat:** when merging a QLoRA-trained adapter for deployment, do NOT merge into the 4-bit base. Reload the base at FP16 and merge there. Merging into the 4-bit model loses the adapter's quantization-error compensation.

**Follow-up:** "Does NF4 quantization work for all models, or only transformer language models?"
NF4 is optimal for normally-distributed weights. Most large neural networks (including vision transformers, multimodal models) have approximately normal weight distributions, so NF4 works broadly. However, some specialized architectures or very small models may have non-normal weight distributions, where NF4 provides less benefit over uniform int4.
:::

**Q4. What is the difference between LoRA adapter layers and classic Houlsby adapter layers?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Classic Houlsby adapters (2019) insert small feedforward modules between the attention and MLP sub-layers of each transformer block. The module is typically: layer norm → down-project → non-linearity → up-project → residual. Only the adapter module parameters are trained.

**Inference overhead:** at inference, the adapter module adds two matrix multiplications per layer per forward pass. For a 32-layer transformer, that's 64 extra matrix multiplications. For latency-sensitive applications, this is non-negligible — typically 5–15% slower than the base model.

**LoRA's key advantage:** LoRA merges into the base weight matrices, so the merged model has zero inference overhead. This is why LoRA displaced adapter layers in most production systems after 2022.

**When adapter layers are still used:** multi-task scenarios where you need to switch between tasks at inference time without reloading the model, and where the latency overhead is acceptable. For most modern fine-tuning use cases, LoRA is the better choice.

**Code comparison:**

```python
# Adapter layer (Houlsby-style) — adds latency at inference
# Typically configured via adapter-transformers library
model.add_adapter("my_adapter", config=HoulsbyConfig(reduction_factor=16))
model.train_adapter("my_adapter")  # Only train adapter params

# LoRA — zero inference overhead after merge
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
lora_config = LoraConfig(r=16, lora_alpha=32, ...)
model = get_peft_model(model, lora_config)
# After training: model.merge_and_unload() removes all overhead
```
:::

**Q5. How do you handle catastrophic forgetting with LoRA?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
LoRA inherently mitigates forgetting because **the base model weights are frozen** — the model cannot forget what's encoded in the base weights directly. The forgetting that occurs with LoRA is subtler:

**What actually gets "forgotten" with LoRA:**
1. Instruction-following behavior if you fine-tune a base model instead of an instruction-tuned model
2. General capabilities if the LoRA adapter learns representations that compete with the base model's instruction-following patterns (rank too high + overfitted adapter)
3. Format consistency if the training data doesn't match the model's chat template

**Mitigations:**
- Always fine-tune from an instruction-tuned checkpoint for chat tasks
- Use a diverse training mix: task-specific examples + a small proportion (~5–10%) of general instruction-following examples (replay buffer)
- Keep rank low enough that the adapter acts as a targeted adjustment, not a broad override (start at $r=16$, not $r=128$)
- Evaluate not just on the fine-tuned task but on a general instruction-following benchmark after training

**Follow-up:** "What's a replay buffer in this context?"
A small set of general-purpose instruction-following examples mixed into the fine-tuning training data. Typically 5–10% of the batch. It reminds the adapter that the model needs to maintain general chat behavior, not just perform the new task.
:::

**Q6. Compare LoRA vs. full fine-tuning on a 7B model: when would you choose full fine-tuning despite the cost?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Default to LoRA. Choose full fine-tuning in these specific circumstances:

**1. Fundamental distribution shift** — if you're teaching the model a genuinely new modality, a language it barely knows, or a reasoning paradigm that requires deep architectural changes across all weights. LoRA's low-rank constraint may be insufficient for the scale of adaptation needed.

**2. Continued pretraining** — if you're extending pretraining on large-scale domain data (hundreds of GBs of text) to deeply bake domain knowledge into the base weights. LoRA is too constrained for this scale of change.

**3. Precision-critical numerical applications** — if quantization noise (even at FP16) is unacceptable (e.g., scientific simulation, financial modeling where small numerical errors compound), full FP32 fine-tuning is the safe choice.

**4. When the quality gap matters** — LoRA's ~1–3% quality gap is usually negligible, but for tasks where marginal quality has high value (competitive benchmarks, high-stakes medical applications), full fine-tuning provides that ceiling.

**In practice:** most teams should exhaust LoRA options (expand target modules, increase rank, tune alpha, improve data quality) before concluding that full fine-tuning is necessary. The compute cost difference is an order of magnitude.
:::

### 🔴 Senior Level

**Q1. Explain the LoRA math. Why does low-rank decomposition work for fine-tuning?**

> 🎯 **What great looks like:** Explains the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis, connects it to the Aghajanyan et al. (2021) empirical finding, walks through the $\Delta W = BA$ decomposition with correct matrix dimensions, explains the $\alpha/r$ scaling and why it enables rank-independent hyperparameter transfer, and covers the zero-initialization of B. Bonus: explains that the regularization effect of low-rank constraint is a feature, not just a limitation.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Explaining LoRA as "just adding small matrices" without addressing why low-rank works. The question is about the theoretical justification — the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis — not just the mechanics.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**The theoretical foundation: intrinsic dimensionality of fine-tuning.** Aghajanyan et al. (2021) showed empirically that fine-tuning a pretrained language model can be performed effectively in a very low-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space. For a model with millions of parameters, the essential optimization direction for a new task may require moving along only hundreds or thousands of dimensions. This is the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis: the loss landscape for fine-tuning has low effective dimensionality, concentrated in a small subspace.

**The mathematical implication.** If the optimal weight update $\Delta W^*$ for a task has intrinsic rank $r^*$, then any full-rank update $\Delta W$ trained to convergence will find a solution where the important variation lies in an $r^*$-dimensional subspace — the remaining dimensions converge to near-zero. LoRA makes this explicit: by constraining $\Delta W = BA$ with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, the optimizer is forced to find the task-relevant subspace from the start. If $r \geq r^*$, LoRA can represent the optimal update exactly. Empirically, $r^* \ll \min(d, k)$ for most fine-tuning tasks, which is why $r = 16$ or $r = 32$ captures most of the quality achievable with full fine-tuning.

**The scaling factor.** The forward pass is $h = Wx + \frac{\alpha}{r} BAx$. The $\frac{\alpha}{r}$ term ensures that changing $r$ without changing $\alpha$ scales the adapter's contribution inversely with rank — preventing high-rank adapters from dominating the base model output. Setting $\alpha = 2r$ (scaling = 2.0) is the conventional default, validated empirically across many tasks and model families. This parameterization enables rank-independent hyperparameter transfer: you can change $r$ without retuning the learning rate if you keep $\alpha = 2r$.

**The initialization.** $A$ is initialized from $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$; $B$ is initialized to zero. Therefore $\Delta W = BA = 0$ at training step zero. The model begins from exact pretrained behavior — the base model's capabilities are not perturbed before training starts. As training proceeds, $B$ departs from zero along the directions most relevant for the task, guided by gradient signal. This initialization is necessary for stable training and for preserving base model capabilities during the early training phase.

**The regularization interpretation.** Low-rank constraint is not merely a parameter reduction trick — it's a form of structural regularization. A full-rank update gives the optimizer freedom to modify representations arbitrarily, including in directions that encode noise or dataset-specific artifacts. Constraining to rank $r$ forces the optimizer to find compact, efficient adaptations: the model can only change representations along the $r$ most task-relevant directions. This often results in less overfitting than full fine-tuning on small datasets, beyond the formal fact that base weights are frozen.
:::

**Q2. How does LoRA rank affect model capacity vs. memory? Walk through the calculation for a 7B model.**

> 🎯 **What great looks like:** Knows the weight matrix dimensions for LLaMA-family 7B models (~4096×4096 for attention, ~4096×11008 for MLP), correctly computes the parameter count for a given rank and target module set, walks through the memory formula, and connects rank to expressive capacity with a concrete bound (rank $r$ means updates in a $r$-dimensional subspace of the weight space).

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Stating numbers without the underlying formula. Interviewers asking this question want to see that you can derive the numbers, not just recall them.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**LLaMA-3.1-8B architecture reference:**
- Hidden dimension: $d = 4096$
- Attention heads: 32, head dimension: 128
- Attention projection shapes: $q/k/v/o$: all $4096 \times 4096$
- MLP: `gate_proj` and `up_proj` are $4096 \times 14336$; `down_proj` is $14336 \times 4096$
- Number of transformer layers: 32

**Trainable parameter calculation for $r=16$, attention-only:**

Per attention projection: $r(d + k) = 16 \times (4096 + 4096) = 131{,}072$ params

Four attention projections per layer: $4 \times 131{,}072 = 524{,}288$ params/layer

32 layers: $32 \times 524{,}288 = 16{,}777{,}216$ trainable params ≈ **16.8M params**, or **0.21%** of 8B

**Adding MLP layers ($r=16$):**

`gate_proj` + `up_proj`: $r(4096 + 14336) = 16 \times 18432 = 294{,}912$ params each

`down_proj`: $r(14336 + 4096) = 16 \times 18432 = 294{,}912$ params

Three MLP projections per layer: $3 \times 294{,}912 = 884{,}736$ params/layer

32 layers: $32 \times 884{,}736 = 28{,}311{,}552$ ≈ **28.3M params**

Combined attention + MLP: $16.8 + 28.3 \approx 45M$ params, or **~0.56%** of 8B.

**Memory for adapter optimizer states (Adam, FP32):**

Adam stores 2 moment vectors per trainable parameter at FP32 (4 bytes each).

$45M \times 2 \times 4 \approx 360$ MB for optimizer states — negligible compared to the 14 GB base model.

**Capacity bound:** with rank $r=16$ and 7 target matrix shapes, updates live in at most a $16 \times 7 \times 32 = 3584$-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space. This constrains the optimizer but is empirically sufficient to capture most task-relevant adaptations given that fine-tuning intrinsic rank is typically far below 100.

**Effect of increasing rank:**

Doubling to $r=32$ doubles the trainable parameter count (~90M) and the optimizer state memory (~720 MB). Quality improvement: modest, typically 1–2% on most benchmarks, with diminishing returns above $r=32$. Overfitting risk: increases proportionally — important on small datasets.
:::

**Q3. When would you keep LoRA weights separate vs. merge them into the base model? Give a production scenario for each.**

> 🎯 **What great looks like:** Goes beyond "merge for latency, keep separate for multiple adapters" to give specific production scenarios with concrete tradeoffs — including the serving architecture details (how hot-swapping actually works), the storage economics, and the failure modes of each approach.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Giving a purely theoretical answer without production specifics. Senior candidates should describe actual system behavior — latency numbers, memory management, tooling (vLLM, LoRAX, S-LoRA).

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Scenario A: Merge — single-adapter, latency-critical serving**

A company fine-tunes one model for internal document Q&A and deploys it on a single endpoint. All traffic uses the same fine-tuned behavior. The correct approach:

1. Train with QLoRA on the 7B base
2. Reload base at FP16, merge with `merge_and_unload()`
3. Deploy the merged model with vLLM or TensorRT-LLM

Why merge: zero inference overhead. vLLM's continuous batching and PagedAttention work identically to an unmodified model. No LoRA-specific runtime code in the serving layer — simpler, more maintainable, compatible with any inference framework. The adapter no longer exists as a separate artifact; it's baked into the model weights.

**Scenario B: Keep separate — multi-tenant SaaS with per-customer adapters**

A startup offers a "customize your AI assistant" product. Each customer fine-tunes their own persona and knowledge base. There are 50+ customer adapters, and a customer's requests should always use their adapter.

Merging is operationally infeasible: it requires storing 50 full-size models (700 GB+ for 7B), loading a different model per customer, and cold-loading models for customers who haven't been active recently. Latency: 10–60 seconds to load a 7B model from disk.

With separate adapters:
- One base model (14 GB at FP16) loaded once, permanently warm in GPU HBM
- 50 adapters × ~30 MB = ~1.5 GB — keep all in CPU RAM
- Per-request: identify customer adapter, load adapter matrices into GPU (~5–10 ms PCIE transfer), run forward pass

Systems like vLLM's native LoRA support or Predibase's LoRAX batch requests from multiple customers together, sharing base model compute across the batch while applying different adapters per-sequence. S-LoRA demonstrated that a single A100 can serve 100+ concurrent adapters with <10% throughput penalty vs. a single merged model.

**Operational consideration:** when to regenerate adapters. With separate adapters, you can retrain only the changed customer's adapter when they update their data — the base model and all other customers' adapters are untouched. With merged models, updating one customer requires regenerating and redeploying their full model.

**Edge case:** if you use quantization for deployment (GPTQ/AWQ), you cannot merge into the quantized model — you must merge at FP16 first, then quantize the merged model. Factor this into your deployment pipeline.
:::

**Q4. Compare DoRA vs. LoRA. In what scenarios would you choose DoRA?**

> 🎯 **What great looks like:** Correctly explains the weight decomposition (magnitude + direction), explains why this gives DoRA more flexibility than LoRA, cites tasks where this matters empirically, and gives an honest assessment of when LoRA is still the better default (simpler, faster, less implementation complexity for similar quality).

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Presenting DoRA as strictly better than LoRA. The empirical evidence is nuanced — DoRA has marginal benefits on some tasks but is not consistently better across the board, and it adds complexity and training overhead.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**The DoRA decomposition.** DoRA decomposes each weight matrix $W$ as:

$$W = m \cdot \frac{V}{\|V\|}$$

where $m$ is a scalar magnitude vector (one value per column) and $V / \|V\|$ is the direction matrix (normalized columns). During fine-tuning:
- $m$ is trained directly as a learnable parameter (very few parameters — one per output dimension)
- The direction component is adapted using standard LoRA: $\Delta V = BA$

This gives DoRA two independent channels of adaptation: how much each weight column contributes (magnitude) and what direction it points (direction via LoRA).

**Why this might help.** Standard LoRA updates the weight as $W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$, which couples magnitude and direction changes. If the optimal fine-tuning update primarily requires changing the magnitude of some weight columns without significantly rotating them (or vice versa), LoRA must "waste" rank capacity on the coupled representation. DoRA separates these, allowing more efficient use of the $r$ dimensional budget.

**When DoRA empirically outperforms LoRA:**
- Tasks with large distributional shifts between pretraining and fine-tuning data (the model needs to significantly scale some output directions, not just rotate them)
- Instruction tuning tasks requiring consistent output formatting with dramatically different length distributions
- The original DoRA paper showed improvements on commonsense reasoning and visual instruction tuning benchmarks

**When LoRA is still the right default:**
- Most standard fine-tuning tasks show less than 1–2% difference between DoRA and LoRA
- DoRA adds implementation complexity: the magnitude decomposition requires custom initialization and the PEFT library's DoRA implementation has been slower and less stable than LoRA historically
- DoRA uses more memory per layer (additional magnitude vectors) — minor but non-zero
- The Hugging Face PEFT library supports DoRA (`use_dora=True` in `LoraConfig`) but it's less battle-tested than LoRA

**My recommendation:** default to LoRA. Consider DoRA only if: (a) you've tuned LoRA rank and target modules and still have a quality gap, and (b) you have empirical evidence on your specific task that DoRA closes that gap. Run a controlled A/B (same rank, same target modules, same training data) before committing to DoRA in production.
:::

**Q5. Back-of-envelope: how much GPU memory do you need to QLoRA fine-tune a 7B model on a single GPU?**

> 🎯 **What great looks like:** Walks through each memory component with formulas (not just guesses), arrives at a specific number, correctly identifies gradient checkpointing as the lever for controlling activation memory, and names what hardware is sufficient (RTX 4090, A10G, A100 40GB).

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Giving a number (e.g., "about 10 GB") without the derivation. Senior candidates need to show they can derive memory requirements for arbitrary model sizes, not just recall benchmark figures.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Let's work through each component for a 7B model with QLoRA ($r=16$, all attention + MLP layers):

**1. Base model weights (NF4, 4-bit):**
$$7 \times 10^9 \times 0.5 \text{ bytes/param} = 3.5 \text{ GB}$$

**2. NF4 dequantization buffer (BF16, for active computation):**
Transformer inference dequantizes one layer at a time. Buffer ≈ one layer's weights at BF16. For 7B with 32 layers: $3.5\text{ GB} / 32 \approx 0.11$ GB active buffer. With full layer prefetching: ~0.5–1 GB.

**3. LoRA trainable parameters (FP32):**
From Q2 above: ~45M trainable parameters for all attention + MLP at $r=16$.
$$45 \times 10^6 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 180 \text{ MB}$$

**4. Adam optimizer states for LoRA parameters (FP32, first + second moment):**
$$45 \times 10^6 \times 2 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 360 \text{ MB}$$

**5. Gradients for LoRA parameters (FP32):**
$$45 \times 10^6 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 180 \text{ MB}$$

**6. Activations (the variable component):**
Activations scale as: batch_size × sequence_length × hidden_dim × num_layers × ~4 bytes (BF16 intermediate).
For batch=4, seq=2048, hidden=4096, 32 layers: $4 \times 2048 \times 4096 \times 32 \times 2 \approx 2.1$ GB without checkpointing.

With **gradient checkpointing** (recompute activations during backward pass): reduces activation memory by ~$\sqrt{\text{layers}}$ factor, to approximately **0.5–0.8 GB** at the cost of ~30% more compute.

**Total (with gradient checkpointing):**
| Component | Memory |
|---|---|
| NF4 weights | 3.5 GB |
| Dequant buffer | ~0.5 GB |
| LoRA params + optimizer + gradients | ~0.7 GB |
| Activations (with checkpointing) | ~0.5–1 GB |
| Framework overhead (CUDA context, kernels) | ~1–2 GB |
| **Total** | **~7–9 GB** |

**Hardware sufficiency:** This fits on an **RTX 4090 (24 GB)** with large headroom, or an **A10G (24 GB)**. Even an RTX 3090 (24 GB) or RTX 3080 Ti (12 GB) can run this with aggressive gradient checkpointing and batch size 1. Without gradient checkpointing: add 2–4 GB for activations, still fits on 24 GB hardware with batch size 4.

**Sanity check:** bitsandbytes + PEFT documentation reports 4.8 GB for a 7B model with batch=1, seq=256, gradient checkpointing. At batch=4, seq=2048, my estimate of 7–9 GB is consistent with that baseline.
:::
