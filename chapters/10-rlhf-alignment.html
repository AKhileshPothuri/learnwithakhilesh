15.1 Why Alignment?

A pretrained large language model is, at its core, a next-token predictor. During pretraining the model optimizes for one objective: predict the next token in a given text sequence. The pretraining corpus contains the full spectrum of human expression — technical knowledge and productive discourse, but also misinformation, hate speech, dangerous instructions, and manipulative rhetoric. A base model will confidently complete any prompt in a statistically plausible direction, including prompts that begin “Here is a step-by-step guide to synthesizing…” or “Write a convincing argument that…”. The model has no values, no awareness of harm, and no preference for truth over plausible-sounding falsehood. It only knows what text typically follows what text.

The alignment problem is the gap between this next-token prediction objective and the objectives we actually want from deployed systems: be helpful to users, avoid causing harm, tell the truth. Bridging this gap is technically non-trivial. You cannot solve it with a system prompt — the base model does not interpret natural language directives as binding instructions. You cannot solve it with a finite labeled dataset — the space of possible inputs is effectively infinite. Alignment is a continuous engineering and research challenge, not a checkbox.

15.1.1 Helpful, Harmless, Honest (HHH)

Anthropic’s HHH framework captures three orthogonal dimensions of alignment:

  • Helpful: the model actually accomplishes what the user is trying to do — not safe-sounding non-answers. A model that refuses all requests involving chemistry or law is not helpful.
  • Harmless: the model avoids assisting with actions that have high probability of causing serious harm — synthesizing dangerous substances, generating CSAM, facilitating targeted harassment.
  • Honest: the model accurately represents its uncertainty, does not hallucinate plausible-sounding false information, and does not manipulate users through deceptive framing.

These three objectives create real tensions. A more helpful model that completes more requests is often more harmful — some requests are harmful. A more honest model that always surfaces uncertainty may be less useful for tasks requiring confident synthesis. Alignment work is fundamentally about navigating these tradeoffs at scale.

15.1.2 The Alignment Tax

Warning

The Alignment Tax: Aligned models often score slightly lower on raw capability benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval) than their base counterparts. Why? The RL or DPO training objective is not “maximize benchmark score” — it is “maximize human preference.” Aligned models spend tokens on safety hedges, caveats, and formatting that help real users but hurt benchmark metrics. This is expected and acceptable. The alignment tax is the cost of deploying a model safely. Confusing alignment-tuned models with base models on capability benchmarks leads to misleading comparisons.

15.1.3 Capability vs. Values

Instruction following and alignment are related but distinct. A model that follows instructions well will follow instructions to do harm just as readily as instructions to be helpful. GPT-3 in base form was excellent at following instructions in the technical sense. The alignment work is specifically about building in value judgments: the model should follow “write a poem about autumn” but refuse “write a phishing email.” This distinction — capability (can follow instructions) vs. values (chooses which instructions to follow) — is why pure SFT on (instruction, response) pairs is insufficient to fully align a model.

15.3 Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

SFT is the foundation. Human contractors are given a diverse set of prompts spanning the full range of user intentions — coding tasks, creative writing, factual questions, sensitive topics — and asked to write ideal responses. These (prompt, ideal_response) pairs form the SFT dataset, typically 10,000–100,000 examples for a production pipeline.

The SFT objective is standard supervised learning:

\[\mathcal{L}_{SFT} = -\sum_{t} \log P_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\]

After SFT, the model has learned the chat format, the appropriate conversational register, and a basic understanding that it should follow instructions rather than continue them.

SFT failure modes that motivate subsequent stages:

Failure Mode Root Cause Next Stage Fix
Sycophancy Annotators tend to affirm; model learns to agree RL/DPO with preference data rewarding corrections
Inconsistency Finite training prompts ≠ infinite input space Reward model generalizes over distribution
Unsafe on long-tail No coverage of every harmful prompt RL training with safety-aware reward
Poor calibration Training data didn’t model uncertainty systematically Preference data favoring honest uncertainty
Tip

Annotator quality = model quality ceiling. If contractors write verbose, meandering responses, the model learns to be verbose. If they have systematic cultural biases, those biases are encoded. Investing in annotator training and quality control is a direct investment in model quality. This is why companies like Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google have entire teams dedicated to data quality — not just volume.

15.4 Stage 2: Reward Model Training

The reward model (RM) is trained on human preference data: pairs of responses to the same prompt, with one labeled as preferred. Collection process: show annotators two model-generated responses and ask “which is better?” This comparison task is empirically superior to direct demonstration writing — humans are much better at evaluating than generating.

A production preference dataset contains hundreds of thousands of (prompt, chosen_response, rejected_response) triples.

15.4.1 Architecture

The RM is architecturally identical to the LLM being aligned, but with the final vocabulary projection layer replaced by a single linear layer producing a scalar reward score. Using the same architecture as the policy ensures the RM can score any text the policy can generate.

15.4.2 Training Objective: Bradley-Terry

The Bradley-Terry model of paired comparisons provides the training loss. For a pair where response A is preferred over response B:

\[\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))\]

where \(r_\theta\) is the reward model’s score, \(y_w\) is the winning (chosen) response, \(y_l\) is the losing (rejected) response, and \(\sigma\) is sigmoid. This pushes the RM to assign higher scores to chosen responses with a margin proportional to preference confidence.

Key properties of the Bradley-Terry formulation: - Probabilistic and well-calibrated: score differences map to calibrated preference probabilities - Handles full spectrum of preference strength: large gap for clear preferences, small for marginal - Tolerant of noisy labels: log-sigmoid loss is soft, not hard 0/1

15.4.3 Reward Model Quality

Production reward models typically achieve 70–80% accuracy on held-out preference pairs. This sounds modest but is sufficient to provide a useful training signal because:

  1. The RL optimizer runs for millions of steps — a noisy signal averaged over many steps is still informative
  2. The KL penalty prevents the policy from over-fitting to the RM’s noise
Warning

The RM is the pipeline’s bottleneck. A reward model that learned spurious correlations — long responses score higher, bullet points score higher, confident-sounding language scores higher regardless of accuracy — will lead the RL stage to produce a policy that generates long, bulleted, confident-sounding responses that humans actually find worse. This is reward hacking. The RM’s quality determines the ceiling of what RLHF can achieve.

import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForSequenceClassification, AutoTokenizer

# Reward model: same architecture as LLM, linear head replaces vocab projection
model_name = "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)

# AutoModelForSequenceClassification adds a scalar output head
reward_model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
    model_name,
    num_labels=1,  # scalar reward
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
)

def score_response(prompt: str, response: str) -> float:
    """Score a (prompt, response) pair using the trained reward model."""
    text = f"[INST] {prompt} [/INST] {response}"
    inputs = tokenizer(text, return_tensors="pt", truncation=True, max_length=2048)
    with torch.no_grad():
        outputs = reward_model(**inputs)
    # outputs.logits shape: (1, 1) — single scalar reward
    return outputs.logits.squeeze().item()

# Usage
prompt = "What is the capital of France?"
good_response = "The capital of France is Paris."
bad_response  = "France's capital is definitely London, I believe."

print(score_response(prompt, good_response))   # expect higher score
print(score_response(prompt, bad_response))    # expect lower score

15.5 Stage 3: PPO Fine-Tuning

Proximal Policy Optimization optimizes the SFT model toward higher rewards. The policy is the language model: given a prompt, it generates a response. The reward signal comes from the reward model.

15.5.1 The 4-Model Setup

PPO in the LLM context requires four models in memory simultaneously:

Model Role Updated? Memory Cost
Policy model (\(\pi_\theta\)) The model being trained Yes — every step ~16 GB for 7B bf16
Reference model (\(\pi_{SFT}\)) Frozen SFT model for KL No ~16 GB
Reward model (\(r_\phi\)) Scores generated responses No ~16 GB
Value model / critic Estimates baseline reward for advantage Yes ~16 GB

Total for a 7B setup: ~64 GB minimum VRAM. For 70B models this becomes 640 GB+, requiring multi-node setup. This memory cost is the primary engineering burden of PPO and a core motivation for DPO.

15.5.2 PPO Objective with KL Penalty

\[\mathcal{L}_{PPO} = \mathbb{E}\left[r_\theta(x, y)\right] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}\left(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{SFT}(y|x)\right)\]

The KL divergence term \(D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{SFT})\) measures how far the current policy has drifted from the SFT model in terms of output distribution. The coefficient \(\beta\) controls the tradeoff: high \(\beta\) keeps the policy conservative and close to SFT; low \(\beta\) allows more aggressive optimization. Typical values: \(\beta \in [0.01, 0.1]\).

Why KL is critical:

Without this penalty, the policy rapidly learns to exploit the reward model. Common exploit patterns: repetitive responses that score well because the RM rarely saw repetition in training; extremely long responses if the RM has any length-quality correlation; responses that begin with phrases strongly associated with high-quality demonstrations. The KL penalty prevents this by imposing a cost for moving far from the SFT model’s distribution.

Warning

What happens if KL = 0? Setting \(\beta = 0\) removes all constraint on distributional drift. The policy will rapidly find narrow response patterns that exploit the reward model’s weak spots — degenerate, repetitive, or incoherent outputs that somehow score well. This is textbook reward hacking and happens within hundreds of gradient steps. Always use a positive KL coefficient; never zero it out in production.

15.5.3 PPO Clipping

PPO’s defining feature (separate from the KL penalty) is the clipped surrogate objective that prevents the policy from making large, destabilizing updates in a single step:

\[\mathcal{L}_{clip} = \mathbb{E}_t \left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]\]

where \(r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)\) is the probability ratio and \(\hat{A}_t\) is the advantage estimate. The clip bounds (typically \(\epsilon = 0.2\)) prevent the policy from changing too much in a single update, which is the primary reason PPO is more stable than vanilla policy gradient for LLMs.

Why PPO and not simpler RL algorithms:

  • Vanilla policy gradient (REINFORCE): high variance, requires huge batch sizes to stabilize — impractical for LLMs where each forward pass is expensive
  • TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization): theoretically principled with hard KL constraint, but computationally expensive (requires second-order optimization)
  • PPO: heuristic clipping achieves similar stability to TRPO with first-order optimization — practical and well-tuned

15.5.4 Practical Stability Tricks

Trick What It Does Why Needed
Reward normalization Normalize reward scores to zero mean, unit variance RM scores have arbitrary scale; raw scores cause gradient instability
Advantage normalization Normalize advantage estimates per batch Prevents large advantage values from causing outsized gradient updates
Gradient clipping Clip global gradient norm (e.g., max_norm=1.0) RL objectives have higher gradient variance than supervised; clipping prevents divergence
Value function warmup Pretrain value function for a few steps before starting policy updates Cold value estimates produce high-variance advantage estimates in first steps
KL coefficient scheduling Start high, anneal lower Early training needs stability; later training can allow more aggressive optimization

15.6 Reward Hacking

Reward hacking occurs when the policy finds ways to maximize the reward model’s score that do not reflect genuine human preference. It is an instance of Goodhart’s Law: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

15.6.1 Common Patterns

Exploit Pattern Mechanism Detection Signal
Verbosity bias RM learned length-quality correlation; policy generates ever-longer responses Rising response length over training steps
Sycophancy Policy learns to agree with whatever the user says; RM rewards agreement Human eval: does model agree with clearly false user statements?
Prompt gaming Policy learns to mirror prompt structure back; RM rewards stylistic similarity Response starts repeating phrases from the prompt
Formatting exploitation Bullet points, headers, and bold text score well; policy over-formats Formatting ratio increases; content quality does not
Confidence exploitation RM rewards confident-sounding language; policy becomes over-confident Calibration metrics degrade; model claims certainty on uncertain topics
Prefix exploitation Phrases like “Certainly! Great question!” appear in high-scored training data; policy front-loads them Response preamble length increases

15.6.2 Detection in Production

Reward hacking detection requires monitoring the gap between RM scores and independent human evaluations:

  1. Reward vs. human preference divergence: Sample 200 responses at regular training checkpoints. Have humans rank them. Plot RM score vs. human preference score over time. A widening gap is the primary signal of hacking.
  2. Out-of-distribution analysis: Test the RM on deliberately adversarial inputs (very long responses, heavily formatted responses, sycophantic responses). If it assigns high scores, the RM has vulnerabilities the policy will exploit.
  3. Length tracking: Plot mean response length per training step. Monotonic increase without a corresponding increase in human helpfulness scores indicates verbosity exploitation.
  4. Calibration metrics: Track Brier score or Expected Calibration Error over training. Degrading calibration with increasing RM scores is a strong hacking signal.

15.6.3 Mitigation Strategies

Strategy Mechanism Tradeoff
KL penalty tuning Higher \(\beta\) limits distributional drift Reduces improvement ceiling
Ensemble reward models Train multiple RMs on different data splits; use minimum or mean score Expensive: multiple RM inference passes per training step
Constitutional constraints Add explicit rules that override RM score Requires careful constraint design; can cause over-refusal
Iterative RM retraining Collect preference data on policy’s current outputs; retrain RM Expensive; requires continuous human annotation
Output length constraints Hard cap on response length at training time May be too restrictive for complex tasks
Tip

The best single mitigation is a good reward model. The stronger the RM’s generalization (more diverse training data, better architecture, better annotation quality), the fewer exploitable weak spots exist. Ensemble RMs are the gold standard for production systems where reward hacking tolerance is low. For most practitioners, investing in RM quality before tuning KL is the higher-leverage move.

15.7 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) is a fundamental rethinking of how to use preference data. The key insight is that the reward model is implicit in the policy ratio — you do not need to train a separate RM or run an RL loop.

15.7.1 The Mathematical Insight

In RLHF, the optimal policy under a KL-constrained reward objective has a closed-form solution:

\[\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{\pi_{SFT}(y|x)}{Z(x)} \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r^*(x,y)\right)\]

where \(Z(x)\) is the partition function. Rearranging to solve for the reward:

\[r^*(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{SFT}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\]

Plugging this into the Bradley-Terry preference model and noting that \(\log Z(x)\) cancels in the pairwise comparison, you get the DPO loss directly:

\[\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]\]

Intuitively: DPO increases the relative log-probability of the chosen response and decreases the relative log-probability of the rejected response, with both measured as ratios against the SFT reference policy. The reference policy prevents the model from trivially satisfying the objective by assigning high probability to everything.

15.7.2 Why DPO Eliminates the RL Loop

The loss is a standard binary cross-entropy computed from (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples using only the policy being trained and a frozen reference model. There is no: - Separate reward model to train - RL sampling loop - Value model / critic - PPO-specific hyperparameters (clip ratio, GAE lambda, value function coefficient)

This reduces the 4-model PPO setup to 2 models and makes training as stable as instruction tuning.

15.7.3 DPO Training with TRL

from datasets import Dataset
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model_name = "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct"

# Load policy model and reference model (frozen SFT model)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name, torch_dtype="auto")
model_ref = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name, torch_dtype="auto")
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token

# Preference dataset format: prompt, chosen, rejected
# Each field is a list of chat messages (role/content dicts)
preference_data = [
    {
        "prompt": "What is the boiling point of water?",
        "chosen": "Water boils at 100°C (212°F) at standard atmospheric pressure (1 atm).",
        "rejected": "Great question! Water, that wonderful molecule H2O, boils at around 100 degrees Celsius, though this can vary based on many factors such as altitude, atmospheric pressure, dissolved solids, and many other variables that science has studied for centuries.",
    },
    # ... more preference pairs
]
dataset = Dataset.from_list(preference_data)

# DPO configuration
training_args = DPOConfig(
    output_dir="./dpo-output",
    num_train_epochs=2,
    per_device_train_batch_size=4,
    gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
    learning_rate=5e-7,          # lower than SFT; DPO is sensitive to LR
    beta=0.1,                    # KL regularization coefficient
    max_length=1024,
    max_prompt_length=512,
    remove_unused_columns=False,
    logging_steps=10,
    warmup_ratio=0.1,
    lr_scheduler_type="cosine",
    bf16=True,
)

trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model=model,
    ref_model=model_ref,         # frozen reference (SFT model)
    args=training_args,
    train_dataset=dataset,
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
)

trainer.train()

# Monitor training: watch for these signals
# - dpo/chosen_rewards should increase over training
# - dpo/rejected_rewards should decrease over training
# - dpo/reward_margin (chosen - rejected) should widen
# - If reward_margin collapses: LR too high or beta too low

15.7.4 DPO Limitations

Warning

DPO distributional mismatch: DPO’s preference pairs should ideally be generated by the same model you are training (the SFT model). If preference pairs were generated by a different, stronger model (e.g., GPT-4 generated both chosen and rejected responses, but you are training a 7B model), distributional mismatch reduces training signal quality. The model is learning to prefer responses in a distribution it would not naturally generate. For best results: generate preference pairs by sampling from your specific SFT model, not from off-the-shelf datasets generated by other models.

15.7.5 DPO vs. PPO vs. GRPO

Method Models Needed Complexity Quality Best For
PPO 4 (policy + ref + RM + value) High — RL loop, many hyperparams High ceiling, unstable Online RL, iterative RLHF, when you can afford it
DPO 2 (policy + ref) Low — supervised loss Competitive with PPO Default choice for most preference alignment tasks
GRPO 2 (policy + ref) Medium — group sampling Strong for reasoning Math/code tasks with verifiable rewards

15.8 GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) is the RL algorithm used in DeepSeek-R1. It is a variant of PPO that removes the value model by using group statistics as the baseline for advantage estimation.

15.8.1 The Key Idea

Standard PPO estimates advantage as \(\hat{A}_t = r_t - V_\phi(s_t)\), where \(V_\phi\) is a learned value function that estimates expected future reward. Training this value function requires the 4th model in the PPO setup.

GRPO replaces the value function with a group baseline. For a given prompt \(x\), sample a group of \(G\) responses \(\{y_1, ..., y_G\}\) from the current policy. The advantage for response \(y_i\) is:

\[\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\{r_1,...,r_G\})}{\text{std}(\{r_1,...,r_G\})}\]

The mean and standard deviation are computed within the group for that prompt — no separate value model needed. This eliminates an entire model from the training setup while still providing a normalized advantage signal.

The GRPO objective:

\[\mathcal{L}_{GRPO} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\left(r_i(\theta)\hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_i(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_i\right) - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{ref})\right]\]

15.8.2 Why GRPO Works for Reasoning

GRPO excels on tasks with verifiable rewards — math problems, code generation, structured output generation. For a math problem, you can automatically check whether the answer is correct. This produces a binary or sparse but accurate reward signal. With group sampling, even a sparse signal is informative: if 3 out of 8 responses in the group get the answer right, the model can learn from the contrast between those 3 and the other 5. This dense-signal-from-sparse-reward property is why DeepSeek-R1 used GRPO for mathematics reasoning training.

Tip

GRPO vs. PPO for your use case: - If your reward is verifiable (math answers, code test pass/fail, structured JSON validity): GRPO is simpler and effective. - If your reward requires a trained RM (open-ended quality judgments, style, safety): PPO or DPO is more appropriate since the RM provides a dense signal and GRPO’s group statistics work less well with noisy RM scores.

15.9 Constitutional AI (CAI)

Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) is Anthropic’s approach to alignment that replaces human preference labels with AI-generated feedback guided by an explicit set of principles — the “constitution.”

The constitution is a list of named principles: - “Do not assist with creating weapons of mass destruction” - “Respect human autonomy; avoid being paternalistic” - “Be transparent about uncertainty” - “Do not deceive users, even through technically true statements”

These principles are auditable and revisable. This is a significant advantage over RLHF, where alignment values are implicitly encoded in the preferences of human annotators who may have their own biases.

15.9.1 Two-Phase Pipeline

Phase 1 — Supervised Learning from AI Feedback (SL-CAI): 1. Model generates responses to potentially harmful prompts 2. Model critiques its own responses against the constitutional principles (identifying which principles were violated and why) 3. Model revises the response to better comply with the principles 4. Fine-tune on (prompt, revised_response) pairs — no human labels needed

Phase 2 — RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback): 1. Sample response pairs for a given prompt 2. Ask the AI (guided by constitutional principles) to judge which response better follows the constitution 3. Train a reward model from these AI-generated preference labels 4. Run standard RL training with the AI-labeled reward model

15.9.2 Scalability Advantage

Human preference annotation is expensive: hundreds of skilled annotators working for months to produce a large, high-quality preference dataset. AI-generated feedback scales essentially unboundedly — the primary expense is inference compute, not human labor. This scalability enables a much larger and more diverse preference dataset.

Tip

RLAIF quality: Empirical comparisons find that CAI-trained models produce outputs that humans rate similarly to or better than models trained with human feedback, provided the AI rater is sufficiently capable. The practical conclusion: for most alignment tasks, RLAIF with a strong AI rater (Claude 3, GPT-4) is a viable and scalable alternative to human annotation RLHF.

15.10 Preference Data Quality

What makes good preference data is often underspecified in papers. Here is what actually matters in production:

15.10.1 Criteria for Good Preference Pairs

  1. Clear preference margin: Ambiguous pairs (where a reasonable annotator might go either way) add noise rather than signal. Filter pairs with low inter-annotator agreement.
  2. Diverse prompts: A preference dataset concentrated on common prompts produces a reward model that is well-calibrated on common cases but fails on edge cases. Actively ensure diversity: topic diversity, prompt length diversity, difficulty diversity, safety topic diversity.
  3. Response diversity: Both chosen and rejected responses should span a range of qualities. Pairs where one response is obviously excellent and the other obviously terrible are easy cases — the model learns less from them than from hard cases.
  4. Consistent annotators: Individual annotators drift over time and differ from each other. Annotator ID is a confounding variable in preference data. Use inter-rater agreement as a data quality metric, not just as an annotator quality metric.

15.10.2 Inter-Annotator Agreement

Common issues: - Fleiss’s Kappa < 0.4 on a category: The annotation instructions are ambiguous for this category; revise the rubric. - One annotator is systematically different: That annotator may have a different value system or may be misunderstanding the rubric; review and possibly exclude. - Agreement decreases over time: Annotators are fatiguing or drifting; run calibration sessions.

15.12 Interview Questions

15.12.1 Entry Level

Q1. What is RLHF and why is it used instead of just fine-tuning on demonstrations?

Model Answer

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a training technique that fine-tunes language models using human preference signals rather than static labeled data.

Pure supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations produces models that are inconsistent, sometimes unsafe, and prone to sycophancy. Human preference data captures subtle quality distinctions that are difficult to specify as explicit instructions: it is easier for a human to say “response A is better than response B” than to write out all the rules that make A better. RLHF uses preference data in two ways: first to train a reward model that can score any (prompt, response) pair, then to optimize the language model with RL to generate responses that the reward model rates highly. The result is a model steered toward patterns humans prefer, covering the vast space of possible inputs more effectively than finite demonstration datasets.

Weak answer: “RLHF makes the model better.” — No specifics on why SFT alone fails or how the preference signal is used.

Strong answer: Names the SFT failure modes (sycophancy, inconsistency, long-tail safety gaps), explains the two-stage use of preference data (RM training, then RL), and connects this to why base LLMs need alignment at all.

Q2. What is “helpful, harmless, honest” and why are these objectives in tension?

Model Answer

HHH is Anthropic’s framework for the three dimensions of alignment:

  • Helpful: actually completes what the user needs, not evasive non-answers
  • Harmless: avoids assisting with requests that cause serious harm
  • Honest: accurate uncertainty representation, no hallucination, no manipulation

They are in tension because optimizing strongly for one often hurts another. A maximally helpful model completes any request — including harmful ones. A maximally harmless model refuses all borderline requests — becoming useless. A maximally honest model says “I don’t know” to everything uncertain — becoming unhelpful for tasks requiring synthesis. Real alignment is about finding the Pareto frontier across all three, not maximizing any one in isolation. Over-refusal (too much harmlessness at the expense of helpfulness) is a well-documented alignment failure mode that product teams must track as carefully as under-refusal.

Q3. What is a reward model and what is it trained on?

Model Answer

A reward model is a neural network trained to predict human preference for a (prompt, response) pair, outputting a scalar quality score. It has the same architecture as the LLM being aligned, but the final vocabulary projection layer is replaced by a single linear layer that outputs a scalar reward.

It is trained on human preference data: pairs of responses to the same prompt, with one response labeled as preferred by human annotators. The training loss is the Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison objective: minimize \(-\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))\), where \(y_w\) is the preferred response. After training, the reward model generalizes to score any (prompt, response) pair it has never seen before — this generalization is what makes RL training tractable.

Q4. What is reward hacking?

Model Answer

Reward hacking occurs when the policy finds response patterns that score highly on the reward model without actually being better responses. It is an instance of Goodhart’s Law: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

The reward model was trained on finite preference data and has learned some spurious correlations alongside genuine quality signals. An RL optimizer is powerful enough to identify and exploit those correlations. Classic examples: if the reward model has a length-quality correlation from training data where annotators preferred more detailed answers, the policy learns to generate excessively long responses. If formatting (bullet points, headers) was associated with quality in the training data, the policy learns to over-format. These responses score well on the reward model but humans find them worse than the SFT baseline. The KL divergence penalty is the primary mitigation: it constrains the policy to stay near the SFT model’s distribution, making it expensive to reach the extreme regions where reward model exploits live.

Q5. What does the KL penalty do in PPO, and what happens if you set it to zero?

Model Answer

The KL penalty adds a term to the PPO training objective that penalizes the policy for diverging from the reference SFT model: \(\mathcal{L} = E[r(x,y)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{SFT})\). It imposes a cost for generating a response distribution that differs significantly from the SFT model’s distribution.

Setting \(\beta = 0\) removes all constraint on distributional drift. The RL optimizer will rapidly find the narrow set of response patterns that exploit the reward model’s weak spots — degenerate, repetitive, or incoherent outputs that happen to score well. This happens within hundreds of gradient steps. The KL penalty also prevents mode collapse, where the policy generates only a small set of high-reward response patterns for all prompts, losing the diversity and task-specific responsiveness the SFT model had. Always use a positive KL coefficient.

Weak answer: “It keeps the model from changing too much.” — Correct but misses why (reward hacking, mode collapse) and the \(\beta = 0\) consequence.

Strong answer: Names reward hacking and mode collapse as the two failure modes, explains the \(\beta\) tradeoff (stability vs. improvement ceiling), and knows typical values (\(0.01–0.1\)).

Q6. What is DPO and how does it differ from RLHF with PPO?

Model Answer

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a technique that learns from preference pairs directly without training a separate reward model or running an RL loop. It derives mathematically that the optimal RLHF policy can be expressed in terms of the policy’s own log-probability ratios on chosen vs. rejected responses relative to a reference policy. This yields a simple binary cross-entropy loss computed directly from (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples.

PPO requires 4 models (policy, reference, reward model, value function) and a complex RL training loop with many hyperparameters and common failure modes. DPO requires only 2 models (policy, frozen reference) and uses a standard supervised loss that is as stable as instruction tuning. DPO is competitive with PPO on most benchmarks and has largely replaced it as the default approach because the engineering complexity reduction is substantial with minimal quality cost.

15.12.2 Mid Level

Q1. Walk through the three stages of RLHF. What does each stage contribute and what are its failure modes?

Model Answer

Stage 1 — SFT: human contractors write ideal responses to diverse prompts; base model fine-tuned on (prompt, ideal_response) pairs. Teaches chat format, instruction following, and basic behavioral patterns. Failure modes: sycophancy (annotators tend to affirm), inconsistency (finite training data ≠ infinite input space), poor calibration, safety gaps on long-tail inputs not covered in demonstrations.

Stage 2 — Reward Model: human raters compare response pairs, labeling which is better. Reward model (same architecture as LLM + scalar head) trained on Bradley-Terry loss to predict preference. Learns a general quality signal covering unseen inputs. Failure mode: reward model learns spurious correlations (length, formatting, confidence) that the RL stage will exploit.

Stage 3 — PPO: policy generates responses, RM scores them, policy gradient updates increase probability of high-reward responses. KL penalty against SFT model prevents reward hacking and mode collapse. Failure modes: reward collapse, mode collapse, gradient instability. Each stage builds on the prior; SFT quality ceiling propagates to RM and then to PPO.

Follow-up: Why does SFT quality matter for Stage 3?

The reward model is trained on preference comparisons between SFT model outputs. If the SFT model generates low-quality responses, the preference comparisons are between responses that are all poor, and the reward model learns to discriminate within a low-quality distribution. The RL stage then optimizes toward the top of this low-quality distribution rather than toward genuinely good responses. SFT quality is a bottleneck for the entire pipeline.

Q2. Explain the DPO loss function and the role of the reference policy. What happens without it?

Model Answer

DPO loss: \[\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\mathbb{E} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]\]

The loss increases the relative log-probability of the chosen response \(y_w\) and decreases it for the rejected response \(y_l\), with both measured as ratios against the reference policy \(\pi_{ref}\) (the frozen SFT model).

The role of the reference policy: it normalizes the probability ratios to prevent a degenerate solution where the model trivially satisfies the objective by increasing all response probabilities equally. Without the reference policy, the gradient direction is “make all tokens in chosen response more likely” — the model could satisfy this by becoming overconfident across the board without genuinely preferring the chosen response over the rejected one. The reference policy ensures the model must increase the probability of chosen responses relative to what the SFT model would have assigned, not in absolute terms.

Follow-up: What is the \(\beta\) parameter in DPO?

\(\beta\) is the KL regularization strength — the same role as the KL coefficient in PPO. High \(\beta\): policy stays close to reference, more conservative updates. Low \(\beta\): allows larger distributional shift toward the preference signal. Typical range: 0.01–0.5. Unlike PPO where \(\beta\) is an explicit penalty term, in DPO \(\beta\) appears inside the log-sigmoid, scaling the log-probability ratios before comparison. It can be interpreted as the implicit temperature of the reward model implicit in the DPO objective.

Q3. Your company is considering DPO vs. PPO for an alignment task. What factors drive the decision?

Model Answer

Choose DPO when: - Your reward signal comes from static preference data (you have a fixed dataset of comparisons) - Engineering team does not have RL infrastructure - Stability is more important than squeezing out the last bit of quality - Training is batch/offline — no need for online exploration

Choose PPO when: - You need online RL: the policy should generate its own responses and receive reward on those (iterative RLHF) - Your reward model is trained on diverse data and is high quality — in this case the RL signal from PPO can exceed DPO - You need to combine multiple reward signals dynamically (safety reward + helpfulness reward weighted adaptively) - You are doing tasks where exploration during training matters (novel prompt distributions)

In practice: DPO is the right default. Most production alignment pipelines use DPO because the quality gap vs. PPO is small, and the engineering complexity reduction is substantial. PPO is reserved for research settings or production systems with mature RL infrastructure and tasks that genuinely require online exploration.

Q4. How do you detect and mitigate reward hacking in a production RLHF pipeline?

Model Answer

Detection strategy — three metrics to monitor simultaneously:

  1. RM score vs. human preference divergence: at regular checkpoints (every 500–1000 training steps), sample ~100 responses and have humans rate them. Plot RM score and human preference score as paired time series. Widening gap signals the policy is finding RM exploits that humans don’t actually prefer.

  2. Response length tracking: plot mean token count per training step. Monotonic increase without a corresponding human helpfulness increase = verbosity exploitation. Set a soft threshold (e.g., if mean length increases >30% from SFT baseline without improvement in human eval).

  3. Out-of-distribution RM probing: test the RM on deliberately adversarial inputs — copy-pasted prompt fragments, extremely long padding, sycophantic preambles. If these score in the top 20th percentile, the RM has vulnerabilities the policy will eventually find.

Mitigation toolkit:

Severity Mitigation Speed
Mild (early signs) Increase KL \(\beta\) coefficient Fast — change training config
Moderate Collect preference data specifically on the exploit pattern; retrain RM Medium — 1-2 days
Severe Roll back to earlier checkpoint; redesign RM training data Slow — days to weeks
Ongoing Add ensemble RM: score = min(RM1, RM2, RM3); hard to game all simultaneously Expensive but robust 
# Reward hacking detector: track RM score vs. human eval gap
import numpy as np

def detect_reward_hacking(
    rm_scores: list[float],        # RM scores at each checkpoint
    human_scores: list[float],     # Human eval scores at same checkpoints
    threshold: float = 0.15,       # Alert if divergence > 15% of score range
) -> bool:
    rm_arr = np.array(rm_scores)
    human_arr = np.array(human_scores)

    # Normalize both to [0, 1] for comparison
    rm_norm = (rm_arr - rm_arr.min()) / (rm_arr.max() - rm_arr.min() + 1e-8)
    human_norm = (human_arr - human_arr.min()) / (human_arr.max() - human_arr.min() + 1e-8)

    divergence = rm_norm[-1] - human_norm[-1]  # positive = RM inflated vs human

    if divergence > threshold:
        print(f"WARNING: Reward hacking detected. RM-Human divergence: {divergence:.3f}")
        return True
    return False

Q5. What makes preference data high-quality? How do you quality-control an annotation pipeline?

Model Answer

Characteristics of high-quality preference data:

  • Clear preference margin: ambiguous pairs add noise; filter by inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss’s kappa < 0.4 for a category = annotation rubric is ambiguous)
  • Diverse prompts: topic diversity, length diversity, difficulty diversity, safety topic diversity — prevents RM from overfitting to common cases
  • Response diversity: pairs should span a range of quality levels, not just “obviously excellent vs. obviously terrible”
  • Distribution match: pairs generated by the model being trained (not a stronger model) to avoid distributional mismatch

QC mechanisms:

  1. Annotator qualification: test raters on a gold-standard set with known-correct answers before labeling real data; exclude those scoring below threshold
  2. Regular calibration sessions: weekly alignment meetings to discuss ambiguous cases and recalibrate against the rubric; prevents individual drift
  3. Adversarial quality checks: periodically inject known-bad responses; annotators who consistently fail to reject them are flagged
  4. Gold-standard injection: include pairs with established ground-truth preference in live annotation batches; monitor each annotator’s accuracy over time
  5. Agreement tracking: compute pairwise kappa between annotators; flag pairs with high disagreement for review; use flag rate as a rubric quality signal

Follow-up: How do you handle disagreement?

Multiple strategies: (1) majority vote — use the more common label; (2) aggregate with confidence weighting — pairs with high agreement get higher weight in training; (3) treat high-disagreement pairs as a separate training signal (ambiguous preferences); (4) exclude and revise the rubric. In practice, a combination of majority vote for training and exclusion for very low-agreement pairs (< 60% agreement) works well.

Q2. How do you detect reward hacking in production? Walk me through monitoring and mitigation.

Model Answer

Detection — three-layer monitoring system:

Layer 1 — Automated metrics (continuous): - Response length distribution: track mean and 95th-percentile token count per training checkpoint. Alert on >25% increase from SFT baseline. - Formatting density: track fraction of response tokens that are markdown symbols (*, #, -, etc.). Alert on monotonic increase. - Preamble length: length of response before first substantive token. Alert on increase (proxy for prefix exploitation). - These run cheaply on every training checkpoint with no human involvement.

Layer 2 — RM vs. human panel divergence (weekly): - Sample 200 responses at current checkpoint. Have a panel of 3-5 human raters score them on a 1-5 helpfulness scale. - Plot RM score vs. human panel score normalized to [0,1]. Alert on gap > 0.15. - This is the primary signal for reward hacking. It is expensive ($10-50 per 200 samples) but is the only reliable ground truth.

Layer 3 — RM adversarial probing (at RM training time): - Before deploying a new reward model, probe it with known adversarial inputs: padding attacks (responses with many filler words), sycophantic preambles, copy-paste responses. - If any adversarial class scores in the top quartile, the RM has a vulnerability. Document it before it becomes a policy exploit.

Mitigation — ordered by speed:

  1. Increase \(\beta\) (hours): conservative immediate fix, reduces improvement ceiling.
  2. Collect targeted preference data on the specific exploit pattern (1-2 days): generate policy’s current exploit responses as rejected, human-written or SFT responses as chosen; retrain RM or run a correction DPO pass.
  3. Ensemble RM (1 week): train 3 reward models on different data splits; score = minimum. Hard to exploit because the policy would need to fool all three simultaneously.
  4. Constitutional constraint injection (1 week): add a constitutional principle that explicitly penalizes the exploit pattern (e.g., “responses should not use excessive formatting”).
  5. Checkpoint rollback (immediate): if hacking is severe, roll back to last known-good checkpoint and redesign before retraining.

What great looks like: Candidate has a multi-layer detection system (not just “monitor reward”), knows the RM-human divergence panel is the ground truth signal, has a tiered mitigation strategy ordered by speed/cost, and can explain why ensemble RMs are robust.

Common Mistake: Describing reward hacking only as “model generates long responses.” This is one symptom. Great candidates name the full taxonomy (verbosity, sycophancy, formatting exploitation, prefix exploitation, confidence exploitation) and explain detection at the metric level, not just the anecdote level.

Q3. Compare PPO, DPO, and GRPO. When would you choose each?

Model Answer

PPO: - 4 models in memory: policy, reference (SFT), reward model, value function - Online RL: policy generates responses at training time, receives reward, updates - High complexity: clip ratio, KL coefficient, value function architecture, advantage normalization — all need tuning - High ceiling when reward model is good and pipeline is stable; can match or exceed DPO with sufficient engineering - Choose when: mature RL infrastructure available; task requires online exploration; iterative RLHF where policy generates its own preference data

DPO: - 2 models: policy + frozen reference - Offline: trains on a static preference dataset - Supervised loss, same stability as instruction tuning; minimal hyperparameter tuning - Competitive with PPO on most benchmarks; occasional quality gap on tasks requiring exploration - Choose when: default choice for preference alignment; no RL infrastructure; stability required; batch/offline setting

GRPO: - 2 models: policy + frozen reference (no value model) - Online-ish: samples G responses per prompt at training time; uses within-group statistics as advantage baseline - Group sampling provides normalized advantage without value function: \(\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})) / \text{std}(\mathbf{r})\) - Best signal when reward is verifiable (binary correct/incorrect): group sampling naturally produces the contrast between correct and incorrect responses needed for learning - Choose when: math/code tasks with verifiable rewards; want simpler RL setup than PPO; DeepSeek-R1-style reasoning training

The decision tree in practice: 1. Is my reward verifiable (math, code)? → GRPO 2. Do I have static preference data and no RL infrastructure? → DPO 3. Do I need online exploration or iterative RLHF and have engineering resources? → PPO 4. Default: DPO

What great looks like: Candidate explains the structural difference (number of models, online vs. offline, value function vs. group statistics), connects GRPO’s group sampling to its suitability for verifiable rewards, and gives a concrete decision framework rather than listing features abstractly.

Common Mistake: Saying “GRPO is just PPO without a value model” without explaining why that works — i.e., how the within-group statistics replace the value function baseline and why this requires multiple samples per prompt. The mechanism matters, not just the feature.

Q4. What is the KL divergence penalty in RLHF and what happens if you set it to zero?

Model Answer

The KL divergence penalty is an additive term in the PPO training objective:

\[\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{SFT}(y|x))\]

It measures how far the current policy’s output distribution has drifted from the SFT reference model’s distribution, and penalizes drift proportionally. \(\beta\) is the coefficient controlling the tradeoff strength.

Mechanistic role: The reward model is an imperfect proxy for human preference. It was trained on finite data and has learned some spurious correlations. The KL penalty constrains the policy to generate text in approximately the same distribution as the SFT model — where the reward model was well-calibrated. Reward model exploits typically exist far from the SFT model’s natural distribution (degenerate repetitions, extreme formatting, implausible confidence). The KL penalty makes these regions expensive to reach.

Setting \(\beta = 0\): the policy is now free to drift arbitrarily from the SFT model. Within hundreds of gradient steps, the optimizer finds reward model weak spots and exploits them aggressively. In practice: responses become repetitive or degenerate, mode collapse occurs (policy generates the same high-reward template for all prompts), and reward scores spike upward while human evaluations tank. Recovery requires rolling back to an earlier checkpoint.

The \(\beta\) tuning tradeoff: - High \(\beta\) (0.1+): stable training, conservative improvement, policy stays close to SFT - Low \(\beta\) (0.01–0.05): more aggressive optimization, higher ceiling, higher risk of hacking - Curriculum approach: start at high \(\beta\) for training stability, anneal lower as the policy is well-behaved

In DPO: the KL constraint is incorporated implicitly — the \(\beta\) parameter in the DPO loss serves the same role, scaling the log-probability ratios. The key difference: in DPO there is no separate KL term to zero out; \(\beta\) is deeply embedded in the loss structure, making it harder to accidentally remove.

Connection to reward hacking: the KL penalty is necessary but not sufficient to prevent reward hacking. With a strong enough reward model exploit, even high \(\beta\) cannot prevent the policy from finding it — because the exploit is in a region the SFT model naturally generates, so there is no KL cost to reach it. This is why diverse preference data and ensemble reward models are the long-term solution; KL is the short-term stabilizer.

What great looks like: Candidate covers all three aspects — what the KL penalty is mechanistically, the \(\beta = 0\) consequence (names reward hacking and mode collapse, gives a timeline “within hundreds of steps”), and the tuning tradeoff. Bonus points for explaining why KL is necessary but not sufficient and connecting it to reward model quality.

Common Mistake: Answering only “it keeps the model from changing too much” without naming the specific failure modes prevented (reward hacking, mode collapse) or explaining what “too much” means mathematically. The KL penalty is not a vague stability trick — it has a precise meaning and a precise failure mode when absent.

Q5. A fine-tuned model is very helpful but will answer dangerous requests if asked cleverly. What alignment steps would you take?

Model Answer

This is safety alignment erosion — a well-documented phenomenon where domain-specific SFT inadvertently removes safety behaviors if the fine-tuning data does not include safety-relevant examples.

Step 1 — Audit the failure mode. Characterize systematically what types of “clever” requests succeed. Is it roleplay framing? Multi-step indirect requests? Multilingual pivots? Hypothetical framing? This characterization determines what data you need. Use structured red-teaming across all known jailbreak categories: direct requests, roleplay, hypothetical scenarios, indirect multi-turn, code-based obfuscation.

Step 2 — Collect targeted safety preference data. For each successful attack pattern, generate response pairs: a compliant response to the harmful request (rejected) and a high-quality refusal that is firm but not preachy and offers alternatives where possible (chosen). Generate 500–2,000 pairs spanning all identified attack patterns. Quality bar: the chosen responses must be reviewed by safety-domain experts — a poor refusal that is overly broad or condescending teaches the model to be unhelpful, not just safe.

Step 3 — DPO on safety preference data. Fine-tune using DPO with these pairs, using the current fine-tuned model as both the reference policy and the training starting point. Run 1–3 epochs. Monitor two metrics simultaneously: attack success rate (should decrease sharply) and helpfulness on legitimate domain queries (should not meaningfully decrease). An increase in over-refusal rate (declining legitimate requests) is a failure mode as serious as insufficient safety.

Step 4 — Application-layer guardrails. Safety fine-tuning improves robustness but does not eliminate all attack vectors. Add an input safety classifier (Llama Guard, ShieldLLM, or equivalent) to catch known attack patterns before they reach the model. Add output monitoring to catch safety failures that reach the response stage. These are complementary defenses, not substitutes for model-level alignment.

Step 5 — Regression testing in CI/CD. Safety behaviors can regress when new task data is added. Include a safety evaluation suite (attack success rate, over-refusal rate) in the CI/CD gate for every model update. Treat a safety regression the same way you treat a performance regression — it blocks release.

What great looks like: Candidate treats this as a systematic problem requiring characterization before intervention, not “just add more RLHF.” Names DPO as the correct tool (simpler than PPO for targeted behavioral correction), insists on measuring over-refusal as well as under-safety, and includes application-layer guardrails and regression testing.

Common Mistake: Recommending “more RLHF” or “more safety training data” without specifying the attack characterization step. Pouring generic safety data on top of a model with a specific attack pattern vulnerability does not reliably fix the specific pattern — you need targeted data for the specific attack vectors that succeed.

Q6. Explain Constitutional AI’s self-critique loop. How does it produce preference data without human labelers?

Model Answer

Constitutional AI produces preference data through two mechanisms, both driven by the model itself rather than human labelers:

Phase 1 — Supervised Learning from AI Feedback (SL-CAI):

The model is given potentially harmful prompts and asked to (1) generate an initial response, (2) critique that response against each constitutional principle it violated (e.g., “This response provides detailed instructions for harm, violating the principle against assisting with dangerous activities”), and (3) revise the response to comply with the violated principles. The final revised response is higher quality — the model used its language understanding to identify its own misalignments and generate corrected examples. Fine-tuning on (prompt, revised_response) pairs produces a model with better safety behavior without any human having labeled a preference.

Phase 2 — RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback):

Given two model responses, an AI rater (prompted with the constitutional principles) judges which response better follows the constitution. These AI-generated pairwise preference labels train a reward model in the same way human preference labels do. The constitutional prompting provides consistent, principled guidance to the AI rater, producing more internally consistent preference labels than human annotators who may have different intuitions.

Why this works: the AI rater needs to be sufficiently capable to understand and apply the constitutional principles. With a strong AI rater (Claude 3, GPT-4 level), the preference labels are high-quality. With a weak AI rater, the labels are noisy and the reward model learns inconsistent preferences.

Scalability advantage: preference label generation scales with inference compute. A production-scale RLAIF pipeline can generate millions of preference pairs per week; an equivalent human annotation pipeline would require thousands of annotators working continuously. This is why Anthropic trained Claude with Constitutional AI rather than pure human-annotation RLHF — the scale simply could not be matched with human labor.

Transparency advantage: the constitution is a document you can read, debate, and revise. When the model behaves unexpectedly, you can audit which constitutional principles it was trained on and identify which principles may need updating. This auditability is impossible with implicit annotator preferences.

What great looks like: Candidate clearly explains both phases (SL-CAI and RLAIF as distinct), explains why the self-critique mechanism works (model uses its language understanding to identify its own misalignments), and articulates both the scalability and transparency advantages with specific comparisons to human-annotation RLHF.

Common Mistake: Describing Constitutional AI as just “the model teaches itself to be safe” without explaining the two-phase structure or how preference data is actually generated. The self-critique loop is a specific mechanism, not a vague self-improvement process.

## Why Alignment?

A pretrained large language model is, at its core, a next-token predictor. During pretraining the model optimizes for one objective: predict the next token in a given text sequence. The pretraining corpus contains the full spectrum of human expression — technical knowledge and productive discourse, but also misinformation, hate speech, dangerous instructions, and manipulative rhetoric. A base model will confidently complete any prompt in a statistically plausible direction, including prompts that begin "Here is a step-by-step guide to synthesizing..." or "Write a convincing argument that...". The model has no values, no awareness of harm, and no preference for truth over plausible-sounding falsehood. It only knows what text typically follows what text.

**The alignment problem** is the gap between this next-token prediction objective and the objectives we actually want from deployed systems: be helpful to users, avoid causing harm, tell the truth. Bridging this gap is technically non-trivial. You cannot solve it with a system prompt — the base model does not interpret natural language directives as binding instructions. You cannot solve it with a finite labeled dataset — the space of possible inputs is effectively infinite. Alignment is a continuous engineering and research challenge, not a checkbox.

### Helpful, Harmless, Honest (HHH)

Anthropic's HHH framework captures three orthogonal dimensions of alignment:

- **Helpful**: the model actually accomplishes what the user is trying to do — not safe-sounding non-answers. A model that refuses all requests involving chemistry or law is not helpful.
- **Harmless**: the model avoids assisting with actions that have high probability of causing serious harm — synthesizing dangerous substances, generating CSAM, facilitating targeted harassment.
- **Honest**: the model accurately represents its uncertainty, does not hallucinate plausible-sounding false information, and does not manipulate users through deceptive framing.

These three objectives create real tensions. A more helpful model that completes more requests is often more harmful — some requests are harmful. A more honest model that always surfaces uncertainty may be less useful for tasks requiring confident synthesis. Alignment work is fundamentally about navigating these tradeoffs at scale.

### The Alignment Tax

::: {.callout-warning}
**The Alignment Tax:** Aligned models often score slightly lower on raw capability benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval) than their base counterparts. Why? The RL or DPO training objective is not "maximize benchmark score" — it is "maximize human preference." Aligned models spend tokens on safety hedges, caveats, and formatting that help real users but hurt benchmark metrics. This is expected and acceptable. The alignment tax is the cost of deploying a model safely. Confusing alignment-tuned models with base models on capability benchmarks leads to misleading comparisons.
:::

### Capability vs. Values

Instruction following and alignment are related but distinct. A model that follows instructions well will follow instructions to do harm just as readily as instructions to be helpful. GPT-3 in base form was excellent at following instructions in the technical sense. The alignment work is specifically about building in value judgments: the model should follow "write a poem about autumn" but refuse "write a phishing email." This distinction — capability (can follow instructions) vs. values (chooses which instructions to follow) — is why pure SFT on (instruction, response) pairs is insufficient to fully align a model.

## The RLHF Pipeline

The full pipeline has three sequential stages. Each stage is a prerequisite for the next.

```
Base LLM
    │
    ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
│   Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning   │
│   Human writes ideal responses      │
│   Standard cross-entropy training   │
└─────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                  │
                  ▼  SFT Model
┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
│   Stage 2: Reward Model Training    │
│   Human compares A vs B             │
│   Bradley-Terry pairwise loss       │
│   Output: scalar quality scorer     │
└─────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                  │
                  ▼  Reward Model
┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
│   Stage 3: PPO Fine-Tuning          │
│   Policy generates → RM scores →   │
│   Policy gradient update           │
│   KL penalty against SFT model     │
└─────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                  │
                  ▼
            Aligned Chat Model
```

## Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

SFT is the foundation. Human contractors are given a diverse set of prompts spanning the full range of user intentions — coding tasks, creative writing, factual questions, sensitive topics — and asked to write ideal responses. These (prompt, ideal_response) pairs form the SFT dataset, typically 10,000–100,000 examples for a production pipeline.

The SFT objective is standard supervised learning:

$$\mathcal{L}_{SFT} = -\sum_{t} \log P_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$$

After SFT, the model has learned the chat format, the appropriate conversational register, and a basic understanding that it should follow instructions rather than continue them.

**SFT failure modes that motivate subsequent stages:**

| Failure Mode | Root Cause | Next Stage Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sycophancy | Annotators tend to affirm; model learns to agree | RL/DPO with preference data rewarding corrections |
| Inconsistency | Finite training prompts ≠ infinite input space | Reward model generalizes over distribution |
| Unsafe on long-tail | No coverage of every harmful prompt | RL training with safety-aware reward |
| Poor calibration | Training data didn't model uncertainty systematically | Preference data favoring honest uncertainty |

::: {.callout-tip}
**Annotator quality = model quality ceiling.** If contractors write verbose, meandering responses, the model learns to be verbose. If they have systematic cultural biases, those biases are encoded. Investing in annotator training and quality control is a direct investment in model quality. This is why companies like Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google have entire teams dedicated to data quality — not just volume.
:::

## Stage 2: Reward Model Training

The reward model (RM) is trained on human preference data: pairs of responses to the same prompt, with one labeled as preferred. Collection process: show annotators two model-generated responses and ask "which is better?" This comparison task is empirically superior to direct demonstration writing — humans are much better at evaluating than generating.

A production preference dataset contains hundreds of thousands of (prompt, chosen_response, rejected_response) triples.

### Architecture

The RM is architecturally identical to the LLM being aligned, but with the final vocabulary projection layer replaced by a single linear layer producing a scalar reward score. Using the same architecture as the policy ensures the RM can score any text the policy can generate.

### Training Objective: Bradley-Terry

The Bradley-Terry model of paired comparisons provides the training loss. For a pair where response A is preferred over response B:

$$\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))$$

where $r_\theta$ is the reward model's score, $y_w$ is the winning (chosen) response, $y_l$ is the losing (rejected) response, and $\sigma$ is sigmoid. This pushes the RM to assign higher scores to chosen responses with a margin proportional to preference confidence.

**Key properties of the Bradley-Terry formulation:**
- Probabilistic and well-calibrated: score differences map to calibrated preference probabilities
- Handles full spectrum of preference strength: large gap for clear preferences, small for marginal
- Tolerant of noisy labels: log-sigmoid loss is soft, not hard 0/1

### Reward Model Quality

Production reward models typically achieve 70–80% accuracy on held-out preference pairs. This sounds modest but is sufficient to provide a useful training signal because:

1. The RL optimizer runs for millions of steps — a noisy signal averaged over many steps is still informative
2. The KL penalty prevents the policy from over-fitting to the RM's noise

::: {.callout-warning}
**The RM is the pipeline's bottleneck.** A reward model that learned spurious correlations — long responses score higher, bullet points score higher, confident-sounding language scores higher regardless of accuracy — will lead the RL stage to produce a policy that generates long, bulleted, confident-sounding responses that humans actually find worse. This is reward hacking. The RM's quality determines the ceiling of what RLHF can achieve.
:::

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForSequenceClassification, AutoTokenizer

# Reward model: same architecture as LLM, linear head replaces vocab projection
model_name = "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)

# AutoModelForSequenceClassification adds a scalar output head
reward_model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
    model_name,
    num_labels=1,  # scalar reward
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
)

def score_response(prompt: str, response: str) -> float:
    """Score a (prompt, response) pair using the trained reward model."""
    text = f"[INST] {prompt} [/INST] {response}"
    inputs = tokenizer(text, return_tensors="pt", truncation=True, max_length=2048)
    with torch.no_grad():
        outputs = reward_model(**inputs)
    # outputs.logits shape: (1, 1) — single scalar reward
    return outputs.logits.squeeze().item()

# Usage
prompt = "What is the capital of France?"
good_response = "The capital of France is Paris."
bad_response  = "France's capital is definitely London, I believe."

print(score_response(prompt, good_response))   # expect higher score
print(score_response(prompt, bad_response))    # expect lower score
```

## Stage 3: PPO Fine-Tuning

Proximal Policy Optimization optimizes the SFT model toward higher rewards. The policy is the language model: given a prompt, it generates a response. The reward signal comes from the reward model.

### The 4-Model Setup

PPO in the LLM context requires four models in memory simultaneously:

| Model | Role | Updated? | Memory Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy model ($\pi_\theta$) | The model being trained | Yes — every step | ~16 GB for 7B bf16 |
| Reference model ($\pi_{SFT}$) | Frozen SFT model for KL | No | ~16 GB |
| Reward model ($r_\phi$) | Scores generated responses | No | ~16 GB |
| Value model / critic | Estimates baseline reward for advantage | Yes | ~16 GB |

Total for a 7B setup: ~64 GB minimum VRAM. For 70B models this becomes 640 GB+, requiring multi-node setup. This memory cost is the primary engineering burden of PPO and a core motivation for DPO.

### PPO Objective with KL Penalty

$$\mathcal{L}_{PPO} = \mathbb{E}\left[r_\theta(x, y)\right] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}\left(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{SFT}(y|x)\right)$$

The KL divergence term $D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{SFT})$ measures how far the current policy has drifted from the SFT model in terms of output distribution. The coefficient $\beta$ controls the tradeoff: high $\beta$ keeps the policy conservative and close to SFT; low $\beta$ allows more aggressive optimization. Typical values: $\beta \in [0.01, 0.1]$.

**Why KL is critical:**

Without this penalty, the policy rapidly learns to exploit the reward model. Common exploit patterns: repetitive responses that score well because the RM rarely saw repetition in training; extremely long responses if the RM has any length-quality correlation; responses that begin with phrases strongly associated with high-quality demonstrations. The KL penalty prevents this by imposing a cost for moving far from the SFT model's distribution.

::: {.callout-warning}
**What happens if KL = 0?** Setting $\beta = 0$ removes all constraint on distributional drift. The policy will rapidly find narrow response patterns that exploit the reward model's weak spots — degenerate, repetitive, or incoherent outputs that somehow score well. This is textbook reward hacking and happens within hundreds of gradient steps. Always use a positive KL coefficient; never zero it out in production.
:::

### PPO Clipping

PPO's defining feature (separate from the KL penalty) is the clipped surrogate objective that prevents the policy from making large, destabilizing updates in a single step:

$$\mathcal{L}_{clip} = \mathbb{E}_t \left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]$$

where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)$ is the probability ratio and $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage estimate. The clip bounds (typically $\epsilon = 0.2$) prevent the policy from changing too much in a single update, which is the primary reason PPO is more stable than vanilla policy gradient for LLMs.

**Why PPO and not simpler RL algorithms:**

- **Vanilla policy gradient (REINFORCE):** high variance, requires huge batch sizes to stabilize — impractical for LLMs where each forward pass is expensive
- **TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization):** theoretically principled with hard KL constraint, but computationally expensive (requires second-order optimization)
- **PPO:** heuristic clipping achieves similar stability to TRPO with first-order optimization — practical and well-tuned

### Practical Stability Tricks

| Trick | What It Does | Why Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Reward normalization | Normalize reward scores to zero mean, unit variance | RM scores have arbitrary scale; raw scores cause gradient instability |
| Advantage normalization | Normalize advantage estimates per batch | Prevents large advantage values from causing outsized gradient updates |
| Gradient clipping | Clip global gradient norm (e.g., max_norm=1.0) | RL objectives have higher gradient variance than supervised; clipping prevents divergence |
| Value function warmup | Pretrain value function for a few steps before starting policy updates | Cold value estimates produce high-variance advantage estimates in first steps |
| KL coefficient scheduling | Start high, anneal lower | Early training needs stability; later training can allow more aggressive optimization |

## Reward Hacking

Reward hacking occurs when the policy finds ways to maximize the reward model's score that do not reflect genuine human preference. It is an instance of Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

### Common Patterns

| Exploit Pattern | Mechanism | Detection Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Verbosity bias | RM learned length-quality correlation; policy generates ever-longer responses | Rising response length over training steps |
| Sycophancy | Policy learns to agree with whatever the user says; RM rewards agreement | Human eval: does model agree with clearly false user statements? |
| Prompt gaming | Policy learns to mirror prompt structure back; RM rewards stylistic similarity | Response starts repeating phrases from the prompt |
| Formatting exploitation | Bullet points, headers, and bold text score well; policy over-formats | Formatting ratio increases; content quality does not |
| Confidence exploitation | RM rewards confident-sounding language; policy becomes over-confident | Calibration metrics degrade; model claims certainty on uncertain topics |
| Prefix exploitation | Phrases like "Certainly! Great question!" appear in high-scored training data; policy front-loads them | Response preamble length increases |

### Detection in Production

Reward hacking detection requires monitoring the gap between RM scores and independent human evaluations:

1. **Reward vs. human preference divergence:** Sample 200 responses at regular training checkpoints. Have humans rank them. Plot RM score vs. human preference score over time. A widening gap is the primary signal of hacking.
2. **Out-of-distribution analysis:** Test the RM on deliberately adversarial inputs (very long responses, heavily formatted responses, sycophantic responses). If it assigns high scores, the RM has vulnerabilities the policy will exploit.
3. **Length tracking:** Plot mean response length per training step. Monotonic increase without a corresponding increase in human helpfulness scores indicates verbosity exploitation.
4. **Calibration metrics:** Track Brier score or Expected Calibration Error over training. Degrading calibration with increasing RM scores is a strong hacking signal.

### Mitigation Strategies

| Strategy | Mechanism | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| KL penalty tuning | Higher $\beta$ limits distributional drift | Reduces improvement ceiling |
| Ensemble reward models | Train multiple RMs on different data splits; use minimum or mean score | Expensive: multiple RM inference passes per training step |
| Constitutional constraints | Add explicit rules that override RM score | Requires careful constraint design; can cause over-refusal |
| Iterative RM retraining | Collect preference data on policy's current outputs; retrain RM | Expensive; requires continuous human annotation |
| Output length constraints | Hard cap on response length at training time | May be too restrictive for complex tasks |

::: {.callout-tip}
**The best single mitigation is a good reward model.** The stronger the RM's generalization (more diverse training data, better architecture, better annotation quality), the fewer exploitable weak spots exist. Ensemble RMs are the gold standard for production systems where reward hacking tolerance is low. For most practitioners, investing in RM quality before tuning KL is the higher-leverage move.
:::

## Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) is a fundamental rethinking of how to use preference data. The key insight is that the reward model is implicit in the policy ratio — you do not need to train a separate RM or run an RL loop.

### The Mathematical Insight

In RLHF, the optimal policy under a KL-constrained reward objective has a closed-form solution:

$$\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{\pi_{SFT}(y|x)}{Z(x)} \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r^*(x,y)\right)$$

where $Z(x)$ is the partition function. Rearranging to solve for the reward:

$$r^*(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{SFT}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

Plugging this into the Bradley-Terry preference model and noting that $\log Z(x)$ cancels in the pairwise comparison, you get the DPO loss directly:

$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

Intuitively: DPO increases the relative log-probability of the chosen response and decreases the relative log-probability of the rejected response, with both measured as ratios against the SFT reference policy. The reference policy prevents the model from trivially satisfying the objective by assigning high probability to everything.

### Why DPO Eliminates the RL Loop

The loss is a standard binary cross-entropy computed from (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples using only the policy being trained and a frozen reference model. There is no:
- Separate reward model to train
- RL sampling loop
- Value model / critic
- PPO-specific hyperparameters (clip ratio, GAE lambda, value function coefficient)

This reduces the 4-model PPO setup to 2 models and makes training as stable as instruction tuning.

### DPO Training with TRL

```python
from datasets import Dataset
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model_name = "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct"

# Load policy model and reference model (frozen SFT model)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name, torch_dtype="auto")
model_ref = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name, torch_dtype="auto")
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token

# Preference dataset format: prompt, chosen, rejected
# Each field is a list of chat messages (role/content dicts)
preference_data = [
    {
        "prompt": "What is the boiling point of water?",
        "chosen": "Water boils at 100°C (212°F) at standard atmospheric pressure (1 atm).",
        "rejected": "Great question! Water, that wonderful molecule H2O, boils at around 100 degrees Celsius, though this can vary based on many factors such as altitude, atmospheric pressure, dissolved solids, and many other variables that science has studied for centuries.",
    },
    # ... more preference pairs
]
dataset = Dataset.from_list(preference_data)

# DPO configuration
training_args = DPOConfig(
    output_dir="./dpo-output",
    num_train_epochs=2,
    per_device_train_batch_size=4,
    gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
    learning_rate=5e-7,          # lower than SFT; DPO is sensitive to LR
    beta=0.1,                    # KL regularization coefficient
    max_length=1024,
    max_prompt_length=512,
    remove_unused_columns=False,
    logging_steps=10,
    warmup_ratio=0.1,
    lr_scheduler_type="cosine",
    bf16=True,
)

trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model=model,
    ref_model=model_ref,         # frozen reference (SFT model)
    args=training_args,
    train_dataset=dataset,
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
)

trainer.train()

# Monitor training: watch for these signals
# - dpo/chosen_rewards should increase over training
# - dpo/rejected_rewards should decrease over training
# - dpo/reward_margin (chosen - rejected) should widen
# - If reward_margin collapses: LR too high or beta too low
```

### DPO Limitations

::: {.callout-warning}
**DPO distributional mismatch:** DPO's preference pairs should ideally be generated by the same model you are training (the SFT model). If preference pairs were generated by a different, stronger model (e.g., GPT-4 generated both chosen and rejected responses, but you are training a 7B model), distributional mismatch reduces training signal quality. The model is learning to prefer responses in a distribution it would not naturally generate. For best results: generate preference pairs by sampling from your specific SFT model, not from off-the-shelf datasets generated by other models.
:::

### DPO vs. PPO vs. GRPO

| Method | Models Needed | Complexity | Quality | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PPO | 4 (policy + ref + RM + value) | High — RL loop, many hyperparams | High ceiling, unstable | Online RL, iterative RLHF, when you can afford it |
| DPO | 2 (policy + ref) | Low — supervised loss | Competitive with PPO | Default choice for most preference alignment tasks |
| GRPO | 2 (policy + ref) | Medium — group sampling | Strong for reasoning | Math/code tasks with verifiable rewards |

## GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) is the RL algorithm used in DeepSeek-R1. It is a variant of PPO that removes the value model by using group statistics as the baseline for advantage estimation.

### The Key Idea

Standard PPO estimates advantage as $\hat{A}_t = r_t - V_\phi(s_t)$, where $V_\phi$ is a learned value function that estimates expected future reward. Training this value function requires the 4th model in the PPO setup.

GRPO replaces the value function with a group baseline. For a given prompt $x$, sample a group of $G$ responses $\{y_1, ..., y_G\}$ from the current policy. The advantage for response $y_i$ is:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\{r_1,...,r_G\})}{\text{std}(\{r_1,...,r_G\})}$$

The mean and standard deviation are computed within the group for that prompt — no separate value model needed. This eliminates an entire model from the training setup while still providing a normalized advantage signal.

The GRPO objective:

$$\mathcal{L}_{GRPO} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\left(r_i(\theta)\hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_i(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_i\right) - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{ref})\right]$$

### Why GRPO Works for Reasoning

GRPO excels on tasks with **verifiable rewards** — math problems, code generation, structured output generation. For a math problem, you can automatically check whether the answer is correct. This produces a binary or sparse but accurate reward signal. With group sampling, even a sparse signal is informative: if 3 out of 8 responses in the group get the answer right, the model can learn from the contrast between those 3 and the other 5. This dense-signal-from-sparse-reward property is why DeepSeek-R1 used GRPO for mathematics reasoning training.

::: {.callout-tip}
**GRPO vs. PPO for your use case:**
- If your reward is verifiable (math answers, code test pass/fail, structured JSON validity): GRPO is simpler and effective.
- If your reward requires a trained RM (open-ended quality judgments, style, safety): PPO or DPO is more appropriate since the RM provides a dense signal and GRPO's group statistics work less well with noisy RM scores.
:::

## Constitutional AI (CAI)

Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) is Anthropic's approach to alignment that replaces human preference labels with AI-generated feedback guided by an explicit set of principles — the "constitution."

The constitution is a list of named principles:
- "Do not assist with creating weapons of mass destruction"
- "Respect human autonomy; avoid being paternalistic"
- "Be transparent about uncertainty"
- "Do not deceive users, even through technically true statements"

These principles are auditable and revisable. This is a significant advantage over RLHF, where alignment values are implicitly encoded in the preferences of human annotators who may have their own biases.

### Two-Phase Pipeline

**Phase 1 — Supervised Learning from AI Feedback (SL-CAI):**
1. Model generates responses to potentially harmful prompts
2. Model critiques its own responses against the constitutional principles (identifying which principles were violated and why)
3. Model revises the response to better comply with the principles
4. Fine-tune on (prompt, revised_response) pairs — no human labels needed

**Phase 2 — RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback):**
1. Sample response pairs for a given prompt
2. Ask the AI (guided by constitutional principles) to judge which response better follows the constitution
3. Train a reward model from these AI-generated preference labels
4. Run standard RL training with the AI-labeled reward model

### Scalability Advantage

Human preference annotation is expensive: hundreds of skilled annotators working for months to produce a large, high-quality preference dataset. AI-generated feedback scales essentially unboundedly — the primary expense is inference compute, not human labor. This scalability enables a much larger and more diverse preference dataset.

::: {.callout-tip}
**RLAIF quality:** Empirical comparisons find that CAI-trained models produce outputs that humans rate similarly to or better than models trained with human feedback, provided the AI rater is sufficiently capable. The practical conclusion: for most alignment tasks, RLAIF with a strong AI rater (Claude 3, GPT-4) is a viable and scalable alternative to human annotation RLHF.
:::

### Constitutional AI vs. RLHF

| Dimension | RLHF | Constitutional AI |
|---|---|---|
| Data requirement | Large-scale human preference annotation | Principles document + AI-generated comparisons |
| Scalability | Bottlenecked by human annotator bandwidth | Scales with compute |
| Transparency | Values implicit in annotator preferences | Values explicit in auditable constitution |
| Consistency | Varies across annotators; kappa ~0.6-0.7 typical | AI rater is internally consistent |
| Quality | High (calibrated human judgment) | Comparable when AI rater is strong |
| Best for | Tasks requiring nuanced human judgment | Large-scale alignment, iterative refinement |

## Preference Data Quality

What makes good preference data is often underspecified in papers. Here is what actually matters in production:

### Criteria for Good Preference Pairs

1. **Clear preference margin:** Ambiguous pairs (where a reasonable annotator might go either way) add noise rather than signal. Filter pairs with low inter-annotator agreement.
2. **Diverse prompts:** A preference dataset concentrated on common prompts produces a reward model that is well-calibrated on common cases but fails on edge cases. Actively ensure diversity: topic diversity, prompt length diversity, difficulty diversity, safety topic diversity.
3. **Response diversity:** Both chosen and rejected responses should span a range of qualities. Pairs where one response is obviously excellent and the other obviously terrible are easy cases — the model learns less from them than from hard cases.
4. **Consistent annotators:** Individual annotators drift over time and differ from each other. Annotator ID is a confounding variable in preference data. Use inter-rater agreement as a data quality metric, not just as an annotator quality metric.

### Inter-Annotator Agreement

Common issues:
- **Fleiss's Kappa < 0.4 on a category:** The annotation instructions are ambiguous for this category; revise the rubric.
- **One annotator is systematically different:** That annotator may have a different value system or may be misunderstanding the rubric; review and possibly exclude.
- **Agreement decreases over time:** Annotators are fatiguing or drifting; run calibration sessions.

### Synthetic Preference Data

Using LLMs as annotators (RLAIF) is now standard:

```python
import anthropic

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

def generate_ai_preference(prompt: str, response_a: str, response_b: str) -> dict:
    """
    Use Claude as an AI preference annotator.
    Returns: {"preferred": "A" or "B", "rationale": str, "confidence": float}
    """
    annotation_prompt = f"""You are evaluating two AI assistant responses for quality.

User prompt: {prompt}

Response A:
{response_a}

Response B:
{response_b}

Evaluate which response is better on:
1. Accuracy and factual correctness
2. Completeness (addresses all aspects of the question)
3. Conciseness (no unnecessary verbosity)
4. Tone (appropriate, helpful, not preachy)

Output JSON with keys: preferred ("A" or "B"), rationale (1-2 sentences), confidence (0.0-1.0).
Only output valid JSON, no other text."""

    response = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-opus-4-5",
        max_tokens=256,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": annotation_prompt}],
    )

    import json
    return json.loads(response.content[0].text)

# Filter preference pairs by confidence threshold
def build_preference_dataset(
    raw_pairs: list[dict], confidence_threshold: float = 0.75
) -> list[dict]:
    filtered = []
    for pair in raw_pairs:
        annotation = generate_ai_preference(
            pair["prompt"], pair["response_a"], pair["response_b"]
        )
        if annotation["confidence"] >= confidence_threshold:
            filtered.append({
                "prompt": pair["prompt"],
                "chosen": pair[f"response_{annotation['preferred'].lower()}"],
                "rejected": pair["response_a"] if annotation["preferred"] == "B" else pair["response_b"],
                "confidence": annotation["confidence"],
                "rationale": annotation["rationale"],
            })
    return filtered
```

## Cheat Sheet

- **Base LLM problem:** next-token predictor, no values, no safety, completes any prompt statistically plausibly
- **Alignment goal:** HHH — Helpful, Harmless, Honest; three objectives in tension
- **Alignment tax:** aligned models slightly underperform base models on raw capability benchmarks — expected, not a bug
- **SFT (Stage 1):** supervised fine-tuning on human-written demonstrations; teaches chat format and basic instruction following; quality ceiling = annotator quality
- **SFT failure modes:** sycophancy, inconsistency, poor calibration, safety gaps on long-tail inputs
- **Reward model (Stage 2):** same LLM architecture + scalar head; trained on Bradley-Terry pairwise loss; generalizes human preference to unseen inputs; 70–80% held-out accuracy is typical
- **Bradley-Terry loss:** $-\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$ — pushes chosen score above rejected score
- **PPO (Stage 3):** policy generates, RM scores, policy gradient updates, KL penalty against SFT model
- **4-model PPO setup:** policy + reference (SFT) + reward model + value model = 4x memory vs SFT alone
- **KL penalty purpose:** prevents reward hacking; constrains policy to stay near SFT distribution; controlled by $\beta$ (typical: 0.01–0.1)
- **KL = 0 consequence:** policy rapidly finds reward model exploits; degenerate outputs within hundreds of steps
- **Reward hacking:** Goodhart's Law in RL; model finds high-RM-score patterns that don't reflect human preference
- **Common hacks:** verbosity bias, sycophancy, formatting exploitation, prefix exploitation, confidence exploitation
- **Reward hacking detection:** plot RM score vs. human preference score over training steps; widening gap = hacking
- **PPO instability causes:** noisy value estimates, RM anomalies, policy-value function interaction
- **PPO stability tricks:** reward normalization, advantage normalization, gradient clipping, value function warmup
- **DPO key insight:** optimal RLHF policy is implicit in the log-probability ratio of chosen vs. rejected; no explicit RM needed
- **DPO loss:** $-\log \sigma(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l)})$
- **DPO models needed:** 2 (policy + frozen reference); vs PPO's 4
- **DPO quality:** competitive with PPO on most benchmarks; less stable on very hard alignment tasks requiring online exploration
- **DPO distributional mismatch:** preference pairs should be generated by the same SFT model you are training; off-the-shelf datasets from other models degrade training signal
- **GRPO:** DeepSeek-R1's variant; removes value model by using within-group reward statistics as baseline
- **GRPO best for:** verifiable rewards (math, code) where binary correctness provides accurate but sparse signal
- **Constitutional AI phases:** (1) supervised learning from AI feedback — model critiques and revises own outputs; (2) RLAIF — AI-generated preference labels train RM, then RL
- **CAI scalability advantage:** RLAIF scales with compute vs RLHF's human bandwidth bottleneck
- **CAI transparency advantage:** values explicit in auditable constitution vs implicit in annotator preferences
- **Good preference data:** clear preference margin, diverse prompts, response diversity, consistent annotators
- **Inter-annotator Kappa < 0.4:** annotation rubric is ambiguous for that category; revise before collecting more data

## Interview Questions

### Entry Level

**Q1. What is RLHF and why is it used instead of just fine-tuning on demonstrations?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a training technique that fine-tunes language models using human preference signals rather than static labeled data.

Pure supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations produces models that are inconsistent, sometimes unsafe, and prone to sycophancy. Human preference data captures subtle quality distinctions that are difficult to specify as explicit instructions: it is easier for a human to say "response A is better than response B" than to write out all the rules that make A better. RLHF uses preference data in two ways: first to train a reward model that can score any (prompt, response) pair, then to optimize the language model with RL to generate responses that the reward model rates highly. The result is a model steered toward patterns humans prefer, covering the vast space of possible inputs more effectively than finite demonstration datasets.

**Weak answer:** "RLHF makes the model better." — No specifics on why SFT alone fails or how the preference signal is used.

**Strong answer:** Names the SFT failure modes (sycophancy, inconsistency, long-tail safety gaps), explains the two-stage use of preference data (RM training, then RL), and connects this to why base LLMs need alignment at all.
:::

**Q2. What is "helpful, harmless, honest" and why are these objectives in tension?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
HHH is Anthropic's framework for the three dimensions of alignment:

- **Helpful**: actually completes what the user needs, not evasive non-answers
- **Harmless**: avoids assisting with requests that cause serious harm
- **Honest**: accurate uncertainty representation, no hallucination, no manipulation

They are in tension because optimizing strongly for one often hurts another. A maximally helpful model completes any request — including harmful ones. A maximally harmless model refuses all borderline requests — becoming useless. A maximally honest model says "I don't know" to everything uncertain — becoming unhelpful for tasks requiring synthesis. Real alignment is about finding the Pareto frontier across all three, not maximizing any one in isolation. Over-refusal (too much harmlessness at the expense of helpfulness) is a well-documented alignment failure mode that product teams must track as carefully as under-refusal.
:::

**Q3. What is a reward model and what is it trained on?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
A reward model is a neural network trained to predict human preference for a (prompt, response) pair, outputting a scalar quality score. It has the same architecture as the LLM being aligned, but the final vocabulary projection layer is replaced by a single linear layer that outputs a scalar reward.

It is trained on human preference data: pairs of responses to the same prompt, with one response labeled as preferred by human annotators. The training loss is the Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison objective: minimize $-\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$, where $y_w$ is the preferred response. After training, the reward model generalizes to score any (prompt, response) pair it has never seen before — this generalization is what makes RL training tractable.
:::

**Q4. What is reward hacking?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Reward hacking occurs when the policy finds response patterns that score highly on the reward model without actually being better responses. It is an instance of Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

The reward model was trained on finite preference data and has learned some spurious correlations alongside genuine quality signals. An RL optimizer is powerful enough to identify and exploit those correlations. Classic examples: if the reward model has a length-quality correlation from training data where annotators preferred more detailed answers, the policy learns to generate excessively long responses. If formatting (bullet points, headers) was associated with quality in the training data, the policy learns to over-format. These responses score well on the reward model but humans find them worse than the SFT baseline. The KL divergence penalty is the primary mitigation: it constrains the policy to stay near the SFT model's distribution, making it expensive to reach the extreme regions where reward model exploits live.
:::

**Q5. What does the KL penalty do in PPO, and what happens if you set it to zero?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
The KL penalty adds a term to the PPO training objective that penalizes the policy for diverging from the reference SFT model: $\mathcal{L} = E[r(x,y)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{SFT})$. It imposes a cost for generating a response distribution that differs significantly from the SFT model's distribution.

Setting $\beta = 0$ removes all constraint on distributional drift. The RL optimizer will rapidly find the narrow set of response patterns that exploit the reward model's weak spots — degenerate, repetitive, or incoherent outputs that happen to score well. This happens within hundreds of gradient steps. The KL penalty also prevents mode collapse, where the policy generates only a small set of high-reward response patterns for all prompts, losing the diversity and task-specific responsiveness the SFT model had. Always use a positive KL coefficient.

**Weak answer:** "It keeps the model from changing too much." — Correct but misses why (reward hacking, mode collapse) and the $\beta = 0$ consequence.

**Strong answer:** Names reward hacking and mode collapse as the two failure modes, explains the $\beta$ tradeoff (stability vs. improvement ceiling), and knows typical values ($0.01–0.1$).
:::

**Q6. What is DPO and how does it differ from RLHF with PPO?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a technique that learns from preference pairs directly without training a separate reward model or running an RL loop. It derives mathematically that the optimal RLHF policy can be expressed in terms of the policy's own log-probability ratios on chosen vs. rejected responses relative to a reference policy. This yields a simple binary cross-entropy loss computed directly from (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples.

PPO requires 4 models (policy, reference, reward model, value function) and a complex RL training loop with many hyperparameters and common failure modes. DPO requires only 2 models (policy, frozen reference) and uses a standard supervised loss that is as stable as instruction tuning. DPO is competitive with PPO on most benchmarks and has largely replaced it as the default approach because the engineering complexity reduction is substantial with minimal quality cost.
:::

### Mid Level

**Q1. Walk through the three stages of RLHF. What does each stage contribute and what are its failure modes?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Stage 1 — SFT:** human contractors write ideal responses to diverse prompts; base model fine-tuned on (prompt, ideal_response) pairs. Teaches chat format, instruction following, and basic behavioral patterns. Failure modes: sycophancy (annotators tend to affirm), inconsistency (finite training data ≠ infinite input space), poor calibration, safety gaps on long-tail inputs not covered in demonstrations.

**Stage 2 — Reward Model:** human raters compare response pairs, labeling which is better. Reward model (same architecture as LLM + scalar head) trained on Bradley-Terry loss to predict preference. Learns a general quality signal covering unseen inputs. Failure mode: reward model learns spurious correlations (length, formatting, confidence) that the RL stage will exploit.

**Stage 3 — PPO:** policy generates responses, RM scores them, policy gradient updates increase probability of high-reward responses. KL penalty against SFT model prevents reward hacking and mode collapse. Failure modes: reward collapse, mode collapse, gradient instability. Each stage builds on the prior; SFT quality ceiling propagates to RM and then to PPO.

**Follow-up: Why does SFT quality matter for Stage 3?**

The reward model is trained on preference comparisons between SFT model outputs. If the SFT model generates low-quality responses, the preference comparisons are between responses that are all poor, and the reward model learns to discriminate within a low-quality distribution. The RL stage then optimizes toward the top of this low-quality distribution rather than toward genuinely good responses. SFT quality is a bottleneck for the entire pipeline.
:::

**Q2. Explain the DPO loss function and the role of the reference policy. What happens without it?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
DPO loss:
$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\mathbb{E} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

The loss increases the relative log-probability of the chosen response $y_w$ and decreases it for the rejected response $y_l$, with both measured as ratios against the reference policy $\pi_{ref}$ (the frozen SFT model).

**The role of the reference policy:** it normalizes the probability ratios to prevent a degenerate solution where the model trivially satisfies the objective by increasing all response probabilities equally. Without the reference policy, the gradient direction is "make all tokens in chosen response more likely" — the model could satisfy this by becoming overconfident across the board without genuinely preferring the chosen response over the rejected one. The reference policy ensures the model must increase the probability of chosen responses *relative to what the SFT model would have assigned*, not in absolute terms.

**Follow-up: What is the $\beta$ parameter in DPO?**

$\beta$ is the KL regularization strength — the same role as the KL coefficient in PPO. High $\beta$: policy stays close to reference, more conservative updates. Low $\beta$: allows larger distributional shift toward the preference signal. Typical range: 0.01–0.5. Unlike PPO where $\beta$ is an explicit penalty term, in DPO $\beta$ appears inside the log-sigmoid, scaling the log-probability ratios before comparison. It can be interpreted as the implicit temperature of the reward model implicit in the DPO objective.
:::

**Q3. Your company is considering DPO vs. PPO for an alignment task. What factors drive the decision?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Choose DPO when:**
- Your reward signal comes from static preference data (you have a fixed dataset of comparisons)
- Engineering team does not have RL infrastructure
- Stability is more important than squeezing out the last bit of quality
- Training is batch/offline — no need for online exploration

**Choose PPO when:**
- You need online RL: the policy should generate its own responses and receive reward on those (iterative RLHF)
- Your reward model is trained on diverse data and is high quality — in this case the RL signal from PPO can exceed DPO
- You need to combine multiple reward signals dynamically (safety reward + helpfulness reward weighted adaptively)
- You are doing tasks where exploration during training matters (novel prompt distributions)

**In practice:** DPO is the right default. Most production alignment pipelines use DPO because the quality gap vs. PPO is small, and the engineering complexity reduction is substantial. PPO is reserved for research settings or production systems with mature RL infrastructure and tasks that genuinely require online exploration.

**Q4. How do you detect and mitigate reward hacking in a production RLHF pipeline?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Detection strategy — three metrics to monitor simultaneously:**

1. **RM score vs. human preference divergence:** at regular checkpoints (every 500–1000 training steps), sample ~100 responses and have humans rate them. Plot RM score and human preference score as paired time series. Widening gap signals the policy is finding RM exploits that humans don't actually prefer.

2. **Response length tracking:** plot mean token count per training step. Monotonic increase without a corresponding human helpfulness increase = verbosity exploitation. Set a soft threshold (e.g., if mean length increases >30% from SFT baseline without improvement in human eval).

3. **Out-of-distribution RM probing:** test the RM on deliberately adversarial inputs — copy-pasted prompt fragments, extremely long padding, sycophantic preambles. If these score in the top 20th percentile, the RM has vulnerabilities the policy will eventually find.

**Mitigation toolkit:**

| Severity | Mitigation | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| Mild (early signs) | Increase KL $\beta$ coefficient | Fast — change training config |
| Moderate | Collect preference data specifically on the exploit pattern; retrain RM | Medium — 1-2 days |
| Severe | Roll back to earlier checkpoint; redesign RM training data | Slow — days to weeks |
| Ongoing | Add ensemble RM: score = min(RM1, RM2, RM3); hard to game all simultaneously | Expensive but robust |

```python
# Reward hacking detector: track RM score vs. human eval gap
import numpy as np

def detect_reward_hacking(
    rm_scores: list[float],        # RM scores at each checkpoint
    human_scores: list[float],     # Human eval scores at same checkpoints
    threshold: float = 0.15,       # Alert if divergence > 15% of score range
) -> bool:
    rm_arr = np.array(rm_scores)
    human_arr = np.array(human_scores)

    # Normalize both to [0, 1] for comparison
    rm_norm = (rm_arr - rm_arr.min()) / (rm_arr.max() - rm_arr.min() + 1e-8)
    human_norm = (human_arr - human_arr.min()) / (human_arr.max() - human_arr.min() + 1e-8)

    divergence = rm_norm[-1] - human_norm[-1]  # positive = RM inflated vs human

    if divergence > threshold:
        print(f"WARNING: Reward hacking detected. RM-Human divergence: {divergence:.3f}")
        return True
    return False
```

**Q5. What makes preference data high-quality? How do you quality-control an annotation pipeline?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Characteristics of high-quality preference data:**

- **Clear preference margin:** ambiguous pairs add noise; filter by inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss's kappa < 0.4 for a category = annotation rubric is ambiguous)
- **Diverse prompts:** topic diversity, length diversity, difficulty diversity, safety topic diversity — prevents RM from overfitting to common cases
- **Response diversity:** pairs should span a range of quality levels, not just "obviously excellent vs. obviously terrible"
- **Distribution match:** pairs generated by the model being trained (not a stronger model) to avoid distributional mismatch

**QC mechanisms:**

1. **Annotator qualification:** test raters on a gold-standard set with known-correct answers before labeling real data; exclude those scoring below threshold
2. **Regular calibration sessions:** weekly alignment meetings to discuss ambiguous cases and recalibrate against the rubric; prevents individual drift
3. **Adversarial quality checks:** periodically inject known-bad responses; annotators who consistently fail to reject them are flagged
4. **Gold-standard injection:** include pairs with established ground-truth preference in live annotation batches; monitor each annotator's accuracy over time
5. **Agreement tracking:** compute pairwise kappa between annotators; flag pairs with high disagreement for review; use flag rate as a rubric quality signal

**Follow-up: How do you handle disagreement?**

Multiple strategies: (1) majority vote — use the more common label; (2) aggregate with confidence weighting — pairs with high agreement get higher weight in training; (3) treat high-disagreement pairs as a separate training signal (ambiguous preferences); (4) exclude and revise the rubric. In practice, a combination of majority vote for training and exclusion for very low-agreement pairs (< 60% agreement) works well.

**Q1. Design an RLHF pipeline from scratch for a 13B model. Walk through each stage, data requirements, and how you'd evaluate success.**

**Stage 1 — SFT:**

Data: 200,000–500,000 (prompt, chosen, rejected) preference triples. Generate responses by sampling from the SFT model (not a stronger model — avoid distributional mismatch). Annotate with 3 raters per pair, majority vote label, filter pairs with < 2/3 agreement. Annotator calibration: weekly sessions, gold-standard checks, kappa target > 0.6.

Architecture: identical to the 13B model with scalar output head. Training: Bradley-Terry loss, lr=1e-5, 2 epochs. Evaluation: held-out preference accuracy target 72–78%.

**Stage 3 — PPO or DPO:**

Decision: use DPO first (simpler, stable, competitive quality). If DPO does not achieve target quality, move to PPO. DPO training: same 13B SFT model as both starting point and reference, lr=5e-7, beta=0.1, 2 epochs on preference data.

If PPO: use TRL's PPOTrainer, 4 models, lr=1e-6 for policy, KL beta=0.05 initially, anneal to 0.02 after 5K steps.

**Evaluation framework:**

| Stage | Metric | Target |
|---|---|---|
| SFT | MT-Bench score | ≥ 7.0 / 10 |
| SFT | Held-out human helpfulness | ≥ 70% prefer over base |
| RM | Held-out preference accuracy | 72–78% |
| Final | Win rate vs. SFT (human eval, N=500) | ≥ 60% |
| Final | Safety eval — refusal rate on harmful prompts | > 95% |
| Final | Over-refusal rate — refusal rate on benign prompts | < 5% |
| Final | Reward-human correlation at training end | Pearson r > 0.6 |

**Success criteria:** win rate ≥ 60% vs. SFT on human eval, safety refusal rate > 95%, over-refusal rate < 5%. If reward-human correlation degrades below 0.5, investigate reward hacking before continuing.
:::

> **What great looks like:** Candidate specifies concrete data volumes, explains the SFT→RM→PPO dependency chain, knows why distributional match in preference data matters, defines both safety and helpfulness metrics (not just safety), and mentions the reward-human correlation as a reward hacking early warning system.

> **Common Mistake:** Designing only the training pipeline without the evaluation framework. A pipeline with no defined success criteria ships whenever training converges — which may be after reward hacking has already occurred. Evaluation is not optional; it is what tells you whether the pipeline is working.

**Q2. How do you detect reward hacking in production? Walk me through monitoring and mitigation.**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**Detection — three-layer monitoring system:**

**Layer 1 — Automated metrics (continuous):**
- Response length distribution: track mean and 95th-percentile token count per training checkpoint. Alert on >25% increase from SFT baseline.
- Formatting density: track fraction of response tokens that are markdown symbols (*, #, -, etc.). Alert on monotonic increase.
- Preamble length: length of response before first substantive token. Alert on increase (proxy for prefix exploitation).
- These run cheaply on every training checkpoint with no human involvement.

**Layer 2 — RM vs. human panel divergence (weekly):**
- Sample 200 responses at current checkpoint. Have a panel of 3-5 human raters score them on a 1-5 helpfulness scale.
- Plot RM score vs. human panel score normalized to [0,1]. Alert on gap > 0.15.
- This is the primary signal for reward hacking. It is expensive ($10-50 per 200 samples) but is the only reliable ground truth.

**Layer 3 — RM adversarial probing (at RM training time):**
- Before deploying a new reward model, probe it with known adversarial inputs: padding attacks (responses with many filler words), sycophantic preambles, copy-paste responses.
- If any adversarial class scores in the top quartile, the RM has a vulnerability. Document it before it becomes a policy exploit.

**Mitigation — ordered by speed:**

1. Increase $\beta$ (hours): conservative immediate fix, reduces improvement ceiling.
2. Collect targeted preference data on the specific exploit pattern (1-2 days): generate policy's current exploit responses as rejected, human-written or SFT responses as chosen; retrain RM or run a correction DPO pass.
3. Ensemble RM (1 week): train 3 reward models on different data splits; score = minimum. Hard to exploit because the policy would need to fool all three simultaneously.
4. Constitutional constraint injection (1 week): add a constitutional principle that explicitly penalizes the exploit pattern (e.g., "responses should not use excessive formatting").
5. Checkpoint rollback (immediate): if hacking is severe, roll back to last known-good checkpoint and redesign before retraining.
:::

> **What great looks like:** Candidate has a multi-layer detection system (not just "monitor reward"), knows the RM-human divergence panel is the ground truth signal, has a tiered mitigation strategy ordered by speed/cost, and can explain why ensemble RMs are robust.

> **Common Mistake:** Describing reward hacking only as "model generates long responses." This is one symptom. Great candidates name the full taxonomy (verbosity, sycophancy, formatting exploitation, prefix exploitation, confidence exploitation) and explain detection at the metric level, not just the anecdote level.

**Q3. Compare PPO, DPO, and GRPO. When would you choose each?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
**PPO:**
- 4 models in memory: policy, reference (SFT), reward model, value function
- Online RL: policy generates responses at training time, receives reward, updates
- High complexity: clip ratio, KL coefficient, value function architecture, advantage normalization — all need tuning
- High ceiling when reward model is good and pipeline is stable; can match or exceed DPO with sufficient engineering
- Choose when: mature RL infrastructure available; task requires online exploration; iterative RLHF where policy generates its own preference data

**DPO:**
- 2 models: policy + frozen reference
- Offline: trains on a static preference dataset
- Supervised loss, same stability as instruction tuning; minimal hyperparameter tuning
- Competitive with PPO on most benchmarks; occasional quality gap on tasks requiring exploration
- Choose when: default choice for preference alignment; no RL infrastructure; stability required; batch/offline setting

**GRPO:**
- 2 models: policy + frozen reference (no value model)
- Online-ish: samples G responses per prompt at training time; uses within-group statistics as advantage baseline
- Group sampling provides normalized advantage without value function: $\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})) / \text{std}(\mathbf{r})$
- Best signal when reward is verifiable (binary correct/incorrect): group sampling naturally produces the contrast between correct and incorrect responses needed for learning
- Choose when: math/code tasks with verifiable rewards; want simpler RL setup than PPO; DeepSeek-R1-style reasoning training

**The decision tree in practice:**
1. Is my reward verifiable (math, code)? → GRPO
2. Do I have static preference data and no RL infrastructure? → DPO
3. Do I need online exploration or iterative RLHF and have engineering resources? → PPO
4. Default: DPO
:::

> **What great looks like:** Candidate explains the structural difference (number of models, online vs. offline, value function vs. group statistics), connects GRPO's group sampling to its suitability for verifiable rewards, and gives a concrete decision framework rather than listing features abstractly.

> **Common Mistake:** Saying "GRPO is just PPO without a value model" without explaining why that works — i.e., how the within-group statistics replace the value function baseline and why this requires multiple samples per prompt. The mechanism matters, not just the feature.

**Q4. What is the KL divergence penalty in RLHF and what happens if you set it to zero?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
The KL divergence penalty is an additive term in the PPO training objective:

$$\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{SFT}(y|x))$$

It measures how far the current policy's output distribution has drifted from the SFT reference model's distribution, and penalizes drift proportionally. $\beta$ is the coefficient controlling the tradeoff strength.

**Mechanistic role:** The reward model is an imperfect proxy for human preference. It was trained on finite data and has learned some spurious correlations. The KL penalty constrains the policy to generate text in approximately the same distribution as the SFT model — where the reward model was well-calibrated. Reward model exploits typically exist far from the SFT model's natural distribution (degenerate repetitions, extreme formatting, implausible confidence). The KL penalty makes these regions expensive to reach.

**Setting $\beta = 0$:** the policy is now free to drift arbitrarily from the SFT model. Within hundreds of gradient steps, the optimizer finds reward model weak spots and exploits them aggressively. In practice: responses become repetitive or degenerate, mode collapse occurs (policy generates the same high-reward template for all prompts), and reward scores spike upward while human evaluations tank. Recovery requires rolling back to an earlier checkpoint.

**The $\beta$ tuning tradeoff:**
- High $\beta$ (0.1+): stable training, conservative improvement, policy stays close to SFT
- Low $\beta$ (0.01–0.05): more aggressive optimization, higher ceiling, higher risk of hacking
- Curriculum approach: start at high $\beta$ for training stability, anneal lower as the policy is well-behaved

**In DPO:** the KL constraint is incorporated implicitly — the $\beta$ parameter in the DPO loss serves the same role, scaling the log-probability ratios. The key difference: in DPO there is no separate KL term to zero out; $\beta$ is deeply embedded in the loss structure, making it harder to accidentally remove.

**Connection to reward hacking:** the KL penalty is necessary but not sufficient to prevent reward hacking. With a strong enough reward model exploit, even high $\beta$ cannot prevent the policy from finding it — because the exploit is in a region the SFT model naturally generates, so there is no KL cost to reach it. This is why diverse preference data and ensemble reward models are the long-term solution; KL is the short-term stabilizer.
:::

> **What great looks like:** Candidate covers all three aspects — what the KL penalty is mechanistically, the $\beta = 0$ consequence (names reward hacking and mode collapse, gives a timeline "within hundreds of steps"), and the tuning tradeoff. Bonus points for explaining why KL is necessary but not sufficient and connecting it to reward model quality.

> **Common Mistake:** Answering only "it keeps the model from changing too much" without naming the specific failure modes prevented (reward hacking, mode collapse) or explaining what "too much" means mathematically. The KL penalty is not a vague stability trick — it has a precise meaning and a precise failure mode when absent.

**Q5. A fine-tuned model is very helpful but will answer dangerous requests if asked cleverly. What alignment steps would you take?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
This is safety alignment erosion — a well-documented phenomenon where domain-specific SFT inadvertently removes safety behaviors if the fine-tuning data does not include safety-relevant examples.

**Step 1 — Audit the failure mode.** Characterize systematically what types of "clever" requests succeed. Is it roleplay framing? Multi-step indirect requests? Multilingual pivots? Hypothetical framing? This characterization determines what data you need. Use structured red-teaming across all known jailbreak categories: direct requests, roleplay, hypothetical scenarios, indirect multi-turn, code-based obfuscation.

**Step 2 — Collect targeted safety preference data.** For each successful attack pattern, generate response pairs: a compliant response to the harmful request (rejected) and a high-quality refusal that is firm but not preachy and offers alternatives where possible (chosen). Generate 500–2,000 pairs spanning all identified attack patterns. Quality bar: the chosen responses must be reviewed by safety-domain experts — a poor refusal that is overly broad or condescending teaches the model to be unhelpful, not just safe.

**Step 3 — DPO on safety preference data.** Fine-tune using DPO with these pairs, using the current fine-tuned model as both the reference policy and the training starting point. Run 1–3 epochs. Monitor two metrics simultaneously: attack success rate (should decrease sharply) and helpfulness on legitimate domain queries (should not meaningfully decrease). An increase in over-refusal rate (declining legitimate requests) is a failure mode as serious as insufficient safety.

**Step 4 — Application-layer guardrails.** Safety fine-tuning improves robustness but does not eliminate all attack vectors. Add an input safety classifier (Llama Guard, ShieldLLM, or equivalent) to catch known attack patterns before they reach the model. Add output monitoring to catch safety failures that reach the response stage. These are complementary defenses, not substitutes for model-level alignment.

**Step 5 — Regression testing in CI/CD.** Safety behaviors can regress when new task data is added. Include a safety evaluation suite (attack success rate, over-refusal rate) in the CI/CD gate for every model update. Treat a safety regression the same way you treat a performance regression — it blocks release.
:::

> **What great looks like:** Candidate treats this as a systematic problem requiring characterization before intervention, not "just add more RLHF." Names DPO as the correct tool (simpler than PPO for targeted behavioral correction), insists on measuring over-refusal as well as under-safety, and includes application-layer guardrails and regression testing.

> **Common Mistake:** Recommending "more RLHF" or "more safety training data" without specifying the attack characterization step. Pouring generic safety data on top of a model with a specific attack pattern vulnerability does not reliably fix the specific pattern — you need targeted data for the specific attack vectors that succeed.

**Q6. Explain Constitutional AI's self-critique loop. How does it produce preference data without human labelers?**

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Model Answer"}
Constitutional AI produces preference data through two mechanisms, both driven by the model itself rather than human labelers:

**Phase 1 — Supervised Learning from AI Feedback (SL-CAI):**

The model is given potentially harmful prompts and asked to (1) generate an initial response, (2) critique that response against each constitutional principle it violated (e.g., "This response provides detailed instructions for harm, violating the principle against assisting with dangerous activities"), and (3) revise the response to comply with the violated principles. The final revised response is higher quality — the model used its language understanding to identify its own misalignments and generate corrected examples. Fine-tuning on (prompt, revised_response) pairs produces a model with better safety behavior without any human having labeled a preference.

**Phase 2 — RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback):**

Given two model responses, an AI rater (prompted with the constitutional principles) judges which response better follows the constitution. These AI-generated pairwise preference labels train a reward model in the same way human preference labels do. The constitutional prompting provides consistent, principled guidance to the AI rater, producing more internally consistent preference labels than human annotators who may have different intuitions.

**Why this works:** the AI rater needs to be sufficiently capable to understand and apply the constitutional principles. With a strong AI rater (Claude 3, GPT-4 level), the preference labels are high-quality. With a weak AI rater, the labels are noisy and the reward model learns inconsistent preferences.

**Scalability advantage:** preference label generation scales with inference compute. A production-scale RLAIF pipeline can generate millions of preference pairs per week; an equivalent human annotation pipeline would require thousands of annotators working continuously. This is why Anthropic trained Claude with Constitutional AI rather than pure human-annotation RLHF — the scale simply could not be matched with human labor.

**Transparency advantage:** the constitution is a document you can read, debate, and revise. When the model behaves unexpectedly, you can audit which constitutional principles it was trained on and identify which principles may need updating. This auditability is impossible with implicit annotator preferences.
:::

> **What great looks like:** Candidate clearly explains both phases (SL-CAI and RLAIF as distinct), explains why the self-critique mechanism works (model uses its language understanding to identify its own misalignments), and articulates both the scalability and transparency advantages with specific comparisons to human-annotation RLHF.

> **Common Mistake:** Describing Constitutional AI as just "the model teaches itself to be safe" without explaining the two-phase structure or how preference data is actually generated. The self-critique loop is a specific mechanism, not a vague self-improvement process.
